\begin{abstract}
Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) training systems are increasingly deployed as production AI software, yet their runtime failure recovery remains difficult to test, repair, and validate. A single GPU failure can invalidate only part of the training state, but existing recovery mechanisms typically rely on coarse-grained checkpoint restart, forcing the entire training job to reload stale global state and replay lost progress. More importantly, current training software provides limited support for reasoning about whether a partial recovery has preserved model quality, routing stability, and optimizer consistency.
This paper presents MoEGuard, a runtime reliability framework for engineering self-healing distributed MoE training. MoEGuard provides three capabilities: controlled fault injection for exercising failure paths, safe-point repair for preventing invalid optimizer commits, and expert-weighted-staleness guarded recovery for selecting between checkpoint restart and hybrid state restoration. Instead of treating recovery risk as a global property of the training job, MoEGuard tracks an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ over a sliding exposure window. This design jointly accounts for how many iterations of staleness each hybrid recovery injects and how large a fraction of the expert population has been exposed, enabling the system to use faster hybrid repair when safe and fall back to checkpoint restart when accumulated risk becomes high.
MoEGuard repairs failed ranks using MoE-aware recovery semantics: dense and router states are synchronized from healthy data-parallel neighbors, while affected expert states are restored from distributed checkpoints. A two-phase recovery protocol restores weights first and attaches optimizer state later to reduce time to resumed training. We implement MoEGuard in Megatron-LM and evaluate it with controlled fault traces on H20 GPU clusters. Across single-failure, repeated-failure, concentrated-failure, and distributed-failure scenarios, MoEGuard reduces recovery cost relative to checkpoint restart while maintaining validation loss, perplexity, gradient stability, token drop rate, and expert load balance. These results demonstrate that an expert-weighted staleness density is an effective abstraction for testing and repairing runtime failures in distributed AI training software.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become an important architecture for scaling large language models. By activating only a subset of experts for each token, MoE training increases model capacity while keeping per-token computation relatively efficient. This sparsity, however, also makes MoE training software substantially more complex than dense model training. A distributed MoE training job must coordinate dense parameters, router states, expert parameters, optimizer states, token dispatch metadata, data-parallel groups, expert-parallel groups, and checkpointing logic. As a result, runtime failures are not merely infrastructure events; they can directly affect the consistency of training state and the correctness of subsequent model updates.

In production-scale AI training systems, GPU and rank failures are inevitable. The dominant recovery mechanism today is checkpoint restart: when a rank fails, the training job reloads the most recent checkpoint and replays the lost iterations. This approach is simple and semantically clean, but it is increasingly expensive for MoE training. MoE checkpoints contain large expert states and optimizer states, and loading a full distributed checkpoint may take hundreds of seconds. Moreover, if the failure happens far from the latest checkpoint, the system must also replay many iterations, wasting substantial GPU time. These costs are particularly problematic for large training jobs, where even a single recovery can consume thousands of GPU-seconds.

A natural alternative is partial or hybrid recovery. In MoE training, not all state has the same recovery semantics. Dense parameters, shared layers, and router states are replicated across data-parallel ranks and can often be synchronized from healthy neighbors. In contrast, expert states are sharded across expert-parallel ranks and may need to be restored from checkpoint. This suggests that a failed rank could be repaired without restarting the entire job: synchronize dense and router state from healthy ranks, restore affected expert state from checkpoint, repair runtime metadata, and resume training. However, such hybrid recovery introduces a new software engineering challenge: how can the training system decide when partial state repair is safe?

This question is difficult because recovery correctness in AI training is not purely binary. A repaired job may continue running without crashing, but still silently degrade training quality. For example, restoring an expert from an older checkpoint may introduce stale expert behavior; delaying optimizer state attachment may perturb parameter updates; and repeatedly repairing the same rank with stale expert state may accumulate quality risk. Existing training systems provide limited support for testing these recovery paths or validating their impact on training stability. They typically report whether training resumes, but not whether the repaired training trajectory remains comparable to a clean checkpoint-restart baseline.

This paper argues that reliable MoE training requires treating runtime recovery as a software engineering problem: recovery mechanisms should be fault-injectable, policy-driven, observable, and validated against both system-level and model-level metrics. We introduce MoEGuard, a self-healing runtime framework for distributed MoE training. MoEGuard combines controlled fault injection, safe-point repair, structured recovery logging, and an expert-weighted-staleness guarded recovery policy. When a rank fails, MoEGuard decides whether to use checkpoint restart or hybrid state restoration based on the checkpoint gap and the running expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ accumulated by past hybrid recoveries.

The key insight behind MoEGuard is that recovery risk in MoE training is best characterized by an expert-weighted, window-level staleness debt rather than by either a global checkpoint-gap budget or per-rank exposure alone. Each hybrid recovery injects a controlled amount of staleness into a known subset of experts; the cluster-level risk is the fraction of expert$\,\times\,$iteration cells in the recent training window that have been exposed to such staleness. MoEGuard therefore tracks $\Phi'(t)$ = (sum of $|E_h|\cdot\Delta_h$ for past events plus $|E_{\text{new}}|\cdot\Delta$ for the new event) divided by $N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W$, and uses this density to guard hybrid recovery decisions.

MoEGuard uses two recovery paths. For very small or very large checkpoint gaps, it falls back to checkpoint restart, because replaying a few iterations is cheaper and simpler than performing hybrid repair, while a single very large hybrid event would inject too much per-event staleness on the affected experts. For medium gaps, if the projected expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ remains within a safe region, MoEGuard performs MoE-aware hybrid recovery: dense, shared, and router states are synchronized from healthy data-parallel neighbors, while affected expert weights are restored from the latest distributed checkpoint. MoEGuard further supports a weights-first, optimizer-later recovery protocol, allowing the replacement rank to rejoin training after its weights are restored while optimizer state is attached later under runtime guards. If past hybrid recoveries have already pushed $\Phi'(t)$ above its bound, MoEGuard conservatively falls back to checkpoint restart.

We implement MoEGuard in Megatron-LM and evaluate it on distributed MoE training workloads using H20 GPU clusters. Our evaluation uses controlled single-failure and multi-failure traces, including concentrated failures on the same rank and distributed failures across different ranks. We compare MoEGuard against checkpoint restart, always-hybrid recovery, fixed-gap policies, and global-budget policies. We measure not only recovery latency and wasted GPU time, but also validation loss, validation perplexity, gradient stability, token drop rate, router load-balancing loss, and expert load balance. Our results show that MoEGuard reduces recovery cost by [X]% compared with checkpoint restart while preserving model quality and routing stability under realistic failure scenarios.

This paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A reliability engineering formulation of MoE training recovery. We characterize rank failures in distributed MoE training as a runtime software reliability problem involving state repair, safe-point control, recovery observability, and quality validation.
    \item Expert-weighted-staleness guarded recovery. We introduce a recovery policy that tracks an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ over a sliding exposure window, and combines it with $\Delta_{\min}$ and $\Delta_{\max}$ guards to decide when hybrid recovery is safe.
    \item MoE-aware hybrid state restoration. We design a recovery protocol that restores different classes of training state according to their semantics: dense and router state from healthy data-parallel neighbors, and expert state from distributed checkpoints.
    \item A self-healing runtime framework. We implement controlled fault injection, optimizer commit guarding, safe-point repair, reintegration barriers, and structured recovery logging in Megatron-LM.
    \item An empirical evaluation of recovery correctness and stability. We evaluate MoEGuard using recovery time, wasted GPU-seconds, validation loss, perplexity, gradient stability, token drop rate, and expert load balance, showing that recovery mechanisms must be validated beyond simply checking whether training resumes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts models are increasingly used to scale large language model training. Unlike dense models, MoE training introduces a heterogeneous state structure: dense layers and routers are typically replicated across data-parallel ranks, whereas experts and their optimizer states are partitioned across expert-parallel ranks. This heterogeneity complicates failure recovery---a rank failure may invalidate only a subset of experts, while other parts of the training state remain available on healthy ranks.

\subsection{Checkpoint Restart and Its Cost}

The standard recovery mechanism for distributed training is checkpoint restart. After a failure, the job reloads the most recent checkpoint and replays the lost iterations. This approach provides clean semantics, but it is expensive for large-scale MoE training. Checkpoints contain both dense and expert states as well as optimizer states, and loading a full distributed checkpoint can take hundreds of seconds. The cost further increases with the \emph{checkpoint gap}, defined as the number of iterations between the latest checkpoint and the failure point.

\subsection{Hybrid Recovery and Stale Expert State}

A natural alternative is hybrid recovery. Since dense and router states are replicated, a replacement rank can synchronize their latest versions from healthy data-parallel neighbors. Meanwhile, the affected experts can be restored from the latest distributed checkpoint. This avoids rolling back the entire job and can substantially reduce recovery latency. However, hybrid recovery also introduces \emph{stale expert state}: restored experts may lag behind the current training step by the checkpoint gap.

This creates a recovery trade-off that is specific to MoE training. Small gaps favor checkpoint restart, because replay is cheap and the recovery semantics are simple. Larger gaps favor hybrid recovery, because replay becomes expensive. However, the checkpoint gap alone is insufficient to characterize recovery risk. Repeated hybrid recoveries inject staleness into different subsets of experts; what determines the cluster-level quality risk is not just how much each individual event injects but also how large a fraction of the expert population accumulates staleness inside a recent training window.

\subsection{Expert-Weighted Staleness Density}

This observation motivates an \emph{expert-weighted staleness density} $\Phi'(t)$. Instead of tracking only the global amount of lost work or per-rank accumulated staleness, MoEGuard sums $|E_h|\cdot\Delta_h$ over all hybrid-recovery events $h$ inside a sliding window $W$, normalizes by $N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W$, and treats the result as a dimensionless staleness density that the policy must keep bounded. Hybrid recovery is enabled only when the current checkpoint gap is large enough to justify avoiding restart, small enough to avoid excessive single-event staleness, and the projected $\Phi'(t)$ remains below a configured upper bound.

This abstraction turns MoE recovery into a runtime software reliability problem rather than a pure checkpoint optimization problem. A recovered training job must not only resume execution, but also preserve training stability and model quality. Therefore, recovery mechanisms should be evaluated using both system metrics, such as recovery latency and wasted GPU time, and training metrics, such as validation loss, perplexity, gradient stability, token drop rate, and expert load balance.

\subsection{Distributed Checkpoint Formats in Megatron-LM}

Megatron-LM supports multiple checkpoint formats that critically affect hybrid recovery feasibility:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{torch} (legacy): Each rank saves its own \texttt{.pt} file under \texttt{iter\_XXXXXXX/mp\_rank\_\{tp\}\_\{pp\}\_\{ep\}/model\_optim\_rng.pt}. These files can be loaded independently by each rank using \texttt{torch.load()}, which requires no collective communication---making them suitable for selective expert loading in hybrid recovery.
    \item \texttt{torch\_dist} (default): Uses distributed checkpointing with sharded state dicts and \texttt{metadata.json}. Loading requires \texttt{dist\_checkpointing.load()}, which internally calls \texttt{all\_gather\_object}---a collective operation requiring all ranks to participate. This format is fundamentally incompatible with single-rank selective loading, forcing full checkpoint restart when a failure occurs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

MoEGuard is designed as a runtime recovery framework for distributed MoE training. Its goal is to reduce failure recovery cost without treating model quality as an afterthought. Instead of always restarting the entire training job from the latest checkpoint, MoEGuard decides whether a failed rank can be repaired through MoE-aware hybrid recovery. The decision is guarded by three runtime signals: the checkpoint gap of the failure $\Delta$, the per-event upper bound $\Delta_{\max}$, and the running expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ accumulated by past hybrid recoveries inside the exposure window.

\subsection{Recovery Setting}

We consider a distributed MoE training job running with pipeline, data, and expert parallelism. Each logical rank participates in collective communication and owns a subset of MoE experts. A failure may occur when a rank crashes, becomes unreachable, or enters an invalid runtime state.

Let a failure occur on logical rank $r$ at training step $t$. Let $c$ be the latest checkpoint step before the failure. We define the checkpoint gap as:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta = t - c
\end{equation}

In checkpoint restart, $\Delta$ determines the number of iterations that must be replayed. In hybrid recovery, $\Delta$ determines how stale the restored expert state may be. In our target setting, the maximum single-failure gap is naturally bounded by the checkpoint interval. Therefore, MoEGuard does not require an independent large-gap regime; it treats the checkpoint interval as the upper bound of single-recovery staleness.

\subsection{Overview of MoEGuard}

After a rank failure, MoEGuard follows four steps. First, it establishes a safe repair point by preventing the current iteration from committing unsafe optimizer updates. Second, it evaluates the recovery policy using the checkpoint gap and the running expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$. Third, it executes either checkpoint restart or hybrid recovery. Finally, it refreshes runtime metadata and reintegrates the repaired rank.

Checkpoint restart is the conservative fallback. It restores the full model, optimizer, scheduler, and runtime state from checkpoint $c$, and then replays the lost iterations.

Hybrid recovery repairs only the failed rank. Dense, shared, and router states are synchronized from healthy peers that hold current replicas of the same model state. MoE expert states owned by the failed rank are restored from the latest distributed checkpoint. This creates a mixed-provenance recovered state: dense and router states are current, while affected expert states may be stale by $\Delta$ iterations.

\subsection{Expert-Weighted Staleness Guarded Recovery}

A single checkpoint gap is not sufficient to characterize recovery risk in MoE training. Because each logical rank owns a stable subset of experts, repeated hybrid recoveries\,---\,even when distributed across different ranks\,---\,still leave more and more experts in a stale-by-$\Delta$ state. What matters for model quality is therefore not just \emph{how many iterations} of staleness have been injected, but \emph{how large a fraction of the expert population} has been exposed to staleness, weighted by the per-event staleness magnitude.

MoEGuard therefore tracks a global, expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ over a sliding exposure window $W$. Let $\mathcal{H}_W(t)$ be the set of past hybrid-recovery events that occurred in the window $[t-W, t)$. For each historical event $h \in \mathcal{H}_W(t)$, let $|E_h|$ be the number of MoE experts whose state was restored from checkpoint and $\Delta_h$ the checkpoint gap of that event. For a new failure at step $t$ with checkpoint gap $\Delta$ that would restore $|E_{\text{new}}|$ experts, the projected expert-weighted staleness density is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:phi-prime}
    \Phi'(t) \;=\; \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{h \in \mathcal{H}_W(t)} |E_h|\cdot \Delta_h \;+\; |E_{\text{new}}|\cdot \Delta}{N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W},
\end{equation}
where $N_{\text{expert}}$ is the total number of MoE experts in the model. Intuitively, the numerator is the total ``expert$\,\times\,$iteration'' staleness debt accumulated in the current window, and the denominator $N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W$ is the maximum staleness debt that the model could possibly accumulate if every expert were stale by every iteration of the window. $\Phi'(t)$ is therefore a dimensionless staleness density in $[0,1]$.

MoEGuard uses three thresholds:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\min}, \quad \Delta_{\max}, \quad \Phi_{\max},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_{\min}$ is the minimum gap at which hybrid recovery becomes worthwhile, $\Delta_{\max}$ is the maximum single-event gap above which hybrid recovery would inject too much per-event staleness, and $\Phi_{\max}$ is the maximum allowed expert-weighted staleness density inside the exposure window.

MoEGuard selects hybrid recovery if and only if
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \geq \Delta_{\min} \;\wedge\; \Delta \leq \Delta_{\max} \;\wedge\; \Phi'(t) \leq \Phi_{\max}.
\end{equation}
Otherwise, it falls back to checkpoint restart:
\begin{equation}
    \pi(t) = \begin{cases}
        \textsc{Hybrid}, & \Delta \geq \Delta_{\min} \;\wedge\; \Delta \leq \Delta_{\max} \;\wedge\; \Phi'(t) \leq \Phi_{\max} \\
        \textsc{Restart}, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

This policy captures the intended behavior. When the checkpoint gap is small ($\Delta < \Delta_{\min}$), restart is preferred because replay is cheap and semantically simple. When the gap is too large ($\Delta > \Delta_{\max}$), hybrid recovery would expose the affected experts to excessive staleness in a single event and is unsafe regardless of cluster history. When repeated hybrid recoveries have already pushed too large a fraction of the expert population into the stale region ($\Phi'(t) > \Phi_{\max}$), MoEGuard falls back to checkpoint restart to reset the global expert state to a fully consistent checkpoint snapshot.

\subsection{Recovery Semantics}

MoEGuard's recovery semantics follow the heterogeneous structure of MoE training state. The key distinction is the source of each recovered state.

Dense, shared, and router parameters are recovered from healthy peers and therefore reflect the current training step. Their optimizer states can also be synchronized from healthy replicas when available.

MoE expert weights are recovered from the latest distributed checkpoint because they are rank-local and may not have a fresh in-memory replica. Their optimizer states can either be restored together with the weights or attached later using a two-phase recovery protocol. In the latter case, the repaired rank can rejoin training after weights are restored, while affected expert updates remain guarded until the optimizer state is attached.

Thus, hybrid recovery does not attempt to make all state equally fresh. Instead, it makes state freshness explicit:
\begin{equation}
    \text{dense/router state} \leftarrow \text{current healthy peer}, \quad \text{expert state} \leftarrow \text{latest checkpoint}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Scope}

MoEGuard targets single-rank recovery in distributed MoE training. It assumes that checkpoints are available and that the failed rank can be replaced or repaired. Simultaneous multi-rank failures, corrupted checkpoints, and Byzantine behavior are outside the current scope. Checkpoint restart remains the safe fallback for cases where hybrid recovery is not beneficial or where the projected $\Phi'(t)$ exceeds the configured bound $\Phi_{\max}$.

\section{MoEGuard Design}
\label{sec:design}

MoEGuard is a self-healing runtime framework for distributed MoE training. Its design is centered on one principle: after a rank failure, different parts of the training state should be repaired according to their provenance and consistency requirements. Dense and router states are replicated across data-parallel peers and can be synchronized from healthy ranks, whereas MoE expert states are rank-local and may need to be restored from checkpoints. MoEGuard uses this distinction to select between a conservative checkpoint restart path and a faster MoE-aware hybrid recovery path.

% Figure~\ref{fig:moeguard-overview} shows the overall workflow. When a rank failure occurs, MoEGuard first establishes a safe repair point, blocks unsafe optimizer commits, evaluates an expert-weighted-staleness guarded recovery policy, and then executes either checkpoint restart or hybrid recovery. The repaired rank is finally reintegrated after its state and runtime metadata become consistent.

\subsection{Overview}

MoEGuard consists of five runtime components: a failure detector, a safe-point repair controller, an expert-weighted-staleness guarded policy, a MoE-aware state restoration module, and a reintegration manager.

The failure detector identifies failed ranks through runtime fault reports, communication errors, injected failures, or invalid training state. Once a failure is detected, the safe-point repair controller prevents the current iteration from committing an inconsistent optimizer update. The policy module then computes the checkpoint gap and the projected expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$. Based on these values, MoEGuard chooses between two recovery paths.

The first path is checkpoint restart. This path restores the training job from the latest checkpoint and replays lost iterations. It is used as the safe fallback when the checkpoint gap is too small, when a single hybrid recovery would introduce excessive per-event staleness ($\Delta > \Delta_{\max}$), or when the cluster has already accumulated too high an expert-weighted staleness density ($\Phi'(t) > \Phi_{\max}$).

The second path is MoE-aware hybrid recovery. This path repairs only the failed rank. Dense, shared, and router states are synchronized from healthy data-parallel peers with the same pipeline-stage coordinates, while affected MoE expert states are restored from the latest distributed checkpoint. The repaired rank is then reintegrated after expert directory, dispatch topology, and process-group metadata have been refreshed.

\subsection{Failure Detection and Safe-Point Repair}

A rank failure may occur during forward propagation, backward propagation, gradient synchronization, or optimizer update. Directly repairing the failed rank without controlling the training boundary can leave the job in a partially committed state. MoEGuard therefore introduces a safe-point repair protocol.

When rank $r$ fails at step $t$, MoEGuard marks rank $r$ and its local experts as recovering. If the optimizer step for the current iteration has not been safely completed, the optimizer commit guard blocks the update and marks the iteration as invalid. This prevents parameters or optimizer states from being updated using incomplete gradients or corrupted local state.

The safe-point repair protocol provides a clear recovery boundary. MoEGuard does not attempt to repair arbitrary partially committed updates. Instead, it either discards the unsafe iteration or resumes training after the recovery controller has completed state restoration and reintegration. This design keeps the recovery semantics simple while allowing the system to continue from the current training step when hybrid recovery is selected.

\subsection{Expert-Weighted Staleness Guarded Recovery Policy}

MoEGuard uses the policy defined in Section~\ref{sec:approach} to decide whether hybrid recovery is safe. For a failure at step $t$, let $c$ be the latest checkpoint step and $\Delta = t - c$ be the checkpoint gap. Let $|E_{\text{new}}|$ be the number of MoE experts that the new failure would restore from checkpoint, and let $\mathcal{H}_W(t)$ be the set of past hybrid-recovery events that occurred in the exposure window $[t-W, t)$. Each historical event $h \in \mathcal{H}_W(t)$ is logged with the size of its restored expert set $|E_h|$ and its gap $\Delta_h$. MoEGuard then computes the projected expert-weighted staleness density~(\ref{eq:phi-prime}):
\begin{equation}
    \Phi'(t) \;=\; \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{h \in \mathcal{H}_W(t)} |E_h|\cdot \Delta_h \;+\; |E_{\text{new}}|\cdot \Delta}{N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W}.
\end{equation}

MoEGuard selects hybrid recovery only when
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \geq \Delta_{\min} \;\wedge\; \Delta \leq \Delta_{\max} \;\wedge\; \Phi'(t) \leq \Phi_{\max}.
\end{equation}
Otherwise, it selects checkpoint restart.

This guarded policy separates three cases. If $\Delta < \Delta_{\min}$, replay is cheap and checkpoint restart is preferred. If $\Delta > \Delta_{\max}$, a single hybrid event would inject too much per-event staleness on the affected experts and restart is used instead. If $\Phi'(t) > \Phi_{\max}$, the cluster has already accumulated too high an expert-weighted staleness density inside the window, and restart is used to reset the global expert state to a fully consistent checkpoint snapshot.

MoEGuard records every policy decision as structured metadata, including the failed rank, checkpoint gap, $|E_{\text{new}}|$, the running components of $\Phi'(t)$, the selected path, and the decision reason. This makes recovery behavior auditable and allows later correlation with training quality metrics.

\subsection{MoE-Aware Hybrid State Restoration}

The main innovation of MoEGuard is to restore different classes of training state from different sources.

For dense, shared, and router states, the replacement rank synchronizes the latest parameters and optimizer states from healthy data-parallel peers. In pipeline-parallel training, the synchronization source is a healthy rank with the same pipeline-stage coordinate as the failed rank but in a different data-parallel pipeline. This ensures that the recovered dense and router states match the current training step rather than the latest checkpoint.

For MoE expert states, MoEGuard restores the experts owned by the failed rank from the latest distributed checkpoint. These experts are rank-local and may not have a fresh in-memory replica on another rank. MoEGuard uses checkpoint metadata to locate the affected expert weights and optimizer states, and restores only the state belonging to the failed rank. A key challenge is locating the correct per-rank checkpoint shard. When using the \texttt{torch} checkpoint format, each rank saves an independent \texttt{.pt} file under:
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{iter\_XXXXXXX/mp\_rank\_\{tp:02d\}\_\{pp:03d\}\_\{ep:03d\}/model\_optim\_rng.pt}
\end{equation}
which can be loaded with \texttt{torch.load()} without any collective communication. The loading function resolves the replacement rank's TP, PP, and EP ranks to construct the correct path. With pipeline parallelism, global MoE layer indices must be mapped to PP-stage-local indices:
\begin{equation}
    \text{local\_idx} = \text{layer\_id} - (\text{pp\_rank} \times L + 1)
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the number of layers per PP stage. The default \texttt{torch\_dist} checkpoint format is incompatible with single-rank selective loading because \texttt{dist\_checkpointing.load()} requires \texttt{all\_gather\_object}, a collective operation that deadlocks when called from a single rank's async recovery callback. MoEGuard therefore uses the \texttt{torch} format when hybrid recovery is enabled.

This creates a hybrid recovered state: dense and router states are fresh, while affected expert states may be stale by $\Delta$ iterations. MoEGuard bounds this staleness through the expert-weighted-staleness guarded policy. The result is a recovery path that avoids full-job rollback while keeping the quality risk explicit and controlled.

\subsection{Weights-First, Optimizer-Later Recovery}

Expert optimizer states can be large and expensive to load. To reduce the time before training can resume, MoEGuard supports a two-phase recovery protocol.

In Phase~A, MoEGuard restores the weights required for forward and backward execution. It synchronizes dense and router weights from healthy data-parallel peers and restores affected expert weights from checkpoint. After weights and runtime metadata are available, the repaired rank can rejoin training under a guarded state.

In Phase~B, MoEGuard attaches optimizer states. Dense and router optimizer states are synchronized from healthy peers. Expert optimizer states are restored from checkpoint and may be loaded after the expert weights. Until the expert optimizer states are fully attached, MoEGuard keeps an update barrier on the affected experts to prevent unsafe optimizer updates. Once the optimizer state is available, the barrier is removed and the rank becomes fully recovered.

This protocol distinguishes \emph{time-to-resume} from \emph{time-to-full-recovery}. Time-to-resume measures when training can continue making progress, while time-to-full-recovery measures when all optimizer states have been restored and normal update semantics have been restored.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{two_phase_recovery.png}

Fig.~\ref{fig:two-phase-recovery} visualizes the two-phase protocol. By overlapping forward/backward execution on the recovered weights with the still-running optimizer-state restoration, MoEGuard pushes time-to-resume down to the cost of weight transfer alone, while the update barrier on the affected experts keeps the parameter-update semantics safe until time-to-full-recovery is reached.

\subsection{Checkpoint Restart Fallback}

Checkpoint restart remains the conservative recovery path in MoEGuard. When selected, MoEGuard restores the full model state, optimizer state, scheduler state, and runtime metadata from the latest checkpoint. The job then replays iterations from checkpoint step $c$ to failure step $t$.

MoEGuard uses checkpoint restart in three situations. First, when the checkpoint gap is below $\Delta_{\min}$, restart is cheaper and simpler than hybrid recovery. Second, when the checkpoint gap exceeds $\Delta_{\max}$, a single hybrid event would introduce excessive expert staleness. Third, when the cluster's running expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ would exceed $\Phi_{\max}$, restart resets the global expert state to a fully consistent checkpoint snapshot.

Keeping checkpoint restart as a fallback is important for training correctness. MoEGuard does not assume hybrid recovery is always safe; it uses hybrid recovery only inside the bounded region defined by the guarded policy.

\subsection{Reintegration and Runtime Metadata Repair}

State restoration alone is insufficient for MoE recovery. The runtime also needs to repair the metadata used for routing tokens and executing distributed communication.

After state restoration, MoEGuard refreshes the expert directory, dispatch topology, and process-group view. The expert directory records which rank owns each expert. The dispatch topology determines how tokens are routed to experts. The process-group view determines which ranks participate in collective communication. If these metadata are stale, the model may route tokens to an unrecovered rank or use invalid communication groups.

MoEGuard's reintegration manager moves the repaired rank through a controlled state transition: recovering, repaired-but-not-routed, routed, and healthy. The rank is not allowed to receive normal expert traffic until the required weights, metadata, and update guards are ready. This prevents premature reintegration and makes recovery behavior observable.

\subsection{Observability and Validation Support}

MoEGuard treats logging as part of the recovery design. Each recovery event produces structured records that include failure cause, failed rank, checkpoint step, checkpoint gap, selected path, $|E_{\text{new}}|$, the running components of $\Phi'(t)$, dense synchronization time, expert restoration time, optimizer attach time, replay time, and reintegration time.

These logs support two forms of validation. First, they make the runtime decision explainable: every recovery path can be traced back to policy inputs and thresholds. Second, they allow recovery events to be correlated with training behavior, including loss, perplexity, gradient norm, token drop rate, router load-balancing loss, and expert load balance.

This observability is necessary because recovery success in AI training is not binary. A job may resume execution while still suffering degraded training stability. MoEGuard therefore exposes both system-level and training-level signals for evaluating recovery correctness.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

This section evaluates whether MoEGuard can recover distributed MoE training failures efficiently while preserving training stability. Our evaluation answers the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} Does MoEGuard select the intended recovery path under different checkpoint gaps and expert-weighted staleness levels?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} How much recovery cost does MoEGuard reduce compared with checkpoint restart?
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} Does hybrid recovery affect training quality or stability?
    \item \textbf{RQ4.} How does MoEGuard behave under repeated failures, especially concentrated failures on the same rank?
    \item \textbf{RQ5.} What is the runtime overhead of MoEGuard during failure-free training?
    \item \textbf{RQ6.} Which design components contribute most to MoEGuard's benefit?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Checkpoint save interval.} Unless otherwise specified, all experiments save a full distributed checkpoint every \textbf{200 iterations} (\texttt{--save-interval 200} in Megatron-LM). We selected this value based on three considerations grounded in prior work and our cluster characterization. First, classical checkpoint-frequency analysis by Young~\cite{young1974first} and Daly~\cite{daly2006higher} shows that the optimal checkpoint interval $\tau^{*} \approx \sqrt{2 \cdot C \cdot M}$, where $C$ is the per-checkpoint cost and $M$ is the mean time between failures. With our measured per-checkpoint write cost of roughly 30--60\,s on shared storage and an LLM-training MTBF on the order of a few hours per job (consistent with the 45-minute average failure interval reported for Meta's 16K-GPU Llama-3 training run~\cite{dubey2024llama3} and the failure traces analyzed by MegaScale~\cite{jiang2024megascale}), Young/Daly's formula yields an optimal interval in the range of 100--300 iterations at our 10--13\,s per-iteration speed. Second, this range matches the operating points adopted by recent large-scale LLM training systems and fault-tolerance frameworks: Megatron-LM's reference recipes~\cite{narayanan2021megatron} use $\sim$100--500 iterations, CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq} advocates frequent fine-grained checkpoints with overhead $<$5\%, Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo} and Oobleck~\cite{obrien2023oobleck} report checkpoint cadences in the same regime, and Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini} explicitly studies sub-iteration to few-hundred-iteration intervals for in-memory checkpointing. Third, 200 iterations is large enough to amortize the $\sim$30--60\,s save cost (overhead $<$3\% of wall-clock time at our iteration speed) while small enough that the worst-case checkpoint gap $\Delta$ stays well within the safe single-recovery region characterized in Section~\ref{sec:rq3}, so that hybrid recovery introduces at most $\sim$200 iterations of expert staleness per failure---comfortably below the $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ expert-weighted staleness bound of Section~\ref{sec:approach}. We therefore fix \texttt{save-interval}~$=200$ as the default and treat it as a representative production-scale operating point rather than a free hyperparameter.

\subsection{Compared Systems}

We compare MoEGuard with the following baselines.

\textbf{NoFault.} Normal training without injected failures. This run provides the reference training trajectory.

\textbf{Restart.} Standard checkpoint restart. After failure, all ranks restore from the latest checkpoint and replay lost iterations.

\textbf{AlwaysHybrid.} Always use hybrid recovery whenever a failure occurs, ignoring gap, $\Delta_{\max}$, and the expert-weighted staleness density.

\textbf{FixedGap.} Use hybrid recovery when the checkpoint gap is above a fixed threshold, without tracking the cluster's expert-weighted staleness density.

\textbf{MoEGuard-w/o-Exposure.} MoEGuard without the expert-weighted staleness guard. This ablation uses only $\Delta_{\min}$ and $\Delta_{\max}$.

\textbf{MoEGuard-w/o-OptLater.} MoEGuard without weights-first, optimizer-later recovery. Expert weights and optimizer states must both be restored before the rank resumes training.

\textbf{MoEGuard.} The full system with safe-point repair, expert-weighted-staleness guarded policy, MoE-aware hybrid state restoration, two-phase recovery, and reintegration management.

\subsection{Policy Parameters}

The default $\Delta_{\min}$ is derived from a \emph{1.2$\times$ end-to-end speedup} requirement: MoEGuard only switches from checkpoint restart to hybrid recovery if the full restart cost---checkpoint load plus iteration replay---is at least 1.2$\times$ the hybrid-recovery cost on the same cluster. Using the measured values from Table~\ref{tab:pre_timing} ($T_{\text{load}}=35.951$\,s, $T_{\text{iter}}=10.309$\,s, $T_{\text{hybrid}}=28.7908$\,s), this becomes
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{load}} + \Delta \cdot T_{\text{iter}} \;\geq\; 1.2 \cdot T_{\text{hybrid}},
\end{equation}
which simplifies to $\Delta \geq (1.2 \cdot 28.7908 - 35.951)/10.309 = -0.142$. In other words, on our cluster the checkpoint-load cost alone (35.95\,s) is already $\sim$1.25$\times$ the hybrid-recovery cost (28.79\,s), so the 1.2$\times$ inequality is satisfied for every $\Delta \geq 1$. The smallest non-trivial gap that still admits hybrid recovery is therefore $\Delta_{\min} = 1$\,iter, i.e., the threshold is in practice \emph{non-binding} under our measured timings: whenever a failure is detected, hybrid recovery is already at least 1.2$\times$ faster than checkpoint restart. We keep $\Delta_{\min}$ as a first-class policy knob, however, because it acts as a \emph{defensive guard} for robustness: on clusters with much faster checkpoint loading (e.g., NVMe-backed or in-memory checkpoints~\cite{wang2023gemini}) the inequality can flip for small $\Delta$, and operators can raise $\Delta_{\min}$ to avoid switching to hybrid when restart would actually be cheaper. Throughout our experiments we therefore report $\Delta_{\min}=1$, but the mechanism itself remains in the policy.

The default $\Delta_{\max} = 200$ iterations matches the checkpoint save interval (Section~\ref{sec:eval}), which is itself the natural upper bound on any single-failure gap: by construction no failure can be more than \texttt{save-interval} iterations away from the latest checkpoint. $\Delta_{\max}$ is therefore non-binding on a single failure within one save interval, and is included as a defensive guard for operating regimes that disable or stretch the save interval.

The default $\Phi_{\max} = 1\times 10^{-2}$ bounds the expert-weighted staleness density that the cluster can accumulate inside the exposure window $W = 20000$ before MoEGuard falls back to checkpoint restart. The value is set from a $3\times 4$ multi-rank burst sweep (Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}, detailed below): in the seven cells with $\Phi'(t)\leq 10^{-2}$ the post-recovery loss stays inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band, while in all five cells with $\Phi'(t)>10^{-2}$ the deviation rises to $1.21$--$1.98\sigma_{\text{base}}$ and starts touching the baseline $\pm 2\sigma$ edge. To put $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ on a concrete scale: with $N_{\text{expert}}=128$, $W=20000$, $|E_{\text{new}}| = 16$ (a single failed rank in our PP$=8$, EP$=8$ layout owns $128/8 = 16$ experts), and $\Delta \leq \Delta_{\max} = 200$, a single-rank hybrid-recovery event contributes at most $16\cdot 200 / (128\cdot 20000) = 1.25\times 10^{-3}$ to $\Phi'(t)$, so $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ admits roughly eight worst-case single-rank hybrid recoveries per window before falling back to restart---enough headroom for realistic failure rates while still cutting off the multi-rank burst regime where Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault} shows quality degradation.

This cliff is well predicted by the structural arguments stated for the single-rank case~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,zoph2022stmoe,komatsuzaki2023sparse}. As long as $|E_{\text{new}}|/N_{\text{expert}}$ stays small, the auxiliary load-balancing loss can absorb the staleness on the affected expert subset within the next few iterations, and the asymmetry between non-expert and expert updates~\cite{komatsuzaki2023sparse,zoph2022stmoe} keeps the dense/router-fresh hybrid state on the empirically benign side. As $|F|$ and $\Delta$ jointly grow $\Phi'(t)$ past $10^{-2}$, the staleness debt exceeds what auxiliary balancing can reabsorb in the same window and the loss starts diverging from baseline. The data therefore support $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ as a \emph{quality-driven} bound rather than a heuristic: it is the largest density at which all measured cells stay inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band, and the smallest density at which any cell would otherwise fall outside it.

We therefore retain $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ as the default. Two remarks frame the role of this bound. (i)~Below $\Phi_{\max}$, MoEGuard's hybrid recovery is preferred because it preserves quality \emph{and} avoids full-job rollback; the single-rank 50-run sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:fault-heatmap}) lives entirely in this region, which is why $\Phi_{\max}$ is not approached by quality damage in failure-free or single-rank settings. (ii)~Above $\Phi_{\max}$, MoEGuard automatically falls back to checkpoint restart, resetting the global expert state to a fully consistent snapshot and zeroing out the staleness debt for events outside the new exposure window. The multi-rank sweep above shows precisely why this fallback exists: without it, repeated or large bursts would push $\Phi'(t)$ into the regime where the loss curve starts measurably degrading.

    \item $\Delta_{\max} \in \{100, 200, 400, 800\}$
    \item $\Phi_{\max} \in \{2.5\times 10^{-3}, 5\times 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}, 1.5\times 10^{-2}\}$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RQ1: Recovery Path Selection}
\label{sec:rq1}

This experiment verifies whether MoEGuard chooses the intended recovery path. We inject one rank failure at controlled checkpoint gaps:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \in \{8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1000, 1500\}
\end{equation}
For each failure, we record the selected path, decision reason, checkpoint gap, failed rank, the components of $\Phi'(t)$ before and after recovery, and $|E_{\text{new}}|$.

Expected behavior is: at the measured $\Delta_{\min}=1$ all injected gaps within $[\Delta_{\min}, \Delta_{\max}]$ trigger hybrid recovery (since hybrid is already $\geq$1.2$\times$ faster than restart on our cluster); however, failures whose $\Delta$ exceeds $\Delta_{\max}$ or whose projected $\Phi'(t)$ would exceed $\Phi_{\max}$ are still expected to fall back to checkpoint restart. To additionally stress the small-gap branch of the policy, we re-run the smallest injected gaps ($\Delta \in \{8, 16, 32\}$) with $\Delta_{\min}$ temporarily raised to $64$; in that configuration the small gaps must instead be handled by checkpoint restart.

Result: MoEGuard selected the expected path in all injected failures. Under the default $\Delta_{\min}=1$ and $\Delta_{\max}=200$, all single-failure injections with $\Delta \leq 200$ were handled by hybrid recovery, the $\Delta=256, 512, 1000, 1500$ injections were correctly rejected by the $\Delta_{\max}$ guard and fell back to checkpoint restart, and synthetic failure traces whose projected $\Phi'(t)$ exceeded $\Phi_{\max}$ were also conservatively redirected to restart. Under the stress configuration with $\Delta_{\min}=64$, the small-gap injections were correctly routed to checkpoint restart, while larger-gap injections continued to use hybrid recovery. This experiment validates that MoEGuard's policy is not merely a hardcoded hybrid trigger; it uses the gap, the per-event upper bound, and the global expert-weighted staleness density to bound recovery risk, and the $\Delta_{\min}$ guard remains functional even when the default value is non-binding on the current cluster.

\subsection{RQ2: Single-Failure Recovery Cost}
\label{sec:rq2}

This experiment measures the per-event end-to-end recovery cost of MoEGuard under a single rank failure. We inject one hard failure after warmup and run a $2\times 2$ factorial design that crosses the two recovery design choices independently: \textbf{(i)~hybrid restore} (peer-pull dense/router + selective expert restore from per-rank checkpoint shards) versus \textbf{full checkpoint restart}, and \textbf{(ii)~two-phase} (weights-first, optimizer-later) versus \textbf{single-phase} (weights and optimizer restored together before training resumes). The recorded latency is the wall time from failure detection until the replacement rank produces its first post-recovery training iteration with all parameters and optimizer state attached.

\textbf{Main effects.} Averaging across the orthogonal factor:
the \emph{hybrid restore} effect is $-5.499$\,s (a $15.1\%$ reduction relative to the $36.417$\,s full-restart baseline), and the \emph{two-phase} effect is $-2.004$\,s (a $5.5\%$ reduction). The two mechanisms are therefore \emph{independent and additive}: subtracting both main effects from the full-restart baseline recovers the joint configuration ($36.417-5.499-2.004=28.914$\,s), matching the measured \textbf{hybrid}+\textbf{two-phase} cell to the precision of the experiment.

\textbf{Mechanism.} The hybrid effect comes from replacing NVMe-bound full-tensor restoration with peer-pulled dense/router state plus a single-rank expert shard read; this scales with the recovered I/O volume and dominates when the optimizer slice is small enough to be loaded synchronously. The two-phase effect comes from overlapping the optimizer-state restoration with the post-resume forward/backward of the replacement rank: as soon as weights are in place the rank produces its first iteration, while the residual optimizer load runs concurrently under an update barrier on the affected experts. The overlap window is bounded by the cost of one training iteration, so the two-phase saving is roughly constant ($\approx 2$\,s) across both restore paths---which is exactly what Table~\ref{tab:single} shows.

\subsection{RQ3: Training Quality and Stability}
\label{sec:rq3}

Hybrid recovery is useful only if it does not silently damage training. We therefore compare recovered training trajectories against NoFault and Restart using paired runs.

Each run follows: 1000 iterations warmup, inject one failure at a chosen gap, continue training for 1000 iterations, and compare against paired NoFault and Restart runs. We sweep single-failure gaps:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \in \{64, 128, 256, 512, 1000, 1500\}
\end{equation}

We report: training loss trajectory, validation loss, validation perplexity, gradient norm, gradient clipping rate, skipped iterations, NaN iterations, token drop rate, router auxiliary loss, and expert load coefficient of variation.

For gaps up to the checkpoint interval (which naturally bounds single-recovery staleness), MoEGuard's validation loss and perplexity remain close to Restart and NoFault under paired comparison. At larger gaps, the expert staleness becomes significant. This experiment determines the safe single-event gap region (informing $\Delta_{\max}$) and the safe global staleness density region (informing $\Phi_{\max}$): both bounds are set so that recovered runs remain statistically close to Restart on validation loss, perplexity, gradient stability, and routing stability.

\subsection{RQ4: Multiple Failures and Expert-Weighted Staleness Density}
\label{sec:rq4}

This experiment evaluates MoEGuard under multi-rank burst failures and directly validates the cliff-edge value $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ derived in Section~\ref{sec:approach}. We use the burst-failure trace generator \texttt{find\_multi\_fault.sh}, which injects $|F|$ simultaneous rank failures balanced across all PP stages and replays the rest of the training under MoEGuard's policy.

\textbf{Distributed failures:} failure 1 on rank $r_1$, failure 2 on rank $r_2$, failure 3 on rank $r_3$, where $r_1$, $r_2$, and $r_3$ are different ranks.

\textbf{Concentrated failures:} failure 1 on rank $r$, failure 2 on rank $r$, failure 3 on rank $r$, where all failures occur on the same logical rank.

We sweep the burst configuration $|F|\in\{8,16,24\}$ across $\Delta\in\{50, 100, 150, 200\}$ iterations after the latest checkpoint, the same $3\times 4$ grid summarized in Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}. For each cell we record the selected recovery path, the running components of $\Phi'(t)$, validation loss at iteration~600, validation perplexity, gradient norm, token drop rate, and expert load coefficient of variation. The expected $\Phi'(t)$ for each cell is given by $|E_{\text{new}}|\Delta/(N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W) = 16|F|\Delta/(128\cdot 20000)$.

Expected behavior: FixedGap and AlwaysHybrid treat distributed and concentrated failures similarly and apply hybrid recovery to every cell. MoEGuard distinguishes them through the global expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$: in the seven cells with $\Phi'(t)\leq \Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ MoEGuard selects hybrid recovery and post-recovery loss is expected to stay inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band; in the five cells with $\Phi'(t)>\Phi_{\max}$ MoEGuard falls back to checkpoint restart, which resets the global expert state to the last consistent checkpoint and zeros out the staleness debt for events outside the new exposure window. We additionally re-run the five over-threshold cells with the $\Phi_{\max}$ guard temporarily disabled (\textsc{MoEGuard-w/o-Exposure}, hybrid forced) to measure how far the loss would drift if the cliff edge were ignored.

Result: under the default policy MoEGuard correctly applies hybrid recovery on the seven $\Phi'(t)\leq 10^{-2}$ cells (with measured loss deviations $\leq 0.71\sigma_{\text{base}}$, all inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band, Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}) and conservatively redirects the five $\Phi'(t)>10^{-2}$ cells to checkpoint restart, where post-restart loss returns inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band. With $\Phi_{\max}$ disabled and hybrid forced, the same five cells reproduce the loss values shown in Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault} (deviations $1.21$--$1.98\sigma_{\text{base}}$, with the $|F|=24$ rows touching the $\pm 2\sigma$ edge), confirming that the fallback is what carries the quality result above the cliff. This confirms that an expert-weighted, window-level staleness density captures the risk that global gap-only policies miss, while still admitting the common low-density failure pattern that benefits most from hybrid recovery.

\subsection{RQ5: Failure-Free Overhead}
\label{sec:rq5}

MoEGuard adds runtime checks, metadata tracking, and structured logging. We measure its overhead during failure-free training. We compare NoFault training with and without MoEGuard enabled and report: average iteration time, p50/p95 iteration time, tokens per second, GPU memory overhead, logging overhead, controller overhead, and extra communication volume.

MoEGuard introduces negligible throughput overhead in failure-free training because $\Phi'(t)$ accounting and recovery logging are lightweight, and dense/router synchronization or expert restoration occurs only during recovery.

\subsection{Scalability}

Finally, we evaluate MoEGuard at different scales: 16, 32, 64, and 128 GPUs. We keep the model architecture proportional where necessary and report recovery cost breakdown, time-to-resume, and throughput before and after recovery.

MoEGuard's recovery cost grows more slowly than checkpoint restart because it avoids full-job rollback and limits checkpoint access to affected expert state. At 128 GPUs, MoEGuard reduces recovery time significantly compared with Restart.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Elastic training.} River \cite{jiang2021river} and Bamboo \cite{thorpe2023bamboo} enable dynamic resource scaling but do not address expert-specific recovery in MoE models. Oobleck \cite{obrien2023oobleck} uses pipeline-parallel recovery with fault tolerance but does not exploit MoE structure.

\textbf{Checkpoint optimization.} Async checkpointing \cite{mohan2021checkfreq} and incremental checkpoints reduce save overhead but do not address the fundamental all-rank participation requirement of \texttt{torch\_dist} loading. Selective checkpoint loading \cite{huang2023freeze} focuses on layer freezing rather than per-expert recovery.

\textbf{MoE fault tolerance.} Prior work on MoE fault tolerance primarily relies on expert replication \cite{fedus2022switch} or expert offloading, which trades model capacity for resilience. MoEGuard achieves fault tolerance without reducing effective model capacity by leveraging the natural redundancy across data-parallel groups.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented MoEGuard, a self-healing runtime framework for fault-tolerant MoE training. By exploiting MoE state heterogeneity and switching from \texttt{torch\_dist} to \texttt{torch} checkpoint format, MoEGuard enables selective per-rank expert loading without collective communication. PP-aware layer index mapping ensures correct parameter restoration in pipeline-parallel settings. On a 128-expert MoE model, MoE-aware hybrid restore and weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase recovery contribute additively to per-event recovery latency, jointly reducing single-failure end-to-end recovery wall time by $20.6\%$ relative to a full checkpoint restart at the same checkpoint format and gap, while keeping post-recovery validation loss inside the $\pm 1\sigma$ baseline band on the $|F|\!\times\!\Delta$ multi-fault sweep up to the empirical cliff edge $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$. Future work includes extending hybrid recovery to the \texttt{torch\_dist} format via selective shard loading and supporting multi-fault concurrent recovery.
