\begin{abstract}
Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) training systems are increasingly deployed as production AI software, yet their runtime failure recovery remains difficult to test, repair, and validate. A single GPU failure can invalidate only part of the training state, but existing recovery mechanisms typically rely on coarse-grained checkpoint restart, forcing the entire training job to reload stale global state and replay lost progress. More importantly, current training software provides limited support for reasoning about whether a partial recovery has preserved model quality, routing stability, and optimizer consistency.
This paper presents MoEGambit, a runtime reliability framework for engineering self-healing distributed MoE training. MoEGambit provides two core capabilities: safe-point repair for preventing invalid optimizer commits, and expert-weighted-staleness guarded recovery for selecting between checkpoint restart and hybrid state restoration. Instead of treating recovery risk as a global property of the training job, MoEGambit tracks an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ over a sliding exposure window. This design jointly accounts for how many iterations of staleness each hybrid recovery injects and how large a fraction of the expert population has been exposed, enabling the system to use faster hybrid repair when safe and fall back to checkpoint restart when accumulated risk becomes high.
MoEGambit repairs failed ranks using MoE-aware recovery semantics: dense and router states are synchronized from healthy data-parallel neighbors, while affected expert states are restored from distributed checkpoints. When concurrent failures eliminate every healthy data-parallel peer at the failed rank's pipeline/tensor-parallel coordinate (so that no surviving peer holds a current-step replica of the dense/router state), MoEGambit transparently falls back to checkpoint restart, since hybrid recovery is by construction predicated on the existence of at least one such peer. A two-phase recovery protocol restores weights first and attaches optimizer state later to reduce time to resumed training. We implement MoEGambit in Megatron-LM and evaluate it with injected fault traces on H20 GPU clusters. Across single-failure, repeated-failure, concentrated-failure, and distributed-failure scenarios, MoEGambit reduces recovery cost relative to checkpoint restart while maintaining validation loss, perplexity, gradient stability, token drop rate, and expert load balance. These results demonstrate that an expert-weighted staleness density is an effective abstraction for repairing runtime failures in distributed AI training software.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become a dominant architecture for frontier large language models, powering systems such as Mixtral~\cite{jiang2024mixtral}, DeepSeek-MoE~\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe}, and Qwen3-MoE~\cite{qwen3report}, because they decouple model capacity from per-token compute by routing each input through only a small subset of experts. Training these models at scale, however, is a long-running, multi-week, multi-thousand-GPU software process in which failures are routine rather than exceptional. Meta's Llama-3 training trace reports a failure roughly every 45\,minutes across a 16K-GPU cluster~\cite{dubey2024llama3}; MegaScale documents hundreds of distinct failure modes during a 10K-GPU production deployment~\cite{jiang2024megascale}; the OPT-175B logbook records weeks of human-in-the-loop intervention~\cite{zhang2022opt}. Because the dominant recovery mechanism in production is \emph{checkpoint restart}---reload the most recent globally consistent snapshot and replay the lost iterations---each failure event on a large MoE job typically consumes hundreds of seconds of checkpoint I/O plus hundreds of replayed iterations, costing thousands of GPU-seconds per event and, cumulatively, days of wall time over a training run.

A substantial body of fault-tolerant training systems has been developed to reduce this cost, but almost all of it targets dense models. CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq}, DeepFreeze~\cite{nicolae2020deepfreeze}, and Check-N-Run~\cite{eisenman2022check} shrink per-checkpoint cost via asynchronous and incremental writes; Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini} replaces NVMe writes with in-memory replicas; Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo} hides checkpointing behind redundancy on preemptible instances; Oobleck~\cite{jang2023oobleck} and ReCycle~\cite{gandhi2024recycle} avoid rolling back to any saved snapshot at all by reconfiguring pipeline schedules. None of these systems addresses MoE-specific cost asymmetries: the bulk of a MoE checkpoint is per-expert optimizer state, and the dominant Megatron-LM distributed checkpoint format (\texttt{torch\_dist}) requires every rank to participate in the load through a collective \texttt{all\_gather\_object}, which forbids single-rank selective restoration by construction. The only MoE-specific fault-tolerance system we are aware of, MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc}, optimizes \emph{save-side} cost via Partial Experts Checkpointing but at recovery time still falls back to full global checkpoint restart---paying the full load + replay bill on every failure and leaving the recovery critical path essentially unchanged.

The gap is therefore a \emph{recovery-side} fault-tolerance framework that is structured around the heterogeneous state of MoE training---one that can repair a failed rank without rolling back the whole job, and do so in a way that is fast, model-quality-preserving, and stable across repeated failures. We present \textbf{MoEGambit}\footnote{The name reflects the system's core design philosophy: like a chess gambit, MoEGambit deliberately tolerates a bounded amount of expert staleness ($\Delta$ steps, bounded by $\Delta_{\max}$ and $\Phi_{\max}$) in order to skip global checkpoint restart and resume training at memory-to-memory speed, trading a small, quantified positional sacrifice for a decisive recovery-time advantage. The ``MoE'' prefix names the domain; ``Gambit'' names the move.}, such a framework, integrated into Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron}. The core technical idea exploits a structural asymmetry of distributed MoE training: dense parameters and routers are \emph{replicated} across the data-parallel (DP) dimension, while only the expert parameters and their optimizer state are EP-sharded. When a rank fails, the replacement rank therefore does not need to read dense state from disk at all---it can synchronize the current-step dense and router parameters directly from a healthy DP peer, and only the rank-local expert weights and expert optimizer state need to be loaded from the latest checkpoint shard. The intrinsic redundancy that the DP dimension already provides for free is, in other words, sufficient to repair the much larger replicated half of the state at memory-to-memory speed, leaving only the genuinely rank-unique experts on the disk path. A weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase protocol further overlaps optimizer-state restoration with the replacement rank's resumed forward/backward computation, so time-to-resume is bounded by a single weight transfer rather than by the full optimizer load.

We implement MoEGambit in Megatron-LM and evaluate it on Qwen3-30B-A3B~\cite{qwen3report} (48 layers, 128 routed experts, top-8 routing) on a 64-GPU H20-3e cluster with FineWeb~\cite{penedo2024fineweb} as the pre-training corpus. Compared to MoC-System on identical hardware and identical checkpoint gap, MoEGambit reduces end-to-end recovery wall time by roughly $4\times$ on single-rank failures and by $3\times$--$5\times$ across single-, repeated-, and concentrated-failure scenarios. Because every recovered run is paired with a NoFault baseline using identical seed, data order, checkpoint, and injected failure step~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}, we can attribute residual model-quality differences specifically to recovery rather than to seed noise: across all evaluated scenarios, post-recovery validation loss, perplexity, gradient norms, token drop rate, and expert load balance all stay within the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band, i.e., model quality is essentially unaffected by recovery.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A formulation of MoE training fault tolerance as a recovery-side problem distinct from save-side checkpoint optimization, and an articulation of why state-of-the-art dense-oriented systems (Bamboo, Oobleck, ReCycle, Gemini) and MoC-System leave this side unsolved (\S\ref{sec:background}).
    \item A DP-redundancy-driven hybrid recovery mechanism that synchronizes dense and router state from healthy DP peers at the current step and restores only the rank-local expert weights and optimizer state from per-rank checkpoint shards, together with a two-phase weights-first / optimizer-later protocol that further reduces time-to-resume below time-to-full-recovery (\S\ref{sec:moegambit}).
    \item A safe-point repair protocol that prevents partial optimizer commits during recovery, an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ used as a runtime-checkable guard against accumulated quality risk over repeated hybrid recoveries, and structured recovery logs that make every recovery decision auditable (\S\ref{sec:moegambit}).
    \item An empirical evaluation against MoC-System and full checkpoint restart on Qwen3-30B-A3B, showing $3\times$--$5\times$ end-to-end recovery speedup while keeping model quality inside the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

This section establishes the minimum context required to read the rest of the paper. §\ref{sec:bg-concept} defines distributed MoE training as a stateful software system and identifies the heterogeneous state we will repair. §\ref{sec:bg-flow} describes the de facto recovery flow in production---checkpoint restart---and the structural reason it fails to exploit MoE-specific redundancy. §\ref{sec:bg-scope} narrows the discussion to the concrete technical surface that MoEGambit operates on: the distributed checkpoint formats supported by Megatron-LM, and the research scope of this paper. Comparison with prior fault-tolerance \emph{systems} is deferred to §\ref{sec:related}.

\subsection{Concepts: MoE Training as a Heterogeneous-State Distributed Program}
\label{sec:bg-concept}

A distributed MoE training job~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard,fedus2022switch,rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe,hwang2023tutel,gale2023megablocks} is a long-running, stateful, multi-process program executing collective communication on tens to thousands of GPUs. Unlike dense Transformers, MoE training maintains a \emph{heterogeneous state} that we partition along its data-parallel (DP), tensor-parallel (TP), pipeline-parallel (PP), and expert-parallel (EP) axes (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Replicated state.} Dense layers, shared parameters, and router weights are replicated across every DP rank that owns the same PP/TP coordinate. At any iteration $t$ at least one healthy peer holds the current step's value.
    \item \emph{Sharded expert state.} Expert weights and their AdamW first/second moments are partitioned across EP ranks. In a $128$-expert, EP$=8$ layout, each rank exclusively owns a fixed subset of $128/8=16$ experts; no peer holds a current in-memory replica.
    \item \emph{Runtime metadata.} The expert directory, the dispatch topology used by all-to-all routing~\cite{he2022fastermoe,zhai2023smartmoe}, and the process-group view are derived from the rank layout and become stale the moment any rank is replaced.
\end{itemize}
The software-engineering consequence is that \emph{the same failure invalidates different state classes with different recovery semantics}---a property that the rest of this section will show standard recovery flows do not exploit.

\subsection{Existing Flow: Checkpoint Restart and the Hidden Risk of Partial Recovery}
\label{sec:bg-flow}

The de facto recovery mechanism in production LLM training is \emph{checkpoint restart}~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,dubey2024llama3,jiang2024megascale}: on a failure, the job reloads the most recent globally consistent snapshot at step $c$ and replays the $t-c$ lost iterations. Classical first-order analysis~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher} shows that the optimal checkpoint interval scales as $\tau^{*}\!\approx\!\sqrt{2CM}$, where $C$ is the per-checkpoint cost and $M$ is the mean time between failures; with $M$ collapsing to tens of minutes on 10K-GPU clusters~\cite{dubey2024llama3,jiang2024megascale}, every individual failure costs hundreds of seconds of checkpoint I/O plus hundreds of replayed iterations and, cumulatively, days of wall time per training run.

Restart treats the entire job as a single all-or-nothing state object. Looking at Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}, this is structurally wasteful: a single failed rank invalidates only its EP-sharded expert state, while the DP-replicated dense/router state of every other rank at the same PP/TP coordinate remains current at step $t$. A natural alternative is \emph{hybrid recovery}: synchronize dense/router state from a healthy DP peer at the current step, and load only the rank-local experts from the latest checkpoint shard at step $c$. Hybrid recovery, however, introduces a side effect that restart does not: the recovered rank's expert state is now stale by exactly $\Delta = t-c$ iterations relative to the rest of the model.

This staleness is not a binary defect---restored experts still produce valid forward/backward signals---but it perturbs the auxiliary-loss-driven balancing dynamics that MoE training depends on~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,zoph2022stmoe,komatsuzaki2023sparse}. Two regimes of risk arise:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Per-event staleness.} A single hybrid recovery exposes the experts owned by the failed rank to stale-by-$\Delta$ weights. As long as the affected fraction $|E_{\text{new}}|/N_{\text{expert}}$ stays small, the auxiliary load-balancing loss can reabsorb this staleness within the next few hundred iterations.
    \item \emph{Window-level staleness debt.} Because each rank owns a stable subset of experts, repeated hybrid recoveries---even distributed across different ranks---accumulate stale-by-$\Delta$ exposure across an increasingly large fraction of the expert population. No per-failure or per-rank metric (checkpoint gap, time since last failure, number of past failures) captures this accumulating risk.
\end{enumerate}
A recovery system that admits hybrid repair therefore needs more than a fast mechanism; it needs a \emph{runtime-checkable bound} on both regimes. The MoEGambit policy in §\ref{sec:policy} is exactly that bound.

\subsection{Scope: Distributed Checkpoint Formats and the Surface of This Paper}
\label{sec:bg-scope}

The feasibility of hybrid recovery in production training runtimes is not a tuning choice---it is a checkpoint-format choice. Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,korthikanti2023reducing} supports two checkpoint formats whose semantics directly determine whether hybrid recovery can run at all:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{torch} (legacy): each rank saves its own \texttt{.pt} file under \texttt{iter\_XXXXXXX/mp\_rank\_\{tp\}\_\{pp\}\_\{ep\}/\\model\_optim\_rng.pt}. These files can be loaded independently by each rank using \texttt{torch.load()}, which requires no collective communication---making them suitable for selective expert loading inside an asynchronous recovery callback.
    \item \texttt{torch\_dist} (default): uses distributed checkpointing with sharded state dicts and \texttt{metadata.json}. Loading requires \texttt{dist\_checkpointing.load()}, which internally calls \texttt{all\_gather\_object}---a collective operation requiring every rank to participate. This format is fundamentally incompatible with single-rank selective loading and deadlocks when called from a single rank's recovery thread.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope of this paper.} MoEGambit operates on the recovery side of MoE training and commits to the \texttt{torch} format so that single-rank expert shards can be read collective-free. Within this scope it answers two questions: (i)~how should the heterogeneous state of Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance} be reconstituted on a failure so that recovery is fast on the wall clock and statistically indistinguishable from failure-free training on model quality (§\ref{sec:moegambit}); and (ii)~when does cluster-wide accumulated expert staleness exceed the regime in which hybrid repair is safe, so that the system must fall back to checkpoint restart (§\ref{sec:policy}). Save-side optimization (e.g., partial-experts or in-memory checkpointing), pipeline-template reconfiguration, non-fail-stop fault models, and the \texttt{torch\_dist} format are outside this scope; we contrast MoEGambit with the corresponding lines of prior work in §\ref{sec:related} and discuss the limits of our assumptions in §\ref{sec:threats}.

\section{MoEGambit}
\label{sec:moegambit}

\subsection{Problem Statement: Input, Output, Goals, Assumptions, and Requirements}
\label{sec:problem}

We first state precisely what MoEGambit is asked to do and what it is allowed to assume. This subsection is intentionally self-contained: a reader who skips the rest of §\ref{sec:moegambit} should still be able to reproduce the recovery contract from §\ref{sec:problem} alone.

\textbf{Input.} A live distributed MoE training job running with $(\text{TP},\text{PP},\text{DP},\text{EP})$ parallelism on $G$ GPUs, using Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,korthikanti2023reducing} as the runtime, the \texttt{torch} checkpoint format (§\ref{sec:bg-scope}), and an external fail-stop failure signal published by the existing watchdog / process-manager infrastructure. At step $t$, MoEGambit receives a \emph{failure event} $\langle r, t, c \rangle$ where $r$ is the failed logical rank, $t$ is the training step at which the failure was detected, and $c\!\le\!t$ is the step of the most recent globally consistent checkpoint.

\textbf{Output.} For each failure event, MoEGambit produces (i)~a deterministic \emph{recovery decision} $\pi(t)\in\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$, (ii)~a fully reconstructed and reintegrated replacement rank such that the job resumes producing post-recovery training iterations whose computation is byte-identical to a fresh \texttt{optimizer.step()} under the chosen recovery path, and (iii)~a structured recovery log entry that records the policy inputs, the chosen path, the per-segment recovery latency, and the rank's state-machine trace.

\textbf{Design goals.} MoEGambit is engineered against three explicit goals; each one separates it from generic checkpoint restart and pinpoints why a new system is needed.

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{G1. Recovery-side fast path.}] Reduce end-to-end recovery latency on a single rank failure to well below the full checkpoint-restart cost of Megatron-LM by exploiting the DP-replication redundancy that is already present at step $t$ on healthy peers, so that only the genuinely rank-unique expert state has to traverse the disk path.
    \item[\textbf{G2. Recovery-side quality contract.}] Bound the post-recovery training trajectory, not just the event of resumption. Concretely, every recovery decision must be justified against a runtime-checkable, cluster-wide quantity whose threshold is calibrated against the failure-free training noise floor, not picked by hand.
    \item[\textbf{G3. Auditability and structural honesty.}] Make every recovery decision traceable to its inputs and to its downstream model-quality outcome. When the structural pre-conditions of the fast path are violated, the system must fall back to checkpoint restart \emph{transparently}, with the reason recorded.
\end{description}

\textbf{Assumptions.} MoEGambit is correct under four assumptions consistent with standard fault-tolerant training settings~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo,jang2023oobleck,wang2023gemini}: \textbf{(A1)} the failure detector~\cite{jiang2024megascale} reports rank failures in a fail-stop model (no Byzantine ranks~\cite{dubey2024llama3}); \textbf{(A2)} at least one checkpoint exists for $c\!\geq\!0$; \textbf{(A3)} for every failed rank $r$, at least one healthy DP peer holds the current-step replica of $r$'s dense/router state at the same PP/TP coordinate; \textbf{(A4)} the failed rank can be replaced or restarted on a hot-spare GPU within the training job's process-manager infrastructure. (A3) is the structural pre-condition that distinguishes the fast path from restart: when a failure (or concurrent burst) eliminates every healthy DP peer at the failed rank's PP/TP coordinate, (A3) is violated and MoEGambit must fall back to checkpoint restart (§\ref{sec:policy}). Concurrent multi-rank failures, corrupted checkpoints, silent data corruption~\cite{chowdhery2023palm}, and bit-flips are deferred to §\ref{sec:threats}.

\textbf{Requirements (R1--R3).} The three design goals refine into three SE-style contract requirements that the rest of §\ref{sec:moegambit} instantiates one by one. We borrow the vocabulary of self-adaptive and self-healing systems~\cite{kephart2003autonomic,garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}; existing training runtimes are silent on all three.

\begin{description}
    \item[(R1) Safe-point pre-conditions.] A repair invoked in the middle of forward/backward propagation or an in-flight optimizer step may commit parameter updates derived from invalid local state. The runtime must define an explicit safe repair point and gate optimizer commits against it.
    \item[(R2) Runtime-checkable specifications.] Partial repair must be governed by a quantitative, $O(1)$-computable guard that bounds the staleness it injects into the cluster. The guard must distinguish single-event risk, cluster-wide accumulated risk, and per-event size, and its threshold must be supported by a data-driven cliff edge rather than a hand-tuned constant.
    \item[(R3) Traceable observability.] Every recovery decision must produce a structured record that links policy inputs, the selected path, and the downstream model-quality outcome---turning ``did recovery succeed?'' into a falsifiable question.
\end{description}

R1--R3 form the spine of the rest of this section: §\ref{sec:safepoint} instantiates R1; §\ref{sec:policy} instantiates R2 as the expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ and the policy function $\pi(t)$; §\ref{sec:obs} instantiates R3. The state-restoration mechanism (§\ref{sec:mechanism}) and the two-phase protocol (§\ref{sec:twophase}) are how MoEGambit repairs state \emph{within} the contract R2 defines. We deliberately separate \emph{what is guaranteed} (the contract) from \emph{how it is achieved} (the mechanism), so the recovery contract is auditable independently of the implementation~\cite{garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}.

\subsection{Core Abstractions}
\label{sec:abstractions}

Before describing modules, we define the four core abstractions used throughout §\ref{sec:moegambit}. Each abstraction is given a precise, measurable form so that it can appear in the structured log without ambiguity.

\textbf{(D1) Checkpoint gap $\Delta$.} For a failure event $\langle r, t, c \rangle$, the checkpoint gap is $\Delta = t - c$. In checkpoint restart $\Delta$ determines the number of iterations replayed; in hybrid recovery $\Delta$ determines the magnitude of expert-state staleness that the failed rank's experts are exposed to relative to the rest of the model.

\textbf{(D2) Heterogeneous state classes.} Following Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}, MoE training state at iteration $t$ partitions into \emph{replicated} (dense, shared, router; DP-replicated, freshly available from a healthy peer at step $t$), \emph{sharded expert} (weights and AdamW moments; EP-sharded, only available from a per-rank checkpoint shard at step $c\!<\!t$), and \emph{derived runtime metadata} (expert directory, dispatch topology, process-group view; recomputable from the rank layout). This three-way split is the fundamental object MoEGambit operates on: different classes have different fresh sources and therefore different recovery semantics.

\textbf{(D3) Safe repair point.} A program point at which (i)~the current iteration's gradients have not yet been applied by \texttt{optimizer.step()}, and (ii)~the runtime guarantees no in-flight commit will reference state computed under the \texttt{RECOVERING} mark. Safe repair points are the only program points at which state restoration is allowed to mutate the model parameters.

\textbf{(D4) Expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$.} A scalar, dimensionless, $O(1)$-maintainable summary of the cluster's accumulated post-recovery expert staleness in the sliding window $[t\!-\!W,\,t)$, weighted by per-event expert-set size $|E_h|$ and per-event gap $\Delta_h$. Its formal definition is deferred to (\ref{eq:phi-prime}) in §\ref{sec:policy} because it is the R2 instantiation; here we only note that $\Phi'(t)$ is the quantity the policy $\pi(t)$ guards against and the quantity the multi-rank burst sweep in §\ref{sec:eval} carves an empirical cliff edge through.

\textbf{Notation summary.} Throughout §\ref{sec:moegambit} we use: $r$ for the failed rank; $t$ for the failure step; $c$ for the latest checkpoint step; $\Delta = t - c$; $|E_h|$ for the size of the expert set restored by historical hybrid event $h$; $\Delta_h$ for the gap of that event; $\mathcal{H}_W(t)$ for the set of hybrid events in $[t-W,t)$; $W$ for the exposure window length; $N_{\text{expert}}$ for the total number of MoE experts in the model; $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$ for the binary structural predicate that encodes (A3).

\textbf{Why this structure rather than a monolithic recovery routine?} A naive design would fuse boxes (3)--(5) into a single recovery procedure that always reloads the full checkpoint after a safe-point boundary. We reject that design because it conflates three orthogonal concerns: \emph{when} is repair safe (box 2), \emph{whether} the fast path is admissible (box 3), and \emph{how} state is reconstituted (box 4). Conflating them makes the recovery contract impossible to audit independently of the recovery code, which is precisely the failure mode of existing training runtimes. Separating the boxes is what lets us state R1--R3 as contracts, instantiate each in a single subsection, and verify them experimentally one at a time in §\ref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{Safe-Point Repair Controller (R1)}
\label{sec:safepoint}

\textbf{Goal.} Instantiate R1 by defining a single program point at which repair may run, and a single invariant that forbids any optimizer commit derived from \texttt{RECOVERING}-marked state.

\textbf{Input.} A failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$ from the failure detector and, on every rank, a handle to the current iteration's \texttt{optimizer.step()} call site.

\textbf{Process.} On detection at step $t$, the controller atomically (i)~marks $r$ and its local experts as \texttt{RECOVERING}, (ii)~installs an optimizer commit guard---a pre-condition check inserted immediately before AdamW's parameter update---on every rank for the current iteration, and (iii)~marks the iteration as \texttt{DISCARD}. The guard enforces the explicit invariant ``no optimizer commit may use gradients computed under \texttt{RECOVERING}.'' Any rank reaching its \texttt{optimizer.step()} with the iteration in \texttt{DISCARD} skips the update entirely; the in-flight gradients are dropped. Training then resumes at the next iteration either from the safe repair point (hybrid path, §\ref{sec:mechanism}) or from the last checkpoint (restart path).

\textbf{Output.} A pair $\langle\text{safe-point id},\text{iteration status}\rangle$ where the safe-point id is the iteration boundary at which the repair is allowed to proceed and the status is one of $\{\textsc{Committed},\textsc{Discarded}\}$. The status is recorded in the structured log.

\textbf{Rationale and ``why not Y''.} A natural alternative is \emph{mid-iteration rollback}: re-execute only the partially completed sub-steps of the current iteration after repair, instead of discarding the whole iteration. We reject this design for two reasons. First, distributed AdamW updates can be partially applied across DP/EP ranks at the moment of failure, and reconciling those partial commits requires global state that no single rank holds---an issue observed as a soundness pitfall in earlier resilient-training systems~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo,jang2023oobleck}. Second, dropping a single $\sim$10\,s iteration is negligible compared with the tens-to-hundreds of seconds spent on the recovery path itself, so the engineering complexity of mid-iteration rollback buys essentially no wall-time benefit. The deliberate simplicity of R1's contract---``discard, never partially commit''---is therefore a design choice, not a limitation.

\subsection{Staleness-Density Guarded Policy (R2)}
\label{sec:policy}

\textbf{Goal.} Instantiate R2 by giving a quantitative, $O(1)$-computable guard $\Phi'(t)$ and a deterministic decision function $\pi(t)$ that maps each failure event to a recovery path.

\textbf{Input.} A failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$; the cluster's running sliding-window aggregate over $[t\!-\!W,t)$; the structural predicate $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$; three thresholds $(\Delta_{\min},\Delta_{\max},\Phi_{\max})$ (default values and provenance in §\ref{sec:rq1}--§\ref{sec:eval}).

\textbf{Process.} The module performs two computations and one comparison. \emph{(i) Aggregate update.} Maintain the running sum $S(t)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_W(t)}|E_h|\Delta_h$ as a single scalar; on entry/exit of the window this is an $O(1)$ increment/decrement. \emph{(ii) Projection.} For the incoming event compute the projected density assuming hybrid would be chosen:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:phi-prime}
    \Phi'(t) \;=\; \frac{S(t) \,+\, |E_{\text{new}}|\, \Delta}{N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W}.
\end{equation}
The numerator is the ``expert$\,\times\,$iteration'' staleness debt under that hypothesis; the denominator $N_{\text{expert}}\!\cdot\!W$ is the maximum debt the model could accumulate if every expert were stale on every iteration of $W$. $\Phi'(t)$ is dimensionless and lies in $[0,1]$. \emph{(iii) Decision.}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:policy}
    \pi(t) \;=\;
    \begin{cases}
        \textsc{Hybrid}, & \text{if } \textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t) \;\wedge\; \\
                         & \quad \Delta_{\min} \leq \Delta \leq \Delta_{\max} \;\wedge\; \\
                         & \quad \Phi'(t) \leq \Phi_{\max}, \\[2pt]
        \textsc{Restart}, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:policy} expresses the same logic as imperative pseudocode; the explicit order of the four guards is the order in which the structured log will record the decision reason if any one fails.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Staleness-density guarded recovery decision $\pi(t)$.}
\label{alg:policy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$; window aggregate $S(t)$; thresholds $\Delta_{\min},\Delta_{\max},\Phi_{\max}$; predicate $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$.
\Ensure decision $\pi(t)\in\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$; reason string.
\State $\Delta \gets t - c$
\If{$\neg\,\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``PeerAvail=false''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\If{$\Delta < \Delta_{\min}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``}\Delta\!<\!\Delta_{\min}\text{ (restart is cheaper)''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\If{$\Delta > \Delta_{\max}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``per-event staleness cap''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\State $|E_{\text{new}}| \gets$ number of experts owned by $r$
\State $\Phi'(t) \gets \big(S(t) + |E_{\text{new}}|\cdot\Delta\big)\,/\,\big(N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W\big)$
\If{$\Phi'(t) > \Phi_{\max}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``window-level staleness cap''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\langle\textsc{Hybrid},\ \text{``all guards passed''}\rangle$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Output.} A pair $\langle\pi(t),\text{reason}\rangle$ plus the four individual values $\Delta$, $|E_{\text{new}}|$, $S(t)$, $\Phi'(t)$ that drove the decision; all five are written to the structured log entry created in §\ref{sec:obs}.

\textbf{Rationale and ``why not Y''.} Three obvious alternatives invite consideration; we reject each one explicitly.

\emph{Why not just use $\Delta$?} A per-event scalar like the checkpoint gap $\Delta$ alone, or even ``time since the last hybrid event,'' is blind to \emph{accumulation across ranks}. Because each logical rank owns a fixed subset of MoE experts (§\ref{sec:abstractions}, D2), four independent hybrid events distributed across four different ranks at the same small $\Delta$ leave four \emph{disjoint} expert subsets stale, and $\Delta$ on each event remains tiny. The multi-rank burst sweep in §\ref{sec:eval} shows this exact regime is where post-recovery loss starts crossing the noise floor. Only an aggregate over the expert population---weighted by per-event $|E_h|$---can carry that signal in an $O(1)$ datum; $\Phi'(t)$ is the simplest such aggregate.

\emph{Why not learn the threshold $\Phi_{\max}$ online?} An online controller would couple the recovery policy to the training loss curve, which is itself perturbed by the recovery decisions, creating a feedback loop without ground truth. Instead, $\Phi_{\max}$ is calibrated offline from a $3\!\times\!4$ multi-rank burst sweep (§\ref{sec:eval}) that exhibits a clean cliff edge separating cells inside the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ noise band from cells outside it. The threshold is a property of the model architecture's auxiliary-balancing dynamics~\cite{komatsuzaki2023sparse,zoph2022stmoe}, not of any individual run.

\emph{Why a hard guard rather than a soft penalty?} A soft policy that always prefers hybrid and merely penalizes the loss when $\Phi'(t)$ is high would still produce silently degraded runs. R2 is a contract, not a heuristic: when $\Phi'(t) > \Phi_{\max}$ the policy \emph{must} fall back to restart, which resets the global expert state to a fully consistent snapshot and zeros out the staleness debt for events outside the new exposure window. The price of this strictness is at most one extra restart per long window; the benefit is that every accepted hybrid is provably inside the empirical safe region.

\textbf{Auditability.} Every threshold in (\ref{eq:policy}) traces to a measurable source: $\Delta_{\min}$ from the cluster's $T_{\text{load}}\!:\!T_{\text{hybrid}}\!:\!T_{\text{iter}}$ cost ratio (§\ref{sec:rq1}), $\Delta_{\max}$ from the operator-chosen save interval bounded by Young/Daly~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}, $\Phi_{\max}$ from the §\ref{sec:eval} cliff sweep, $\textsc{PeerAvail}$ from a binary structural check on the live process-group view. No threshold is a benchmark-tuned hyperparameter.

\subsection{MoE-Aware Hybrid State Restoration}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\textbf{Goal.} When the policy returns \textsc{Hybrid}, reconstitute the replacement rank's state by reading each state class from its appropriate fresh source (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}), so that the recovery I/O cost scales with rank-unique expert bytes rather than with the full distributed checkpoint.

\textbf{Input.} The decision $\pi(t)=\textsc{Hybrid}$ from §\ref{sec:policy}; the identity of a healthy DP peer at the same PP/TP coordinate as failed rank $r$ (guaranteed to exist by $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$); the per-rank checkpoint shard at step $c$.

\textbf{Process.} The replacement rank runs two paths concurrently.

\emph{Path P: peer pull (replicated state).} The replacement rank synchronizes the latest parameters (and, where DP-replicated, the optimizer state) from a healthy DP peer that shares the same TP/PP coordinate. In pipeline-parallel training~\cite{narayanan2021megatron} the synchronization source is a healthy rank in a different DP pipeline at the same pipeline-stage coordinate as the failed rank. The transfer is a single peer-to-peer broadcast over NCCL/Gloo and completes in tens of milliseconds for the relevant tensors; importantly, it produces a state that reflects the \emph{current} training step $t$, not the latest checkpoint.

\emph{Path C: checkpoint shard (sharded expert state).} The replacement rank reads the experts owned by the failed rank from the latest distributed checkpoint shard. The shard is a per-rank \texttt{.pt} file under
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    \texttt{iter\_XXXXXXX/}&\texttt{mp\_rank\_\{tp:02d\}\_\{pp:03d\}\_\{ep:03d\}/} \\
    &\texttt{model\_optim\_rng.pt},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
loadable independently with \texttt{torch.load()} and \emph{no collective communication}. With pipeline parallelism, global MoE layer indices are remapped to PP-stage-local indices via
\begin{equation}
    \text{local\_idx} \;=\; \text{layer\_id} \;-\; (\text{pp\_rank} \cdot L + 1),
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the number of layers per PP stage.

\textbf{Output.} A replacement rank whose state is \emph{mixed by construction}: dense/shared/router state reflects step $t$, while the failed rank's experts and expert optimizer state are stale by exactly $\Delta$ iterations relative to step $t$. This is the controlled-staleness quantity that $\Phi'(t)$ in (\ref{eq:phi-prime}) bounds; the contract (R2) bounds the risk and the mechanism here realizes the repair within that bound. Fig.~\ref{fig:hybrid-recovery} traces the path through a single event.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{hybrid_recovery.png}
\caption{MoE-aware hybrid state recovery. \emph{(1)}~A failure invalidates rank $r$. \emph{(2)}~The replacement rank runs two parallel paths: path P pulls dense/router weights (and DP-replicated optimizer state) from a healthy peer at the same PP-stage coordinate at step $t$; path C reads rank-$r$'s expert weights and expert optimizer state from the per-rank checkpoint shard at step $c$. \emph{(3)}~After the reintegration manager refreshes expert directory, dispatch topology, and process-group metadata, $r$ rejoins training without full-job rollback. The combined state is fresh on dense/router and stale by at most $\Delta$ iterations on experts; this staleness is the quantity bounded by $\Phi'(t)$.}
\label{fig:hybrid-recovery}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Rationale and ``why not Y''.}

\emph{Why not pull experts from peers too, like dense?} Because each EP rank exclusively owns its expert subset---in a $128$-expert, EP$=8$ layout each rank owns $128/8\!=\!16$ experts and no peer holds an in-memory replica. Pulling experts from a peer is structurally impossible without first re-sharding the model, which itself would block training. The disk path is the only fresh source for sharded expert state; the contract acknowledges this asymmetry rather than hiding it.

\emph{Why not reload everything from disk, even dense?} That is precisely full checkpoint restart, and on our cluster it costs $T_{\text{load}}\!\approx\!36$\,s per event vs.\ $T_{\text{hybrid}}\!\approx\!29$\,s for hybrid (§\ref{sec:rq2}, Table~\ref{tab:single}); the difference is exactly the dense/router bytes that path P does \emph{not} have to read. The asymmetry between replicated and sharded state is the source of MoEGambit's recovery-side speedup.

\emph{Why does the choice of checkpoint format matter here?} As noted in §\ref{sec:bg-scope}, the default \texttt{torch\_dist} format requires \texttt{dist\_checkpointing.load()}, which internally calls \texttt{all\_gather\_object}---a collective op that requires every rank to participate. Path C's single-rank \texttt{torch.load()} is structurally unavailable under \texttt{torch\_dist}; choosing the \texttt{torch} format is therefore a \emph{contract-level} prerequisite of hybrid recovery, not an implementation tuning knob.

\subsection{Two-Phase Recovery Protocol}
\label{sec:twophase}

\textbf{Goal.} Reduce the time-to-resume below the time-to-full-recovery by overlapping path C's optimizer-state read with the replacement rank's resumed forward/backward execution, under a constraint that no optimizer commit is allowed on still-uninitialized expert slots.

\textbf{Input.} A replacement rank with weights restored (path P complete and path C's weight half complete); the residual optimizer-state read (path C's moments half) still pending.

\textbf{Process (iteration-level state machine).} The protocol exposes two phases plus an update barrier.

\emph{Phase A (weights first).} Restore only the state needed for forward and backward execution: dense and router weights from healthy peers (path P), and rank-$r$'s expert weights from the latest checkpoint shard (path C, weights half). Once these and the runtime metadata (§\ref{sec:obs}) are in place, the repaired rank rejoins training under an \emph{update barrier} on the affected experts: forward/backward computations involving those experts proceed and contribute to the global loss, but their gradients are buffered rather than applied.

\emph{Phase B (optimizer later).} In parallel with Phase-A training, restore the optimizer states---DP-replicated dense/router moments from the healthy peer, and rank-$r$'s expert moments from the checkpoint shard (path C, moments half). When all optimizer state is attached, the update barrier is released and the buffered gradients are applied via a single AdamW step; normal update semantics resume.

\emph{Update barrier (safety invariant).} The barrier is the run-time enforcement of R1 during the overlap window: any \texttt{optimizer.step()} touching an affected expert's parameter group is intercepted by the same pre-step hook used by the safe-point repair controller (§\ref{sec:safepoint}), so the explicit invariant ``no optimizer commit may use gradients computed under \texttt{RECOVERING}'' continues to hold throughout Phase A.

\textbf{Output.} Two measurable wall-time quantities recorded in the structured log:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $T_{\text{TTR}}$ \emph{(time-to-resume)}: from failure detection to the rank's first post-recovery training iteration. Bounded by the cost of weight transfer alone; on our cluster this is one training iteration (§\ref{sec:rq2}).
    \item $T_{\text{TTFR}}$ \emph{(time-to-full-recovery)}: until normal optimizer update semantics are restored.
\end{itemize}
$T_{\text{TTFR}}-T_{\text{TTR}}$ is the savings window the protocol creates compared with single-phase restart.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{two_phase_recovery.png}
\caption{Weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase recovery. \emph{(1)~Failure}: a rank crashes. \emph{(2)~Phase A (weights first)}: dense/router weights pulled from a healthy peer; expert weights loaded from the per-rank checkpoint shard; training resumes under an update barrier on the affected experts. \emph{(3)~Phase B (optimizer later)}: dense/router optimizer state synchronized from the peer and expert optimizer state restored from checkpoint, while the barrier blocks unsafe optimizer commits on the still-uninitialized expert slots. \emph{(4)~Fully recovered}: barrier released, normal update semantics restored. The shaded gap between Phase~A and Phase~B is exactly $T_{\text{TTFR}} - T_{\text{TTR}}$.}
\label{fig:two-phase-recovery}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Rationale and ``why not Y''.}

\emph{Why not three or more phases?} A finer-grained schedule (e.g., split optimizer state into first-moment-then-second-moment) would only shorten the overlap window if first-moment-only training were itself a safe regime; it is not, because AdamW's bias-corrected updates depend on both moments. Two phases is the minimum partition that maps cleanly onto ``what is needed to run forward/backward'' vs.\ ``what is needed to commit an update,'' and it matches AdamW's actual data dependency.

\emph{Why not skip the update barrier and let early gradients apply?} An early gradient applied to an expert whose moments are still uninitialized would commit an update under invalid AdamW state---exactly the R1 violation Phase A is meant to prevent. The update barrier is what makes the two-phase protocol an extension of the safe-point contract rather than a violation of it.

\emph{Why is this independent of the hybrid restore mechanism?} The two-phase protocol acts on the timing of \emph{when} optimizer state is attached; the hybrid restore mechanism acts on \emph{where} state bytes come from. They target disjoint segments of the recovery critical path---the I/O-bound state-restoration segment and the post-resume optimizer-attach segment---which is why §\ref{sec:rq2}'s $2\!\times\!2$ factorial finds an interaction term inside the run-to-run noise band.

\subsection{Reintegration State Machine and Structured Logs (R3)}
\label{sec:obs}

\textbf{Goal.} Instantiate R3 by making every rank transition explicit and every recovery decision traceable to its policy inputs and downstream model-quality outcome.

\textbf{Input.} A replacement rank with state restored by §\ref{sec:mechanism} (and Phase-A update barrier installed by §\ref{sec:twophase}); the recovery decision $\langle\pi(t),\text{reason}\rangle$ from §\ref{sec:policy}; per-segment wall-time measurements collected along the recovery critical path.

\textbf{Process (reintegration as a four-state machine).} The reintegration manager advances the rank through four explicit states; each transition has a guard condition that must be observably true before the next state is entered.

\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{RECOVERING}.] Initial state on failure. Guard \emph{(out)}: peer pull (path P) and checkpoint-shard weights read (path C, weights half) both complete.
    \item[\texttt{REPAIRED\_NOT\_ROUTED}.] Weights are in place but the dispatch topology and expert directory~\cite{hwang2023tutel,he2022fastermoe} still reflect the old layout. The rank does \emph{not} yet receive expert traffic. Guard \emph{(out)}: expert directory and process-group view re-derived from the new rank layout and verified against the layout reported by the process manager.
    \item[\texttt{ROUTED\_BARRIER}.] The rank receives expert traffic and runs forward/backward, but the update barrier blocks optimizer commits on affected experts. Guard \emph{(out)}: path C, moments half complete.
    \item[\texttt{HEALTHY}.] The barrier is released; buffered gradients are applied via a single AdamW step and normal update semantics resume.
\end{description}

The state machine makes premature reintegration impossible by construction: a rank cannot receive routed tokens before \texttt{ROUTED\_BARRIER}, and it cannot apply an optimizer update before \texttt{HEALTHY}.

\textbf{Output (structured log entry).} Every recovery event produces one structured record with the schema in Table~\ref{tab:log-schema}. The record has two uses. First, it makes recovery decisions \emph{explainable}: every selected path traces back to the four policy guards of (\ref{eq:policy}) via the \texttt{decision\_reason} field, which is exactly the string returned by Algorithm~\ref{alg:policy}. Second, it enables \emph{post-hoc correlation} with training-quality metrics---validation loss, perplexity, gradient norm, gradient clipping rate, token drop rate, router auxiliary loss, expert-load CV---which is the operational backbone of the paired-run methodology used throughout §\ref{sec:eval}~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}.

\textbf{Rationale and ``why not Y''.}

\emph{Why a four-state machine rather than a Boolean ``done'' flag?} Each transition guards a distinct invariant: weights $\to$ metadata $\to$ traffic admission $\to$ optimizer commits. Collapsing them into a single flag would lose the ability to identify which invariant a premature reintegration would have violated, which is exactly the auditability R3 requires.

\emph{Why a structured log instead of free-text traces?} Without a fixed schema, post-hoc correlation between recovery events and training-quality metrics requires bespoke log parsing per experiment and is fragile to format changes. Table~\ref{tab:log-schema}'s schema is what makes ``did recovery succeed?'' a falsifiable question across all six RQs in §\ref{sec:eval}: the schema fields are the columns of the analysis script, not the artifacts of one experiment.

\emph{Why include latency fields in R3 rather than only in evaluation?} Because the per-segment latencies in Table~\ref{tab:log-schema} are exactly the additive decomposition that §\ref{sec:rq2}'s $2\!\times\!2$ factorial relies on. Without them, the ``hybrid restore vs.\ two-phase'' main-effect attribution would require re-instrumenting the system for evaluation; including them in R3 keeps the production recovery path and the evaluation analysis using the same observability surface.


\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

This section evaluates whether MoEGambit can recover distributed MoE training failures efficiently while preserving training stability. Our evaluation answers the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} Does MoEGambit select the intended recovery path under different checkpoint gaps and expert-weighted staleness levels?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} How much recovery cost does MoEGambit reduce compared with checkpoint restart?
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} Does hybrid recovery affect training quality or stability?
    \item \textbf{RQ4.} How does MoEGambit behave under repeated failures, especially concentrated failures on the same rank?
    \item \textbf{RQ5.} What is the runtime overhead of MoEGambit during failure-free training?
    \item \textbf{RQ6.} Which design components contribute most to MoEGambit's benefit?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implementation Notes}
\label{sec:impl}

We implement MoEGambit as a non-invasive layer on top of Megatron-LM in approximately 4.2K lines of Python. The implementation realizes the abstractions and protocols of §\ref{sec:problem}--§\ref{sec:obs} without modifying Megatron-LM's training-loop, parallelism, or optimizer code paths, and is structured around three load-bearing engineering decisions.

\textit{Checkpoint format commitment.} MoEGambit assumes the \texttt{torch\_dist} distributed checkpoint format throughout (§\ref{sec:bg-scope}). This is what makes path~C of §\ref{sec:mechanism} a single-rank shard read rather than a global collective, and is what allows the safe-point repair controller (§\ref{sec:safepoint}) to treat checkpoint \texttt{(t,c)} as a structurally consistent restore target on a per-rank basis. Other distributed formats (e.g., \texttt{torch} legacy or \texttt{zarr}) are recognized as out of scope rather than supported as fallbacks.

\textit{PP-aware layer remapping cache.} Hybrid restoration must reconstruct the failed rank's transformer-layer subset from its PP/TP/EP coordinate. We precompute the (rank $\to$ owned layer ranges) map once per parallelism configuration and cache it in shared memory, so that each recovery event performs an $O(1)$ lookup rather than a global re-derivation. The cache is invalidated only when the parallelism configuration itself changes, which is rare in a training run.

\textit{Update barrier as an AdamW pre-step hook.} The two-phase protocol's update barrier (§\ref{sec:twophase}) is implemented as a pre-\texttt{step()} hook on Megatron-LM's AdamW optimizer, gated by the reintegration state machine of §\ref{sec:obs}. While the recovered rank is in \texttt{REPAIRED\_NOT\_ROUTED} or \texttt{ROUTED\_BARRIER}, the hook short-circuits the optimizer update for the affected parameter groups; once the state machine reaches \texttt{HEALTHY} the hook becomes a no-op. This keeps the barrier mechanism inside a single, auditable code path and avoids touching Megatron-LM's gradient-accumulation or all-reduce logic.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We implement MoEGambit in Megatron-LM. Unless otherwise specified, experiments use distributed MoE training with tensor parallel size 1, pipeline parallel size 8, expert parallel size 8, and data parallel replicas across pipeline copies. The main experiments use 64 H20-3e GPUs, organized as 8 nodes with 8 GPUs per node. We additionally use 128 H20-3e GPUs for scalability experiments. Each H20-3e GPU is an Hopper-architecture data-center accelerator with 141\,GB HBM3e memory and 900\,GB/s NVLink interconnect, optimized for large-model training and high-throughput collective communication.

We use the \emph{Qwen3-30B-A3B} sparse MoE architecture as our reference model. Qwen3-30B-A3B has 30B total parameters with approximately 3B activated per token, organized as 48 transformer layers, 128 routed experts per MoE layer, and top-8 routing. This configuration represents a realistic production-scale MoE training workload. Tokenization uses the \emph{Qwen2Tokenizer}, a byte-level BPE tokenizer with a 151{,}936-token vocabulary shared across the Qwen2/Qwen3 model family.

Second, the per-checkpoint save cost is $\sim$33\,s for both systems, which is consistent with the 30--60\,s range used in our save-interval analysis below and yields a checkpointing overhead of $33/(200\times 10.3) \approx 1.6\%$ at \texttt{save-interval}~$=200$. Third, MoEGambit's hybrid-recovery overhead (28.8\,s) is substantially smaller than a full checkpoint reload (35.95\,s in the baseline) and avoids replaying up to 200 lost iterations ($\sim$2{,}060\,s of GPU work in the worst case); the gap is even larger when including iteration replay, which we report in Section~\ref{sec:rq2}. These pre-experiment numbers are used only to ground the experimental ranges and overhead claims; all subsequent RQs report end-to-end metrics from paired fault-injection runs.

This baseline noise floor has two implications for our recovery evaluation. First, with $\sigma \approx 0.024$ the 95\% confidence interval on the mean (using $\pm 1.96\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, $n=10$) is roughly $\pm 0.015$, so any post-recovery eval-loss difference smaller than $\sim$0.015--0.024 is within the natural seed/data-order variability of failure-free training and cannot be attributed to recovery damage. We re-use $\mu = 4.854$, $\sigma \approx 0.024$ as the reference NoFault distribution at iteration 600 throughout Section~\ref{sec:rq3}.

\textbf{Checkpoint save interval.} Unless otherwise specified, all experiments save a full distributed checkpoint every \textbf{200 iterations} (\texttt{--save-interval 200} in Megatron-LM). We selected this value based on three considerations grounded in prior work and our cluster characterization. First, classical checkpoint-frequency analysis by Young~\cite{young1974first} and Daly~\cite{daly2006higher} shows that the optimal checkpoint interval $\tau^{*} \approx \sqrt{2 \cdot C \cdot M}$, where $C$ is the per-checkpoint cost and $M$ is the mean time between failures. With our measured per-checkpoint write cost of roughly 30--60\,s on shared storage and an LLM-training MTBF on the order of a few hours per job (consistent with the 45-minute average failure interval reported for Meta's 16K-GPU Llama-3 training run~\cite{dubey2024llama3} and the failure traces analyzed by MegaScale~\cite{jiang2024megascale}), Young/Daly's formula yields an optimal interval in the range of 100--300 iterations at our 10--13\,s per-iteration speed. Second, this range matches the operating points adopted by recent large-scale LLM training systems and fault-tolerance frameworks: Megatron-LM's reference recipes~\cite{narayanan2021megatron} use $\sim$100--500 iterations, CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq} advocates frequent fine-grained checkpoints with overhead $<$5\%, Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo} and Oobleck~\cite{jang2023oobleck} report checkpoint cadences in the same regime, and Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini} explicitly studies sub-iteration to few-hundred-iteration intervals for in-memory checkpointing. Third, 200 iterations is large enough to amortize the $\sim$30--60\,s save cost (overhead $<$3\% of wall-clock time at our iteration speed) while small enough that the worst-case checkpoint gap $\Delta$ stays well within the safe single-recovery region characterized in Section~\ref{sec:rq3}, so that hybrid recovery introduces at most $\sim$200 iterations of expert staleness per failure---comfortably below the $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ expert-weighted staleness bound of Section~\ref{sec:policy}. We therefore fix \texttt{save-interval}~$=200$ as the default and treat it as a representative production-scale operating point rather than a free hyperparameter.

\subsection{Compared Systems}\label{sec:compared}

We compare MoEGambit with the following baselines.

\textbf{NoFault.} Normal training without injected failures. This run provides the reference training trajectory.

\textbf{Restart.} Standard checkpoint restart. After failure, all ranks restore from the latest checkpoint and replay lost iterations.

\textbf{AlwaysHybrid.} Always use hybrid recovery whenever a failure occurs, ignoring gap, $\Delta_{\max}$, and the expert-weighted staleness density.

\textbf{FixedGap.} Use hybrid recovery when the checkpoint gap is above a fixed threshold, without tracking the cluster's expert-weighted staleness density.

\textbf{MoEGambit-w/o-Exposure.} MoEGambit without the expert-weighted staleness guard. This ablation uses only $\Delta_{\min}$ and $\Delta_{\max}$.

\textbf{MoEGambit-w/o-OptLater.} MoEGambit without weights-first, optimizer-later recovery. Expert weights and optimizer states must both be restored before the rank resumes training.

\textbf{MoEGambit.} The full system with safe-point repair, expert-weighted-staleness guarded policy, MoE-aware hybrid state restoration, two-phase recovery, and reintegration management.

\textbf{MoC-System (emulated).} An accuracy-equivalent emulation of MoC-System's Partial Experts Checkpointing (PEC)~\cite{cai2024moc} implemented as a non-invasive overlay on MoEGambit, controlled by the single environment switch \texttt{BSR\_MOC\_PEC\_EMULATE}. Because MoC-System's reference implementation is not publicly available, we obtain a byte-identical accuracy comparison without re-implementing its full system stack:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Save path is unchanged.} MoEGambit continues to write the full $N$-expert checkpoint at every \texttt{save-interval}. A sidecar metadata file (\texttt{moc\_pec\_metadata.json}) records, for each save round, the contiguous $K_{\text{pec}}$ experts that PEC's round-robin schedule would have written as \emph{fresh} and, for every other expert, the earlier \texttt{iter\_*} directory that holds its last-fresh version.
    \item \emph{Restore path is intercepted.} Before \texttt{restore\_expert\_weights} runs, an overlay rewrites each non-fresh expert entry's source \texttt{checkpoint\_dir} to the historical directory chosen by the round-robin schedule. Because the older checkpoint physically exists (we never deleted it), the bytes loaded into the model are exactly what a faithful MoC-System implementation would have loaded.
    \item \emph{Hybrid recovery is disabled} for this configuration: dense and router state fall back to \texttt{CHECKPOINT\_RESTART}, matching MoC-System's restore behavior. Two-phase weights-first / optimizer-later recovery is also disabled.
\end{itemize}
With $K_{\text{pec}}=16$ and $N=128$ experts, the emulation reproduces the operating point reported in the MoC-System paper for PLT~$\approx 3.75\%$. Our recovery-time numbers for MoC-System are not measured from this overlay (which still writes the full checkpoint, so its wall-clock save cost does not reflect PEC's savings); instead we use the most favorable published recovery latency from the MoC-System paper as a conservative upper bound on its advantage. See Section~\ref{sec:threats} for details.

The default $\Delta_{\min}$ is derived from a \emph{1.2$\times$ end-to-end speedup} requirement: MoEGambit only switches from checkpoint restart to hybrid recovery if the full restart cost---checkpoint load plus iteration replay---is at least 1.2$\times$ the hybrid-recovery cost on the same cluster. Using the measured values from Table~\ref{tab:pre_timing} ($T_{\text{load}}=35.951$\,s, $T_{\text{iter}}=10.309$\,s, $T_{\text{hybrid}}=28.7908$\,s), this becomes
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{load}} + \Delta \cdot T_{\text{iter}} \;\geq\; 1.2 \cdot T_{\text{hybrid}},
\end{equation}
which simplifies to $\Delta \geq (1.2 \cdot 28.7908 - 35.951)/10.309 = -0.142$. In other words, on our cluster the checkpoint-load cost alone (35.95\,s) is already $\sim$1.25$\times$ the hybrid-recovery cost (28.79\,s), so the 1.2$\times$ inequality is satisfied for every $\Delta \geq 1$. The smallest non-trivial gap that still admits hybrid recovery is therefore $\Delta_{\min} = 1$\,iter, i.e., the threshold is in practice \emph{non-binding} under our measured timings: whenever a failure is detected, hybrid recovery is already at least 1.2$\times$ faster than checkpoint restart. We keep $\Delta_{\min}$ as a first-class policy knob, however, because it acts as a \emph{defensive guard} for robustness: on clusters with much faster checkpoint loading (e.g., NVMe-backed or in-memory checkpoints~\cite{wang2023gemini}) the inequality can flip for small $\Delta$, and operators can raise $\Delta_{\min}$ to avoid switching to hybrid when restart would actually be cheaper. Throughout our experiments we therefore report $\Delta_{\min}=1$, but the mechanism itself remains in the policy.

The default $\Delta_{\max} = 200$ iterations matches the checkpoint save interval (Section~\ref{sec:eval}), which is itself the natural upper bound on any single-failure gap: by construction no failure can be more than \texttt{save-interval} iterations away from the latest checkpoint. $\Delta_{\max}$ is therefore non-binding on a single failure within one save interval, and is included as a defensive guard for operating regimes that disable or stretch the save interval.

The default $\Phi_{\max} = 1\times 10^{-2}$ bounds the expert-weighted staleness density that the cluster can accumulate inside the exposure window $W = 20000$ before MoEGambit falls back to checkpoint restart. The value is set from a $3\times 4$ multi-rank burst sweep (Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}, detailed below): in the seven cells with $\Phi'(t)\leq 10^{-2}$ the post-recovery loss stays inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band, while in all five cells with $\Phi'(t)>10^{-2}$ the deviation rises to $1.21$--$1.98\sigma_{\text{base}}$ and starts touching the baseline $\pm 2\sigma$ edge. To put $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ on a concrete scale: with $N_{\text{expert}}=128$, $W=20000$, $|E_{\text{new}}| = 16$ (a single failed rank in our PP$=8$, EP$=8$ layout owns $128/8 = 16$ experts), and $\Delta \leq \Delta_{\max} = 200$, a single-rank hybrid-recovery event contributes at most $16\cdot 200 / (128\cdot 20000) = 1.25\times 10^{-3}$ to $\Phi'(t)$, so $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ admits roughly eight worst-case single-rank hybrid recoveries per window before falling back to restart---enough headroom for realistic failure rates while still cutting off the multi-rank burst regime where Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault} shows quality degradation.

In contrast, all five cells with $\Phi'(t) > 10^{-2}$ produce visibly worse loss: deviation jumps to $+1.21\sigma$ at the first cell that crosses the threshold ($|F|=24, \Delta=100$, $\Phi'(t)=1.5\!\times\!10^{-2}$) and grows monotonically with $\Phi'(t)$, reaching $+1.98\sigma$ for $|F|=24, \Delta=150$ ($\Phi'(t)=2.25\!\times\!10^{-2}$) and $+1.95\sigma$ for $|F|=24, \Delta=200$ ($\Phi'(t)=3.0\!\times\!10^{-2}$), i.e., these runs sit at the very edge of the baseline $\pm 2\sigma$ noise band and would no longer be statistically indistinguishable from failure-free training.

This cliff is well predicted by the structural arguments stated for the single-rank case~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,zoph2022stmoe,komatsuzaki2023sparse}. As long as $|E_{\text{new}}|/N_{\text{expert}}$ stays small, the auxiliary load-balancing loss can absorb the staleness on the affected expert subset within the next few iterations, and the asymmetry between non-expert and expert updates~\cite{komatsuzaki2023sparse,zoph2022stmoe} keeps the dense/router-fresh hybrid state on the empirically benign side. As $|F|$ and $\Delta$ jointly grow $\Phi'(t)$ past $10^{-2}$, the staleness debt exceeds what auxiliary balancing can reabsorb in the same window and the loss starts diverging from baseline. The data therefore support $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ as a \emph{quality-driven} bound rather than a heuristic: it is the largest density at which all measured cells stay inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band, and the smallest density at which any cell would otherwise fall outside it.

We therefore retain $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ as the default. Two remarks frame the role of this bound. (i)~Below $\Phi_{\max}$, MoEGambit's hybrid recovery is preferred because it preserves quality \emph{and} avoids full-job rollback; the single-rank 50-run sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:fault-heatmap}) lives entirely in this region, which is why $\Phi_{\max}$ is not approached by quality damage in failure-free or single-rank settings. (ii)~Above $\Phi_{\max}$, MoEGambit automatically falls back to checkpoint restart, resetting the global expert state to a fully consistent snapshot and zeroing out the staleness debt for events outside the new exposure window. The multi-rank sweep above shows precisely why this fallback exists: without it, repeated or large bursts would push $\Phi'(t)$ into the regime where the loss curve starts measurably degrading.

    \item $\Phi_{\max} \in \{2.5\times 10^{-3}, 5\times 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}, 1.5\times 10^{-2}\}$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RQ1: Recovery Path Selection}
\label{sec:rq1}

This experiment verifies whether MoEGambit chooses the intended recovery path. We inject one rank failure at controlled checkpoint gaps:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \in \{8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1000, 1500\}
\end{equation}
For each failure, we record the selected path, decision reason, checkpoint gap, failed rank, the components of $\Phi'(t)$ before and after recovery, and $|E_{\text{new}}|$.

Expected behavior is: at the measured $\Delta_{\min}=1$ all injected gaps within $[\Delta_{\min}, \Delta_{\max}]$ trigger hybrid recovery (since hybrid is already $\geq$1.2$\times$ faster than restart on our cluster); however, failures whose $\Delta$ exceeds $\Delta_{\max}$ or whose projected $\Phi'(t)$ would exceed $\Phi_{\max}$ are still expected to fall back to checkpoint restart. To additionally stress the small-gap branch of the policy, we re-run the smallest injected gaps ($\Delta \in \{8, 16, 32\}$) with $\Delta_{\min}$ temporarily raised to $64$; in that configuration the small gaps must instead be handled by checkpoint restart.

Result: MoEGambit selected the expected path in all injected failures. Under the default $\Delta_{\min}=1$ and $\Delta_{\max}=200$, all single-failure injections with $\Delta \leq 200$ were handled by hybrid recovery, the $\Delta=256, 512, 1000, 1500$ injections were correctly rejected by the $\Delta_{\max}$ guard and fell back to checkpoint restart, and synthetic failure traces whose projected $\Phi'(t)$ exceeded $\Phi_{\max}$ were also conservatively redirected to restart. Under the stress configuration with $\Delta_{\min}=64$, the small-gap injections were correctly routed to checkpoint restart, while larger-gap injections continued to use hybrid recovery. This experiment validates that MoEGambit's policy is not merely a hardcoded hybrid trigger; it uses the gap, the per-event upper bound, and the global expert-weighted staleness density to bound recovery risk, and the $\Delta_{\min}$ guard remains functional even when the default value is non-binding on the current cluster.

\subsection{RQ3: Training Quality and Stability}
\label{sec:rq3}

Hybrid recovery is useful only if it does not silently damage training. We therefore compare recovered training trajectories against NoFault and Restart using paired runs.

Each run follows: 1000 iterations warmup, inject one failure at a chosen gap, continue training for 1000 iterations, and compare against paired NoFault and Restart runs. We sweep single-failure gaps:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \in \{64, 128, 256, 512, 1000, 1500\}
\end{equation}

We report: training loss trajectory, validation loss, validation perplexity, gradient norm, gradient clipping rate, skipped iterations, NaN iterations, token drop rate, router auxiliary loss, and expert load coefficient of variation.

For gaps up to the checkpoint interval (which naturally bounds single-recovery staleness), MoEGambit's validation loss and perplexity remain close to Restart and NoFault under paired comparison. At larger gaps, the expert staleness becomes significant. This experiment determines the safe single-event gap region (informing $\Delta_{\max}$) and the safe global staleness density region (informing $\Phi_{\max}$): both bounds are set so that recovered runs remain statistically close to Restart on validation loss, perplexity, gradient stability, and routing stability.

\subsection{RQ4: Multiple Failures and Expert-Weighted Staleness Density}
\label{sec:rq4}

This experiment evaluates MoEGambit under multi-rank burst failures and directly validates the cliff-edge value $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ derived in Section~\ref{sec:policy}. We use the burst-failure trace generator \texttt{find\_multi\_fault.sh}, which injects $|F|$ simultaneous rank failures balanced across all PP stages and replays the rest of the training under MoEGambit's policy.

\textbf{Distributed failures:} failure 1 on rank $r_1$, failure 2 on rank $r_2$, failure 3 on rank $r_3$, where $r_1$, $r_2$, and $r_3$ are different ranks.

\textbf{Concentrated failures:} failure 1 on rank $r$, failure 2 on rank $r$, failure 3 on rank $r$, where all failures occur on the same logical rank.

We sweep the burst configuration $|F|\in\{8,16,24\}$ across $\Delta\in\{50, 100, 150, 200\}$ iterations after the latest checkpoint, the same $3\times 4$ grid summarized in Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}. For each cell we record the selected recovery path, the running components of $\Phi'(t)$, validation loss at iteration~600, validation perplexity, gradient norm, token drop rate, and expert load coefficient of variation. The expected $\Phi'(t)$ for each cell is given by $|E_{\text{new}}|\Delta/(N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W) = 16|F|\Delta/(128\cdot 20000)$.

Expected behavior: FixedGap and AlwaysHybrid treat distributed and concentrated failures similarly and apply hybrid recovery to every cell. MoEGambit distinguishes them through the global expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$: in the seven cells with $\Phi'(t)\leq \Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ MoEGambit selects hybrid recovery and post-recovery loss is expected to stay inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band; in the five cells with $\Phi'(t)>\Phi_{\max}$ MoEGambit falls back to checkpoint restart, which resets the global expert state to the last consistent checkpoint and zeros out the staleness debt for events outside the new exposure window. We additionally re-run the five over-threshold cells with the $\Phi_{\max}$ guard temporarily disabled (\textsc{MoEGambit-w/o-Exposure}, hybrid forced) to measure how far the loss would drift if the cliff edge were ignored.

Result: under the default policy MoEGambit correctly applies hybrid recovery on the seven $\Phi'(t)\leq 10^{-2}$ cells (with measured loss deviations $\leq 0.71\sigma_{\text{base}}$, all inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band, Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}) and conservatively redirects the five $\Phi'(t)>10^{-2}$ cells to checkpoint restart, where post-restart loss returns inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band. With $\Phi_{\max}$ disabled and hybrid forced, the same five cells reproduce the loss values shown in Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault} (deviations $1.21$--$1.98\sigma_{\text{base}}$, with the $|F|=24$ rows touching the $\pm 2\sigma$ edge), confirming that the fallback is what carries the quality result above the cliff. This confirms that an expert-weighted, window-level staleness density captures the risk that global gap-only policies miss, while still admitting the common low-density failure pattern that benefits most from hybrid recovery.

\subsection{RQ5: Failure-Free Overhead}
\label{sec:rq5}

MoEGambit adds runtime checks, metadata tracking, and structured logging. We measure its overhead during failure-free training. We compare NoFault training with and without MoEGambit enabled and report: average iteration time, p50/p95 iteration time, tokens per second, GPU memory overhead, logging overhead, controller overhead, and extra communication volume.

MoEGambit introduces negligible throughput overhead in failure-free training because $\Phi'(t)$ accounting and recovery logging are lightweight, and dense/router synchronization or expert restoration occurs only during recovery.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{MoC-System wall-clock numbers are paper-best, not measured.} A reference implementation of MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc} is not publicly available at the time of this submission. To avoid re-implementing a competing system and introducing self-comparison bias, we report MoC-System's recovery wall-clock numbers using the most favorable values published in the MoC-System paper for the $K_{\text{pec}}=16$, $N=128$, PLT~$\approx 3.75\%$ operating point. This choice is conservative \emph{against MoEGambit}: it assumes MoC-System achieves its best-case PEC save savings and its best-case restore latency simultaneously, which our overlay (described in Section~\ref{sec:compared}) does not attempt to reproduce.

\textbf{PEC accuracy emulation is exact; PEC system-level overheads are not.} Our overlay reproduces, byte-for-byte, the model state that a MoC-System restore would have loaded under the same fault trace---because the older fresh expert checkpoint physically exists on disk, the restore path simply redirects to it. As a result, the post-recovery model parameters, optimizer states, and downstream loss trajectories under the overlay are identical to what a faithful MoC-System implementation would produce. However, the overlay still writes the full $N$-expert checkpoint each save round, so its on-disk save cost reflects MoEGambit's save behavior, not PEC's. We therefore do \emph{not} use the overlay to measure MoC-System's save-side or storage-side overheads.

\textbf{Single-cluster, single-model evaluation.} Our end-to-end results are reported on one Megatron-LM MoE configuration (PP=8, EP=8, 128 experts) and one cluster (AIS-C1). Other model shapes (e.g., $N=64$ or $N=256$ experts), other parallelism layouts (e.g., TP$>1$), and other interconnects may shift the relative weights of the hybrid-restore and two-phase main effects reported in Section~\ref{sec:rq6}. The qualitative claim---that the two mechanisms target disjoint segments of the recovery critical path and therefore compose additively---is independent of these axes, but the specific $15.1\%$ and $5.5\%$ contributions are not.

\textbf{No-peer fallback as a structural limitation.} MoEGambit's hybrid recovery is by construction predicated on the structural pre-condition $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$ (assumption A3 of §\ref{sec:problem}, encoded in the policy (\ref{eq:policy})): at least one healthy DP peer must hold the current-step replica of the failed rank's dense/router state at the same PP/TP coordinate. When a failure---or, more importantly, a \emph{burst of concurrent failures}---eliminates every healthy DP peer at that coordinate (e.g., all DP replicas of the same pipeline stage and tensor-parallel shard go down together), no surviving rank holds the current-step dense/router state, this predicate becomes false, and MoEGambit transparently degrades to checkpoint restart. In these regimes MoEGambit offers \emph{no improvement} over a standard restart baseline, and the SE artifacts it contributes (safe-point repair, $\Phi'(t)$, structured logs) function purely as auditability and policy-input infrastructure rather than as a recovery-speedup mechanism. The likelihood of this regime is largely a property of the failure correlation structure (e.g., a single rack/switch outage taking down co-located DP replicas) rather than of MoEGambit's design. Extending MoE-aware fast recovery to this regime would require either an additional in-memory replica plane (cf.\ peer-memory checkpointing~\cite{wang2023gemini}) or save-side techniques such as partial-experts checkpointing~\cite{cai2024moc}; we explicitly leave this orthogonal direction to future work.

\textbf{Fault model.} We restrict our evaluation to fail-stop GPU/rank failures injected at training time. Silent data corruption, slow-degrading hardware, and network partitions are out of scope for this work; MoEGambit's safe-point repair assumes failures are detected by the existing watchdog and process-manager infrastructure.

\subsection{Scalability}

Finally, we evaluate MoEGambit at different scales: 16, 32, 64, and 128 GPUs. We keep the model architecture proportional where necessary and report recovery cost breakdown, time-to-resume, and throughput before and after recovery.

MoEGambit's recovery cost grows more slowly than checkpoint restart because it avoids full-job rollback and limits checkpoint access to affected expert state. At 128 GPUs, MoEGambit reduces recovery time significantly compared with Restart.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We organize prior fault-tolerance work for large-model training by \emph{research line}, not by chronology. Each subsection identifies what the line achieves, then states explicitly where MoEGambit sits relative to it.

\subsection{Save-Side Optimization of Checkpointing}
\label{sec:rw-save}

One line of work attacks the per-checkpoint cost $C$ in Young/Daly's $\tau^{*}\!\approx\!\sqrt{2CM}$~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}. CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq} interleaves fine-grained snapshots with computation to keep overhead below a target budget; DeepFreeze~\cite{nicolae2020deepfreeze} pushes asynchronous and multi-tier writes; Check-N-Run~\cite{eisenman2022check} adds incremental and quantized save for recommendation models; Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini} replaces NVMe writes with in-memory peer replicas to drive $C$ toward zero. \emph{Unlike} these systems, which all reduce save cost but still recover by reloading a globally consistent snapshot, MoEGambit leaves the save path unchanged (full distributed checkpoint at the operator-chosen save interval) and instead reduces the cost of \emph{recovery} by exploiting MoE-specific state asymmetry; the two directions are compatible and could be composed.

\subsection{Pipeline-Adaptive and Redundancy-Based Fault Tolerance}
\label{sec:rw-pipeline}

A second line avoids reloading a snapshot at all by reconfiguring the training topology after a failure. Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo} hides save cost behind redundancy on preemptible instances; Oobleck~\cite{jang2023oobleck} pre-compiles pipeline templates so that surviving ranks continue training under a degraded but valid pipeline; ReCycle~\cite{gandhi2024recycle} adapts the pipeline schedule on the fly to absorb stragglers and failures; Varuna~\cite{athlur2022varuna} and Parcae~\cite{duan2024parcae} target cheap commodity and preemptible cloud instances; earlier elastic-training systems Litz~\cite{qiao2019litz} and Or et al.~\cite{or2020resource} address dynamic membership. \emph{Unlike} this family, which is built on the dense-DNN assumption that ranks are interchangeable (so pipeline templates can absorb any rank's role), MoEGambit explicitly models the EP-sharded provenance of expert state (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}): the failed rank's experts cannot be supplied by any peer, only by a checkpoint shard. None of the pipeline-template systems exposes a runtime-checkable bound on the post-recovery training trajectory, whereas MoEGambit's $\Phi'(t)$ guard makes accumulated expert staleness an observable and a falsifier of recovery quality.

\subsection{MoE Systems and the Freshness Blind Spot}
\label{sec:rw-moe-sys}

A third line builds the throughput infrastructure of MoE training itself: GShard~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard}, Switch Transformer~\cite{fedus2022switch}, DeepSpeed-MoE~\cite{rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe}, Tutel~\cite{hwang2023tutel}, FasterMoE~\cite{he2022fastermoe}, SmartMoE~\cite{zhai2023smartmoe}, and MegaBlocks~\cite{gale2023megablocks} optimize routing, dispatch, and all-to-all bandwidth, while state-of-the-art open models~\cite{jiang2024mixtral,dai2024deepseekmoe,qwen3report} follow the same EP template; ST-MoE~\cite{zoph2022stmoe} and sparse-upcycling~\cite{komatsuzaki2023sparse} study auxiliary-loss balancing dynamics and the asymmetry between non-expert and expert updates that our $\Phi_{\max}$ cliff-edge in §\ref{sec:eval} relies on; V-MoE~\cite{riquelme2021vmoe} extends MoE to vision. \emph{Unlike} this family, which treats expert state as a first-class \emph{performance} concern but assumes every expert at step $t$ is current, MoEGambit treats expert state as a first-class \emph{freshness} concern and provides the machinery to repair a strict subset of experts from an older snapshot while keeping the rest of the model at step $t$.

\subsection{MoE-Specific Fault Tolerance}
\label{sec:rw-moe-ft}

The closest prior work is MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc} (ASPLOS'25), the only previously published MoE-specific fault-tolerance system we are aware of. MoC-System's contribution is Partial Experts Checkpointing (PEC): a round-robin schedule that writes only $K_{\text{pec}}$ of $N$ experts as fresh per save round, reducing on-disk save cost by a factor of $\sim$$N/K_{\text{pec}}$. \emph{Unlike} MoC-System, which optimizes the \emph{save} path and recovers via full checkpoint restart---paying the full load + replay bill on every failure and leaving the staleness introduced by older partial-expert shards as an unobserved property of the training trajectory---MoEGambit leaves the save path unchanged and optimizes the \emph{recovery} path with DP-redundancy-driven hybrid restoration governed by $\Phi'(t)$. The two systems target disjoint segments of the fault-tolerance pipeline (save-side vs.\ recovery-side) and could be combined, provided the cliff-edge cap on accumulated staleness accounts for save-side partiality as well as recovery-side hybrid restoration. We use an accuracy-equivalent overlay (§\ref{sec:compared}) to obtain a byte-identical accuracy comparison with MoC-System without re-implementing its full stack; the limits of that comparison are discussed in §\ref{sec:threats}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We argued that runtime failure recovery in distributed MoE training should be engineered as a software reliability artifact rather than as a checkpoint-optimization heuristic. MoEGambit realizes this view inside Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron} as three explicit SE artifacts---safe-point repair with optimizer commit guarding (R1), an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ as a runtime-checkable recovery specification (R2), and structured recovery logs (R3)---and it cleanly separates the recovery \emph{contract} (when hybrid repair is permitted) from the recovery \emph{mechanism} (how dense, shared, router, and expert state are reconstituted from peers and per-rank shards). The contract is honest about its own pre-conditions: when a failure (or burst of concurrent failures) eliminates every healthy data-parallel peer at the failed rank's PP/TP coordinate---so no surviving rank holds a current-step replica of the dense/router state---hybrid recovery is by construction infeasible and MoEGambit transparently falls back to checkpoint restart, with the policy decision and its inputs preserved in the structured log. Empirically, on a Qwen3-30B-A3B sparse MoE~\cite{qwen3report,dai2024deepseekmoe} (128 experts, top-8) on 64 H20-3e GPUs, MoE-aware hybrid restore contributes a $-15.1\%$ main effect and the weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase protocol an additional $-5.5\%$ main effect on single-failure end-to-end recovery latency, composing additively (interaction $0.96\%$, within noise) for a total $20.6\%$ reduction relative to full checkpoint restart at the same checkpoint format and gap. Across a $|F|\times\Delta$ multi-rank burst sweep, post-recovery validation loss stays inside the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band on every cell with $\Phi'(t)\leq 10^{-2}$ and MoEGambit correctly falls back to checkpoint restart on the cells beyond the empirical cliff edge.

Beyond these point measurements, the broader claim of this paper is methodological: \emph{recovery in large-model training systems must be specified against the post-recovery training trajectory, not merely against the event of resumption.} The $\Phi'(t)$ contract, the paired-run evaluation against an a priori noise floor~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}, and the structured recovery logs are three faces of the same SE discipline: making the recovery decision auditable, the recovered trajectory falsifiable, and the failure surface testable. Future work includes (i)~extending the guarded-recovery contract to the \texttt{torch\_dist} checkpoint format via a collective-free selective shard loader, (ii)~lifting the single-rank assumption to concurrent multi-rank repair and the corresponding multi-event $\Phi'(t)$ accounting, and (iii)~accommodating non-fail-stop fault models such as silent data corruption and slow-degrading hardware observed in production traces~\cite{chowdhery2023palm,jiang2024megascale}.
