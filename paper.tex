\begin{abstract}
Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models now power frontier large language model training, where multi-week, multi-thousand-GPU jobs make GPU failures routine. However, production recovery still relies on coarse-grained checkpoint restart that reloads global state and replays hundreds of iterations on every failure, and the only existing MoE-aware system, MoC-System, optimizes save-side cost while leaving recovery itself unchanged. We present \textbf{MoEGambit}, a recovery-side fault-tolerance framework integrated into Megatron-LM that exploits a structural asymmetry of MoE training: dense parameters and routers are data-parallel-replicated, so a failed rank can synchronize them from a healthy peer at memory-to-memory speed and only its rank-local experts need to load from disk. A safe-point repair protocol prevents partial optimizer commits, and an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ guards accumulated quality risk over repeated hybrid recoveries. On Qwen3-30B-A3B with 64 H20 GPUs, MoEGambit achieves a $36.9\times$ end-to-end recovery speedup over MoC-System (whose restart-style recovery still pays the $\sim$100-iteration replay cost) and a $1.26\times$ reduction in raw recovery latency, while keeping validation loss, perplexity, and expert load balance within the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become a dominant architecture for frontier large language models, powering systems such as Mixtral~\cite{jiang2024mixtral}, DeepSeek-MoE~\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe}, and Qwen3-MoE~\cite{qwen3report}, because they decouple model capacity from per-token compute by routing each input through only a small subset of experts. Training these models at scale, however, is a long-running, multi-week, multi-thousand-GPU software process in which failures are routine rather than exceptional. Meta's Llama-3 training trace reports a failure roughly every 45\,minutes across a 16K-GPU cluster~\cite{dubey2024llama3}; MegaScale documents hundreds of distinct failure modes during a 10K-GPU production deployment~\cite{jiang2024megascale}; the OPT-175B logbook records weeks of human-in-the-loop intervention~\cite{zhang2022opt}. Because the dominant recovery mechanism in production is \emph{checkpoint restart}---reload the most recent globally consistent snapshot and replay the lost iterations---each failure event on a large MoE job typically consumes hundreds of seconds of checkpoint I/O plus hundreds of replayed iterations, costing thousands of GPU-seconds per event and, cumulatively, days of wall time over a training run.

A substantial body of fault-tolerant training systems has been developed to reduce this cost, but almost all of it targets dense models. CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq}, DeepFreeze~\cite{nicolae2020deepfreeze}, and Check-N-Run~\cite{eisenman2022check} shrink per-checkpoint cost via asynchronous and incremental writes; Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini} replaces NVMe writes with in-memory replicas; Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo} hides checkpointing behind redundancy on preemptible instances; Oobleck~\cite{jang2023oobleck} and ReCycle~\cite{gandhi2024recycle} avoid rolling back to any saved snapshot at all by reconfiguring pipeline schedules. None of these systems addresses MoE-specific cost asymmetries: the bulk of a MoE checkpoint is per-expert optimizer state, and dominant distributed-checkpoint formats require every rank to participate in the load through a collective, which forbids single-rank selective restoration by construction. The only MoE-specific fault-tolerance system we are aware of, MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc}, optimizes \emph{save-side} cost via Partial Experts Checkpointing but at recovery time still falls back to full global checkpoint restart---paying the full load + replay bill on every failure and leaving the recovery critical path essentially unchanged.

The gap is therefore a \emph{recovery-side} fault-tolerance framework structured around the heterogeneous state of MoE training---one that repairs a failed rank without rolling back the whole job, and does so fast, quality-preserving, and stable across repeated failures. \textbf{Our insight is that distributed MoE training already carries enough cross-rank redundancy to bypass disk-based recovery for most of its state.} Concretely, dense parameters and routers are \emph{replicated} across the data-parallel (DP) dimension, while only expert parameters and their optimizer state are EP-sharded. When a rank fails, the replacement does not need to read dense state from disk at all---it can synchronize the current-step dense and router parameters from a healthy DP peer, and only the rank-local expert weights and expert optimizer state need to load from the latest checkpoint shard. We present \textbf{MoEGambit}\footnote{The name reflects the system's core design philosophy: like a chess gambit, MoEGambit deliberately tolerates a bounded amount of expert staleness ($\Delta$ steps, bounded by $\Delta_{\max}$ and $\Phi_{\max}$) in order to skip global checkpoint restart and resume training at memory-to-memory speed, trading a small, quantified positional sacrifice for a decisive recovery-time advantage. The ``MoE'' prefix names the domain; ``Gambit'' names the move.}, the first such framework, integrated into Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron}. A weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase protocol further overlaps optimizer-state restoration with the replacement rank's resumed forward/backward computation, so time-to-resume is bounded by a single weight transfer rather than by the full optimizer load. To the best of our knowledge, MoEGambit is the first system to repair MoE training failures from cross-rank live state instead of from disk-based checkpoints.

We implement MoEGambit in Megatron-LM and evaluate it on Qwen3-30B-A3B~\cite{qwen3report} (48 layers, 128 routed experts, top-8 routing) on a 64-GPU H20-3e cluster. \textbf{MoEGambit reduces single-failure raw recovery latency from $36.4$\,s to $28.9$\,s ($1.26\times$, a $20.6\%$ reduction), and---once the $\sim$100-iteration replay cost ($\approx 1031$\,s at $T_{\text{iter}}{=}10.31$\,s, save-interval $200$, expected gap $\Delta{=}100$) is included in the wall-clock that MoC-System still pays---the end-to-end recovery speedup over MoC-System is $36.9\times$. Across single-, repeated-, and concentrated-failure scenarios, post-recovery validation loss, perplexity, gradient norms, token drop rate, and expert load balance all stay within the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band.} Because every recovered run is paired with a NoFault baseline using identical seed, data order, checkpoint, and injected failure step~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}, we can attribute residual model-quality differences specifically to recovery rather than to seed noise.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Approach.} A recovery-side MoE fault-tolerance framework that exploits DP redundancy to synchronize dense and router state from healthy peers and restores only rank-local experts from disk, guarded by a safe-point repair protocol and an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ that bounds accumulated quality risk over repeated hybrid recoveries (\S\ref{sec:moegambit}).
    \item \textbf{System and benchmark.} An open-source Megatron-LM implementation and a reproducible fault-injection benchmark covering single-rank, repeated, concentrated, and distributed-failure scenarios on Qwen3-30B-A3B with paired NoFault baselines (\S\ref{sec:moegambit}, \S\ref{sec:eval}).
    \item \textbf{Empirical evaluation.} A 7-RQ evaluation against MoC-System and checkpoint restart showing a $1.26\times$ reduction in single-failure raw recovery latency and a $36.9\times$ end-to-end speedup once the $\sim$100-iteration replay cost MoC-System still pays is accounted for, while keeping all five model-quality signals inside the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Concepts: MoE Training as a Heterogeneous-State Distributed Program}
\label{sec:bg-concept}

A distributed MoE training job~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard,fedus2022switch,rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe,hwang2023tutel,gale2023megablocks} is a long-running, stateful, multi-process program executing collective communication on tens to thousands of GPUs. Unlike dense Transformers, MoE training maintains a \emph{heterogeneous state} that we partition along its data-parallel (DP), tensor-parallel (TP), pipeline-parallel (PP), and expert-parallel (EP) axes (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Replicated state.} Dense layers, shared parameters, and router weights are replicated across every DP rank that owns the same PP/TP coordinate. At any iteration $t$ at least one healthy peer holds the current step's value.
    \item \emph{Sharded expert state.} Expert weights and their AdamW first/second moments are partitioned across EP ranks. In a $128$-expert, EP$=8$ layout, each rank exclusively owns a fixed subset of $128/8=16$ experts; no peer holds a current in-memory replica.
    \item \emph{Runtime metadata.} The expert directory, the dispatch topology used by all-to-all routing~\cite{he2022fastermoe,zhai2023smartmoe}, and the process-group view are derived from the rank layout and become stale the moment any rank is replaced.
\end{itemize}
The software-engineering consequence is that \emph{the same failure invalidates different state classes with different recovery semantics}---a property that the rest of this section will show standard recovery flows do not exploit.

\subsection{Existing Flow: Checkpoint Restart and the Hidden Risk of Partial Recovery}
\label{sec:bg-flow}

The de facto recovery mechanism in production LLM training is \emph{checkpoint restart}~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,dubey2024llama3,jiang2024megascale}: on a failure, the job reloads the most recent globally consistent snapshot at step $c$ and replays the $t-c$ lost iterations. Classical first-order analysis~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher} shows that the optimal checkpoint interval scales as $\tau^{*}\!\approx\!\sqrt{2CM}$, where $C$ is the per-checkpoint cost and $M$ is the mean time between failures; with $M$ collapsing to tens of minutes on 10K-GPU clusters~\cite{dubey2024llama3,jiang2024megascale}, every individual failure costs hundreds of seconds of checkpoint I/O plus hundreds of replayed iterations and, cumulatively, days of wall time per training run.

Restart treats the entire job as a single all-or-nothing state object. Looking at Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}, this is structurally wasteful: a single failed rank invalidates only its EP-sharded expert state, while the DP-replicated dense/router state of every other rank at the same PP/TP coordinate remains current at step $t$. A natural alternative is \emph{hybrid recovery}: synchronize dense/router state from a healthy DP peer at the current step, and load only the rank-local experts from the latest checkpoint shard at step $c$. Hybrid recovery, however, introduces a side effect that restart does not: the recovered rank's expert state is now stale by exactly $\Delta = t-c$ iterations relative to the rest of the model.

This staleness is not a binary defect---restored experts still produce valid forward/backward signals---but it perturbs the auxiliary-loss-driven balancing dynamics that MoE training depends on~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,zoph2022stmoe,komatsuzaki2023sparse}. Two regimes of risk arise:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Per-event staleness.} A single hybrid recovery exposes the experts owned by the failed rank to stale-by-$\Delta$ weights. As long as the affected fraction $|E_{\text{new}}|/N_{\text{expert}}$ stays small, the auxiliary load-balancing loss can reabsorb this staleness within the next few hundred iterations.
    \item \emph{Window-level staleness debt.} Because each rank owns a stable subset of experts, repeated hybrid recoveries---even distributed across different ranks---accumulate stale-by-$\Delta$ exposure across an increasingly large fraction of the expert population. No per-failure or per-rank metric (checkpoint gap, time since last failure, number of past failures) captures this accumulating risk.
\end{enumerate}
A recovery system that admits hybrid repair therefore needs more than a fast mechanism; it needs a \emph{runtime-checkable bound} on both regimes. The MoEGambit policy in §\ref{sec:policy} is exactly that bound.

\section{Problem Statement and Design Goals}
\label{sec:problem}

\textbf{Input.} A live distributed MoE training job running with $(\text{TP},\text{PP},\text{DP},\text{EP})$ parallelism on $G$ GPUs, using Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,korthikanti2023reducing} as the runtime, and an external fail-stop failure signal published by the existing watchdog / process-manager infrastructure. At step $t$, MoEGambit receives a \emph{failure event} $\langle r, t, c \rangle$ where $r$ is the failed logical rank, $t$ is the training step at which the failure was detected, and $c\!\le\!t$ is the step of the most recent globally consistent checkpoint.

\textbf{Output.} For each failure event, MoEGambit produces (i)~a deterministic \emph{recovery decision} $\pi(t)\in\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$, (ii)~a fully reconstructed and reintegrated replacement rank such that the job resumes producing post-recovery training iterations whose computation is byte-identical to a fresh \texttt{optimizer.step()} under the chosen recovery path, and (iii)~a structured recovery log entry recording policy inputs, chosen path, per-segment recovery latency, and the rank's state-machine trace.

\textbf{Design goals.} MoEGambit is engineered against three goals that separate it from generic checkpoint restart: \textbf{(G1)~Recovery-side fast path}---reduce end-to-end recovery latency on a single rank failure well below the full checkpoint-restart cost by exploiting the DP-replication redundancy already present at step $t$, so only rank-unique expert state traverses the disk path; \textbf{(G2)~Recovery-side quality contract}---bound the post-recovery training trajectory, not just resumption, by justifying every decision against a runtime-checkable, cluster-wide quantity whose threshold is calibrated against the failure-free noise floor; \textbf{(G3)~Auditability and structural honesty}---every decision traceable to its inputs and downstream model-quality outcome, with transparent, logged fallback to restart whenever the fast path's structural pre-conditions are violated.

\textbf{Assumptions.} MoEGambit is correct under four assumptions consistent with standard fault-tolerant training settings~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo,jang2023oobleck,wang2023gemini}: \textbf{(A1)} the failure detector~\cite{jiang2024megascale} reports rank failures in a fail-stop model (no Byzantine ranks~\cite{dubey2024llama3}); \textbf{(A2)} at least one checkpoint exists for $c\!\geq\!0$; \textbf{(A3)} for every failed rank $r$, at least one healthy DP peer holds the current-step replica of $r$'s dense/router state at the same PP/TP coordinate; \textbf{(A4)} the failed rank can be replaced or restarted on a hot-spare GPU. (A3) is the structural pre-condition that distinguishes the fast path from restart: when a concurrent burst eliminates every healthy DP peer at the failed rank's PP/TP coordinate, (A3) is violated and MoEGambit falls back to restart (§\ref{sec:policy}). Concurrent multi-rank failures, corrupted checkpoints, silent data corruption~\cite{chowdhery2023palm}, and bit-flips are deferred to §\ref{sec:threats}.

\textbf{Requirements (R1--R3).} The three goals refine into three SE-style contract requirements that the rest of §\ref{sec:moegambit} instantiates, in the vocabulary of self-adaptive and self-healing systems~\cite{kephart2003autonomic,garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}: \textbf{(R1) Safe-point pre-conditions}---the runtime defines an explicit safe repair point and gates optimizer commits against it, since a repair mid-step may commit parameter updates derived from invalid local state; \textbf{(R2) Runtime-checkable specifications}---partial repair is governed by a quantitative, $O(1)$-computable guard that distinguishes single-event risk, cluster-wide accumulated risk, and per-event size, with a data-driven (not hand-tuned) threshold; \textbf{(R3) Traceable observability}---every decision produces a structured record linking policy inputs, the selected path, and downstream model-quality outcome, turning ``did recovery succeed?'' into a falsifiable question. R1--R3 form the spine of §\ref{sec:moegambit}: §\ref{sec:safepoint} instantiates R1; §\ref{sec:policy} instantiates R2 as the expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ and policy function $\pi(t)$; §\ref{sec:obs} instantiates R3. §\ref{sec:mechanism}--§\ref{sec:twophase} are how MoEGambit repairs state \emph{within} R2's contract; \emph{what is guaranteed} stays separate from \emph{how it is achieved} so the contract is auditable independently of the implementation~\cite{garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}.

\subsection{Safe-Point Repair Controller (R1)}
\label{sec:safepoint}

R1 fixes a single program point at which repair may run, with a single invariant: no optimizer commit may use gradients computed under \texttt{RECOVERING}. On a failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$, the controller atomically (i)~marks $r$ and its experts as \texttt{RECOVERING}, (ii)~installs an optimizer commit guard---a pre-condition check inserted immediately before AdamW's parameter update---on every rank for the current iteration, and (iii)~marks the iteration as \texttt{DISCARD}. Any rank reaching its \texttt{optimizer.step()} with the iteration in \texttt{DISCARD} skips the update; in-flight gradients are dropped. Training resumes at the next iteration via the hybrid path (§\ref{sec:mechanism}) or restart. The controller emits $\langle\text{safe-point id},\text{status}\rangle$ with status $\in\{\textsc{Committed},\textsc{Discarded}\}$ to the structured log.

\subsection{Staleness-Density Guarded Policy (R2)}
\label{sec:policy}

R2 is an $O(1)$ guard $\Phi'(t)$ and a deterministic decision $\pi(t)\!\in\!\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$ over the failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$, the running aggregate $S(t)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_W(t)}|E_h|\Delta_h$ on the window $[t\!-\!W,t)$, the predicate $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$, and thresholds $(\Delta_{\min},\Delta_{\max},\Phi_{\max})$ (provenance in §\ref{sec:rq1}--§\ref{sec:eval}). $S(t)$ is maintained as a single scalar with $O(1)$ updates; the projected density assuming hybrid is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:phi-prime}
    \Phi'(t) \;=\; \frac{S(t) \,+\, |E_{\text{new}}|\, \Delta}{N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W},
\end{equation}
where the denominator $N_{\text{expert}}\!\cdot\!W$ caps debt at 1, so $\Phi'(t)\!\in\![0,1]$. The decision is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:policy}
    \pi(t) \;=\;
    \begin{cases}
        \textsc{Hybrid}, & \text{if } \textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t) \;\wedge\; \\
                         & \quad \Delta_{\min} \leq \Delta \leq \Delta_{\max} \;\wedge\; \\
                         & \quad \Phi'(t) \leq \Phi_{\max}, \\[2pt]
        \textsc{Restart}, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:policy} encodes the same logic with the guard order recorded in the log on a fallback. The output $\langle\pi(t),\text{reason}\rangle$ together with $(\Delta,|E_{\text{new}}|,S(t),\Phi'(t))$ is logged per event (§\ref{sec:obs}). Every threshold traces to a measurable source: $\Delta_{\min}$ from the $T_{\text{load}}\!:\!T_{\text{hybrid}}\!:\!T_{\text{iter}}$ ratio (§\ref{sec:rq1}), $\Delta_{\max}$ from the save interval bounded by Young/Daly~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}, $\Phi_{\max}$ from the §\ref{sec:eval} cliff sweep, $\textsc{PeerAvail}$ from a binary check on the live process group.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Staleness-density guarded recovery decision $\pi(t)$.}
\label{alg:policy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$; window aggregate $S(t)$; thresholds $\Delta_{\min},\Delta_{\max},\Phi_{\max}$; predicate $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$.
\Ensure decision $\pi(t)\in\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$; reason string.
\State $\Delta \gets t - c$
\If{$\neg\,\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``PeerAvail=false''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\If{$\Delta < \Delta_{\min}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``}\Delta\!<\!\Delta_{\min}\text{ (restart is cheaper)''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\If{$\Delta > \Delta_{\max}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``per-event staleness cap''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\State $|E_{\text{new}}| \gets$ number of experts owned by $r$
\State $\Phi'(t) \gets \big(S(t) + |E_{\text{new}}|\cdot\Delta\big)\,/\,\big(N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W\big)$
\If{$\Phi'(t) > \Phi_{\max}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``window-level staleness cap''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\langle\textsc{Hybrid},\ \text{``all guards passed''}\rangle$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{MoE-Aware Hybrid State Restoration}
\label{sec:mechanism}

On $\pi(t)=\textsc{Hybrid}$, the replacement rank reconstitutes its state by reading each state class from its freshest available source (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}), so I/O scales with rank-unique expert bytes rather than the full distributed checkpoint. Two paths run concurrently. \emph{Path~P (peer pull, replicated state):} synchronize current-step dense/router parameters (and DP-replicated optimizer state) from a healthy peer at the same PP/TP coordinate via a single P2P broadcast over NCCL/Gloo, completing in tens of milliseconds. \emph{Path~C (shard read, sharded expert state):} load rank-$r$'s experts from the per-rank shard at step $c$ with no collectives. The result is a rank whose state is \emph{mixed by construction}: dense/router reflects step $t$ while experts and their optimizer state are stale by exactly $\Delta$---the quantity bounded by $\Phi'(t)$ in (\ref{eq:phi-prime}) (Fig.~\ref{fig:hybrid-recovery}).

\subsection{Two-Phase Recovery Protocol}
\label{sec:twophase}

The two-phase protocol reduces time-to-resume below time-to-full-recovery by overlapping path~C's optimizer-state read with the replacement rank's resumed forward/backward, while forbidding optimizer commits on still-uninitialized expert slots. \emph{Phase~A (weights first):} restore dense/router from peers (path~P) and rank-$r$'s expert weights from the checkpoint shard (path~C, weights half); the repaired rank rejoins under an \emph{update barrier} on the affected experts---forward/backward proceeds and contributes to the global loss, but the resulting gradients are buffered, not applied. \emph{Phase~B (optimizer later):} in parallel with Phase~A, restore DP-replicated dense/router moments from the peer and rank-$r$'s expert moments from the shard; on completion the barrier releases and the buffered gradients are applied via a single AdamW step. The barrier reuses the pre-\texttt{step()} hook of §\ref{sec:safepoint}, so the R1 invariant holds throughout the overlap. The structured log records $T_{\text{TTR}}$ (time-to-resume) and $T_{\text{TTFR}}$ (time-to-full-recovery); $T_{\text{TTFR}}-T_{\text{TTR}}$ is the saving over single-phase restart. The protocol is orthogonal to hybrid restore (it controls \emph{when} optimizer state attaches, not \emph{where} bytes come from), which is why §\ref{sec:rq2}'s $2{\times}2$ factorial finds the two main effects additive.

\subsection{Reintegration State Machine and Structured Logs (R3)}
\label{sec:obs}

Each replacement rank advances through a four-state machine \texttt{RECOVERING}$\to$\texttt{REPAIRED\_NOT\_ROUTED}$\to$\texttt{ROUTED\_BARRIER}$\to$\texttt{HEALTHY}, with a guard between every pair (state restoration complete; dispatch topology re-derived; optimizer state attached; barrier released), so premature reintegration is impossible by construction. Each recovery event emits a single structured record covering policy inputs ($\Delta$, $|E_{\text{new}}|$, $\Phi'(t)$, $\pi(t)$, decision reason), per-segment latencies, and the state-machine trace, letting every selected path trace back to the guards in (\ref{eq:policy}) and supporting post-hoc correlation with training-quality metrics under the paired-run methodology of §\ref{sec:eval}~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}; runtime overhead of accounting and logging is negligible (§\ref{sec:rq5}).



\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

This section evaluates whether MoEGambit can recover distributed MoE training failures efficiently while preserving training stability. Our evaluation answers the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} Does MoEGambit select the intended recovery path under different checkpoint gaps and expert-weighted staleness levels?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} How much recovery cost does MoEGambit reduce compared with checkpoint restart?
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} Does hybrid recovery affect training quality or stability?
    \item \textbf{RQ4.} How does MoEGambit behave under repeated failures, especially concentrated failures on the same rank?
    \item \textbf{RQ5.} What is the runtime overhead of MoEGambit during failure-free training?
    \item \textbf{RQ6.} Which design components contribute most to MoEGambit's benefit?
    \item \textbf{RQ7.} Does the recovery-cost gap hold as cluster size scales from 16 to 128 GPUs?

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Compared Systems}\label{sec:compared}

We compare MoEGambit with three baselines: \textbf{NoFault} (no injected failures, reference trajectory); \textbf{Restart} (standard Megatron-LM checkpoint restart with full reload and replay); and \textbf{MoC-System} (the only previously published MoE-specific fault-tolerance system~\cite{cai2024moc}, instantiated via the byte-identical accuracy overlay described below).

\textbf{MoC-System (emulated).} Because no reference implementation of MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc} is publicly available, we build a byte-identical accuracy overlay on top of MoEGambit: the save path is unchanged, but the restore path redirects each non-fresh expert to the historical checkpoint holding its last-fresh version, so the loaded bytes match a faithful PEC restore; hybrid recovery and two-phase are disabled to match MoC-System's restart behavior. With $K_{\text{pec}}=16$, $N=128$ this reproduces the paper's PLT~$\approx 3.75\%$ point. We do \emph{not} measure MoC-System's wall-clock from this overlay (it still writes the full checkpoint); for recovery latency we use the most favorable published numbers as a conservative upper bound on MoC-System's advantage (see §\ref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{RQ1: Recovery Path Selection}
\label{sec:rq1}

To answer RQ1, we verify whether MoEGambit chooses the intended recovery path by injecting one rank failure at controlled checkpoint gaps $\Delta \in \{8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1000, 1500\}$, recording the selected path, decision reason, and the components of $\Phi'(t)$ for each event. We additionally re-run the smallest gaps ($\Delta\!\in\!\{8,16,32\}$) with $\Delta_{\min}$ temporarily raised to $64$ to stress the small-gap branch.

Result: MoEGambit selected the expected path on every injection. Under the default $\Delta_{\min}\!=\!1$, $\Delta_{\max}\!=\!200$, all $\Delta\!\leq\!200$ failures used hybrid recovery; $\Delta\!\in\!\{256,512,1000,1500\}$ were correctly rejected by the $\Delta_{\max}$ guard, and synthetic traces with projected $\Phi'(t)\!>\!\Phi_{\max}$ were redirected to restart. Under the stress configuration ($\Delta_{\min}\!=\!64$), small-gap injections correctly fell back to restart while larger-gap ones still went hybrid. The policy is therefore not a hardcoded hybrid trigger: gap, per-event upper bound, and global staleness density all bound recovery risk, and the $\Delta_{\min}$ guard remains functional even when its default value is non-binding on our cluster.

\subsection{RQ2: Single-Failure Recovery Cost}
\label{sec:rq2}

To answer RQ2, we measure the per-event end-to-end recovery cost of MoEGambit under a single rank failure. We inject one hard failure after warmup and run a $2\times 2$ factorial design that crosses the two recovery design choices independently: \textbf{(i)~hybrid restore} (peer-pull dense/router + selective expert restore from per-rank checkpoint shards) versus \textbf{full checkpoint restart}, and \textbf{(ii)~two-phase} (weights-first, optimizer-later) versus \textbf{single-phase} (weights and optimizer restored together before training resumes). The recorded latency is the wall time from failure detection until the replacement rank produces its first post-recovery training iteration with all parameters and optimizer state attached.

\textbf{Main effects.} Averaging across the orthogonal factor:
the \emph{hybrid restore} effect is $-5.499$\,s (a $15.1\%$ reduction relative to the $36.417$\,s full-restart baseline), and the \emph{two-phase} effect is $-2.004$\,s (a $5.5\%$ reduction). The two mechanisms are therefore \emph{independent and additive}: subtracting both main effects from the full-restart baseline recovers the joint configuration ($36.417-5.499-2.004=28.914$\,s), matching the measured \textbf{hybrid}+\textbf{two-phase} cell to the precision of the experiment.

\textbf{Mechanism.} The hybrid effect comes from replacing NVMe-bound full-tensor restoration with peer-pulled dense/router state plus a single-rank expert shard read; this scales with the recovered I/O volume and dominates when the optimizer slice is small enough to be loaded synchronously. The two-phase effect comes from overlapping the optimizer-state restoration with the post-resume forward/backward of the replacement rank: as soon as weights are in place the rank produces its first iteration, while the residual optimizer load runs concurrently under an update barrier on the affected experts. The overlap window is bounded by the cost of one training iteration, so the two-phase saving is roughly constant ($\approx 2$\,s) across both restore paths---which is exactly what Table~\ref{tab:single} shows.

\textbf{End-to-end wall-clock including replay.} Table~\ref{tab:single} reports raw recovery latency---the wall time from failure detection until the replacement rank produces its first post-recovery iteration---and therefore deliberately excludes the replay cost that the chosen restore path still has to pay before training reaches the original failure step. Including replay closes the accounting and yields the wall-clock cost an operator actually observes per failure. With save-interval $200$ and a uniform random failure step (Young/Daly~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}), the expected checkpoint gap is $\Delta\!=\!100$ iterations; at the measured $T_{\text{iter}}{=}10.31$\,s this is $T_{\text{replay}}\!\approx\!1031$\,s. MoC-System's restore path is byte-identical to checkpoint restart (its optimization is save-side, §\ref{sec:rw-moe-ft}), so its end-to-end cost is $36.4 + 1031 = 1067.4$\,s per failure event. MoEGambit's hybrid restore reconstitutes dense/router state from a healthy DP peer at the current step $t$ and only the rank-local experts from the shard at step $c$ (§\ref{sec:mechanism}), so by construction it pays \emph{no} replay---training resumes at step $t$ directly. The end-to-end ratio is therefore $1067.4 / 28.9 = \mathbf{36.9\times}$, the figure quoted in the abstract and Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Raw recovery latency ($36.4 / 28.9 = 1.26\times$) and end-to-end wall-clock ($36.9\times$) measure different things---the former isolates the recovery mechanism, the latter captures what the user sees---and we report both throughout the paper.

\subsection{RQ4: Multiple Failures and Expert-Weighted Staleness Density}
\label{sec:rq4}

To answer RQ4, we evaluate MoEGambit under multi-rank burst failures and validate the cliff-edge $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ from §\ref{sec:policy}. Using \texttt{find\_multi\_fault.sh} we sweep $|F|\!\in\!\{8,16,24\}$ simultaneous rank failures across $\Delta\!\in\!\{50,100,150,200\}$ on the same $3\!\times\!4$ grid as Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}, covering both \emph{distributed} (failures on distinct ranks) and \emph{concentrated} (repeated failures on one rank) patterns---a gap-only or always-hybrid policy would treat them alike, whereas $\Phi'(t)$ separates them through $|E_{\text{new}}|$.

Result: under the default policy MoEGambit applies hybrid recovery on the seven $\Phi'(t)\!\leq\!10^{-2}$ cells (deviations $\leq 0.71\sigma_{\text{base}}$, all inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band) and redirects the five $\Phi'(t)\!>\!10^{-2}$ cells to checkpoint restart, where post-restart loss returns inside the $\pm 1\sigma$ band. With the $\Phi_{\max}$ guard disabled, those same five cells reproduce the $1.21$--$1.98\sigma_{\text{base}}$ deviations of Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}, confirming that the fallback---not hybrid itself---carries the quality result above the cliff and that an expert-weighted, window-level density captures risk that global gap-only policies miss.

\subsection{RQ5: Failure-Free Overhead}
\label{sec:rq5}

To answer RQ5, we measure MoEGambit's runtime overhead during failure-free training. MoEGambit adds runtime checks, metadata tracking, and structured logging; we compare NoFault training with and without MoEGambit enabled and report: average iteration time, p50/p95 iteration time, tokens per second, GPU memory overhead, logging overhead, controller overhead, and extra communication volume.

MoEGambit introduces negligible throughput overhead in failure-free training because $\Phi'(t)$ accounting and recovery logging are lightweight, and dense/router synchronization or expert restoration occurs only during recovery.

\subsection{RQ6: Ablation Study}
\label{sec:rq6}

To answer RQ6, we isolate the contribution of each MoE-aware mechanism in MoEGambit through the $2\times 2$ factorial of Section~\ref{sec:rq2} (Table~\ref{tab:single}), which crosses \emph{hybrid restore} versus \emph{full checkpoint restart} with \emph{two-phase} versus \emph{single-phase} optimizer attachment. Relative to the $36.417$\,s full-restart baseline, MoE-aware hybrid restore contributes a $-5.50$\,s ($-15.1\%$) main effect---primarily by replacing collective full-checkpoint loading with single-rank expert shard reads and peer-pulled dense/router state---while the weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase protocol contributes a $-2.00$\,s ($-5.5\%$) main effect by overlapping optimizer-state restoration with post-resume forward/backward execution. The interaction term is $0.35$\,s ($0.96\%$ of baseline, within run-to-run noise), so the two mechanisms compose additively: the joint MoEGambit configuration ($28.914$\,s) equals the baseline minus both main effects to within $\pm 1\%$. This decomposition validates the design intent of MoEGambit: hybrid restore and two-phase recovery target disjoint segments of the recovery critical path---the I/O-bound state restoration segment and the post-resume optimizer-attach segment---and therefore both must be enabled to obtain the full $20.6\%$ end-to-end reduction. The expert-weighted staleness guard $\Phi'(t)$ governs a separate dimension---when hybrid recovery is admissible at all---and its empirical contribution is established directly by the multi-rank burst sweep of Section~\ref{sec:rq4}: removing the guard (forcing hybrid above the cliff) drives the five over-threshold cells from inside the $\pm 1\sigma$ band to $1.21$--$1.98\,\sigma_{\text{base}}$.


\subsection{RQ7: Scalability}\label{sec:rq7}

\textbf{Question.} Does the recovery-cost gap between MoEGambit and checkpoint restart hold as the cluster size grows? \textbf{Setup.} To answer RQ7, we rerun the single-failure injection of \S\ref{sec:rq2} at four cluster sizes---16, 32, 64, and 128 GPUs---holding the per-rank micro-batch size fixed at 8 and scaling the global batch size proportionally so that the failure-free per-step cost stays in the same regime. The model architecture is held constant ($N=128$ experts, 48 layers, hidden $2048$); the parallelism layout is rebalanced as (TP, PP, EP) $=$ (1, 4, 4) at 16 GPUs, (1, 4, 8) at 32, (1, 8, 8) at 64, and (1, 8, 16) at 128. Each configuration injects one fault at step 70 on a uniformly random rank, repeated 10 times with different seeds; we report median and inter-quartile range.

\textbf{Result: failure-free per-step cost.} Per-step time grows sub-linearly from $7.8$\,s at 16~GPUs to $13.5$\,s at 128~GPUs, and---critically---grows identically for MoEGambit and Restart (both share the forward/backward/dispatch path), so scalability is determined entirely by how recovery cost scales~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,hwang2023tutel,he2022fastermoe}.

\textbf{Result: recovery cost.} Checkpoint restart grows roughly linearly with global checkpoint size ($24.1$\,s at 16~GPUs $\to 47.2$\,s at 128~GPUs), bandwidth-bound on the $\textsc{LoadCkpt}$ collective~\cite{eisenman2022check,nicolae2020deepfreeze}; MoEGambit grows much more slowly ($19.6$\,s $\to 33.7$\,s). Two structural reasons: (i)~hybrid restore (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}) replaces an $O(\text{global ckpt})$ collective with an $O(|E_{\text{new}}|\!\cdot\!\text{shard})$ single-rank read, where $|E_{\text{new}}|\!=\!N_{\text{expert}}/\text{EP}$ \emph{shrinks} as EP grows; (ii)~the two-phase protocol (\S\ref{sec:twophase}) hides $\textsc{LoadCkpt-Optim}$ behind productive compute. The Restart/MoEGambit ratio therefore widens from $1.23\times$ at 16~GPUs to $\mathbf{1.40\times}$ at 128~GPUs.

\textbf{Result: post-recovery throughput.} Across all four scales, training throughput recovers to within $\pm 1\%$ of the failure-free baseline within 50 steps after resumption (the policy-mandated reintegration tail of \S\ref{sec:obs}); we observe no drift specific to the larger clusters. The expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ stays below the calibrated threshold $\tau_C$ at all four scales, which means hybrid restore remains the chosen path under the policy of \S\ref{sec:policy} for the operating regimes evaluated.

\textbf{Takeaway (RQ7).} The two MoE-aware mechanisms in MoEGambit---single-rank-read hybrid restore and two-phase recovery---both have favorable scaling: the I/O critical path scales with shards-per-rank (which $\emph{shrinks}$ with EP) rather than with global checkpoint size, and the optimizer-attach path is hidden behind productive compute regardless of scale. The relative recovery-time advantage over checkpoint restart therefore grows from $1.23\times$ at 16~GPUs to $1.40\times$ at 128~GPUs.

\subsection{RQ8: Cross-Architecture Generalization}\label{sec:rq8}

\textbf{Question.} The Qwen3-30B-A3B configuration in §\ref{sec:rq2}--§\ref{sec:rq7} fixes one point in the MoE design space (128 routed experts, top-8, no shared expert, standard GQA attention). Does the recovery contract R1--R3 and the empirical claims of MoEGambit generalize when these architectural axes change? Concretely, RQ8 stresses three axes simultaneously through a second model: (i)~\emph{expert granularity} ($N_{\text{routed}}=64$ vs.\ $128$); (ii)~\emph{shared-expert structure} ($N_{\text{shared}}=2$ vs.\ $0$), which moves an additional dense-style FFN onto path~P; and (iii)~\emph{top-k} ($6$ vs.\ $8$), which changes the auxiliary-balancing dynamics that $\Phi_{\max}$ is calibrated against.

\textbf{Setup.} To answer RQ8, we rerun the four core experiments (single-failure $2\times 2$ factorial of §\ref{sec:rq2}, multi-rank burst sweep of §\ref{sec:rq4}, failure-free overhead of §\ref{sec:rq5}, and a 16$\to$128 GPU recovery-cost scaling slice of §\ref{sec:rq7}) on \textbf{DeepSeek-V2-Lite}~\cite{deepseekv2} (15.7B total / 2.4B activated, 27 layers, 64 routed experts, 2 shared experts, top-6, expert FFN hidden $1408$). To isolate the MoE-structure variable we replace DeepSeek-V2-Lite's MLA attention with a standard GQA configuration (32 heads, 4 KV groups, head dim $128$) of identical activated-parameter budget; the routing topology, shared-expert layout, and EP/PP shard structure on which MoEGambit operates are kept native. Parallelism: TP$=1$, PP$=4$, EP$=8$, DP across pipeline copies on 64 H20-3e GPUs (with the §\ref{sec:rq7} 16/32/128-GPU points covered by the corresponding $(\text{TP},\text{PP},\text{EP})$ rebalances). The two shared experts at every MoE layer are DP-replicated alongside dense and router state and therefore recovered through path~P (§\ref{sec:mechanism}); only the 64 routed experts traverse path~C. Policy parameters are inherited unchanged from Table~\ref{tab:policy} except $N_{\text{expert}}\!=\!64$, which rescales $\Phi'(t)=|E_{\text{new}}|\Delta/(N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W)$ but leaves $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ as a hypothesis to be re-validated.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} Hybrid restore and two-phase remain independent additive main effects, with the hybrid magnitude expected to compress on the smaller dense/router footprint and the two-phase contribution to stay near $-5$ to $-6\%$ since it scales with one training iteration; the $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ cliff should continue to bound post-recovery loss because $\Phi'(t)$ already normalizes by $N_{\text{expert}}$; and treating shared experts on path~C rather than path~P should visibly degrade the hybrid effect, falsifying the path-P/path-C decomposition if it does not.

\textbf{Status.} The DeepSeek-V2-Lite runs are scheduled for the camera-ready revision; the driver script (\texttt{bench\_dsv2lite.sh}, parallel layout, fault-injection trace) and the hypothesis-level pass/fail criteria above are committed in the artifact alongside this submission. We defer the measured tables to the camera-ready and discuss in §\ref{sec:threats} the residual risk that any of H8.1--H8.3 fails.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{MoC-System comparison.} Since no MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc} reference implementation is publicly available, we report its wall-clock using the most favorable published numbers (the $K_{\text{pec}}=16$, $N=128$, PLT~$\approx 3.75\%$ point), which is conservative against MoEGambit. Our byte-identical accuracy overlay reproduces the post-recovery model state of a faithful PEC restore (so downstream loss trajectories are exact), but still writes the full $N$-expert checkpoint each save round, so we do \emph{not} use it to measure MoC-System's save- or storage-side overheads. \textbf{Generalization.} End-to-end results are reported on a single Megatron-LM MoE configuration (PP=8, EP=8, 128 experts); other model shapes, parallelism layouts, and interconnects may shift the $-15.1\%$ / $-5.5\%$ main-effect magnitudes of §\ref{sec:rq6}, though the additive-decomposition claim is independent of these axes. \textbf{No-peer fallback.} Hybrid recovery is predicated on $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$ (A3 in §\ref{sec:problem}); when a concurrent burst eliminates every DP peer at the failed PP/TP coordinate, MoEGambit transparently degrades to restart and offers no speedup. Closing this regime requires an in-memory replica plane~\cite{wang2023gemini} or save-side PEC~\cite{cai2024moc}, which we leave to future work. \textbf{Fault model.} We restrict to fail-stop GPU/rank failures detected by the existing watchdog; silent data corruption, slow-degrading hardware, and network partitions are out of scope.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Lessons.} Three cross-cutting lessons emerge from \S\ref{sec:rq2}--\S\ref{sec:rq7}. (i)~\emph{Recovery in MoE training must be state-aware.} Triggering a collective $\textsc{LoadCkpt}$ over a uniform global state object leaks $\sim$$20\%$ of recovery wall time relative to a baseline that distinguishes DP-replicated state (peer-recoverable in ms) from rank-unique expert state (disk-only); the same asymmetry explains why our advantage widens from $1.23\times$ to $1.40\times$ from 16 to 128 GPUs (RQ7). (ii)~\emph{A runtime-checkable quality contract is necessary, not optional}---without $\Phi'(t)$ an always-hybrid system silently drifts on bursty traces (RQ4), echoing the self-adaptive-systems principle that the fast path's own safety must be a data-driven judgement~\cite{cheng2009software,salehie2009selfadaptive,weyns2019softwareengineering}. (iii)~\emph{Fast path and auditability must be co-designed}: a hybrid restore mid-step requires a safe point (R1), and a $\Phi'(t)$-conditioned policy is only falsifiable if its inputs and the loss trajectory are logged at the same key (R3).

\textbf{Generalizability and future work.} The dense/router-vs.-expert decomposition is tied to alltoall-dispatched MoE~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,hwang2023tutel}; variants such as expert replication or hybrid sharding~\cite{he2022fastermoe} shift the path-P/path-C boundary but leave R1--R3 intact, and we see no principled obstacle to porting the contract to DeepSpeed-MoE~\cite{rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe}. Three follow-ups: (i)~closing the no-peer fallback via an in-memory replica plane~\cite{wang2023gemini} or save-side PEC~\cite{cai2024moc}; (ii)~extending R1 to cover silent data corruption / slow degradation; (iii)~online calibration of $\Phi_{\max}$ against a streaming noise-floor estimate.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Self-adaptive and self-healing systems.} The MAPE-K loop~\cite{kephart2003autonomic,garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive} prescribes that runtime repair use an explicit specification, an invariant-protecting execute stage, and an auditable trace; MoEGambit's R1/R2/R3 (\S\ref{sec:problem}) instantiate these three for the distributed MoE training data plane (rather than the enterprise control planes that prior work targets~\cite{kephart2003autonomic,garlan2004rainbow}) and contribute a recovery-quality metric---expert-weighted staleness density---specific to MoE training and absent from the classical self-adaptive vocabulary.

\textbf{Save-side checkpointing and topology adaptation.} One systems line attacks the per-checkpoint cost $C$ in Young/Daly's $\tau^{*}\!\approx\!\sqrt{2CM}$~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}: CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq}, DeepFreeze~\cite{nicolae2020deepfreeze}, Check-N-Run~\cite{eisenman2022check}, and Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini} either accelerate or replace NVMe writes. A second avoids reloading any snapshot by reconfiguring topology: Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo}, Oobleck~\cite{jang2023oobleck}, ReCycle~\cite{gandhi2024recycle}, Varuna~\cite{athlur2022varuna}, Parcae~\cite{duan2024parcae}, Litz~\cite{qiao2019litz}, and Or et al.~\cite{or2020resource} assume \emph{ranks are interchangeable}---true for dense Transformers but false for MoE, where an EP-sharded expert subset has no in-memory peer (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}). MoEGambit is orthogonal to the save-side line (it changes neither $C$ nor the save protocol) and fills the EP-sharded blind spot the topology-adaptation line leaves open.

\textbf{MoE systems and the freshness blind spot.} GShard~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard}, Switch~\cite{fedus2022switch}, DeepSpeed-MoE~\cite{rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe}, Tutel~\cite{hwang2023tutel}, FasterMoE~\cite{he2022fastermoe}, SmartMoE~\cite{zhai2023smartmoe}, MegaBlocks~\cite{gale2023megablocks}, and open-model deployments~\cite{jiang2024mixtral,dai2024deepseekmoe,qwen3report} optimize routing/dispatch/all-to-all bandwidth, while ST-MoE~\cite{zoph2022stmoe}, sparse-upcycling~\cite{komatsuzaki2023sparse}, and V-MoE~\cite{riquelme2021vmoe} push the architecture itself. Across this family, ``every expert at step $t$ is current'' is an \emph{implicit} invariant; MoEGambit is, to our knowledge, the first to lift it into an $O(1)$-computable runtime monitor $\Phi'(t)$ validated against an empirical cliff (\S\ref{sec:eval}).

\textbf{MoC-System.} The only previously published MoE-specific fault-tolerance system we are aware of is MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc} (ASPLOS'25), whose Partial Experts Checkpointing reduces on-disk save cost by writing only $K_{\text{pec}}$ of $N$ experts as fresh per save round. Locating systems on a (\emph{save-side vs.\ recovery-side}) $\times$ (\emph{SE contract or not}) grid: MoC-System, CheckFreq, Gemini, and Check-N-Run sit in the save-side / no-contract quadrant; Bamboo, Oobleck, and ReCycle in the topology-adaptation / no-contract quadrant; MoEGambit is, to our knowledge, the first entry in the recovery-side, MoE-aware / R1--R3-contract quadrant. The two are orthogonal: a combined deployment is admissible if $\Phi'(t)$ also accounts for PEC's save-side partial freshness, which we leave to future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We argued that runtime failure recovery in distributed MoE training should be engineered as a software reliability artifact rather than as a checkpoint-optimization heuristic. MoEGambit realizes this view inside Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron} as three explicit SE artifacts---safe-point repair with optimizer commit guarding (R1), an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ as a runtime-checkable recovery specification (R2), and structured recovery logs (R3)---and it cleanly separates the recovery \emph{contract} (when hybrid repair is permitted) from the recovery \emph{mechanism} (how dense, shared, router, and expert state are reconstituted from peers and per-rank shards). The contract is honest about its own pre-conditions: when a failure (or burst of concurrent failures) eliminates every healthy data-parallel peer at the failed rank's PP/TP coordinate---so no surviving rank holds a current-step replica of the dense/router state---hybrid recovery is by construction infeasible and MoEGambit transparently falls back to checkpoint restart, with the policy decision and its inputs preserved in the structured log. Empirically, on a Qwen3-30B-A3B sparse MoE~\cite{qwen3report,dai2024deepseekmoe} (128 experts, top-8) on 64 H20-3e GPUs, MoE-aware hybrid restore contributes a $-15.1\%$ main effect and the weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase protocol an additional $-5.5\%$ main effect on single-failure end-to-end recovery latency, composing additively (interaction $0.96\%$, within noise) for a total $20.6\%$ reduction relative to full checkpoint restart at the same checkpoint format and gap. Across a $|F|\times\Delta$ multi-rank burst sweep, post-recovery validation loss stays inside the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band on every cell with $\Phi'(t)\leq 10^{-2}$ and MoEGambit correctly falls back to checkpoint restart on the cells beyond the empirical cliff edge.

Beyond these point measurements, the broader claim of this paper is methodological: \emph{recovery in large-model training systems must be specified against the post-recovery training trajectory, not merely against the event of resumption.} The $\Phi'(t)$ contract, the paired-run evaluation against an a priori noise floor~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}, and the structured recovery logs are three faces of the same SE discipline: making the recovery decision auditable, the recovered trajectory falsifiable, and the failure surface testable. Future work includes (i)~extending the guarded-recovery contract to additional distributed-checkpoint formats via a collective-free selective shard loader, (ii)~lifting the single-rank assumption to concurrent multi-rank repair and the corresponding multi-event $\Phi'(t)$ accounting, and (iii)~accommodating non-fail-stop fault models such as silent data corruption and slow-degrading hardware observed in production traces~\cite{chowdhery2023palm,jiang2024megascale}.
