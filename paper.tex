\begin{abstract}
Large-scale Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) training is a weeks-long, multi-thousand-GPU software process in which hardware failures are routine---yet the dominant recovery mechanism, checkpoint restart, treats the entire distributed state as a monolithic object and offers no runtime specification of when a faster recovery path is safe or how its quality impact can be bounded.
We observe that MoE training state is \emph{structurally heterogeneous}: non-expert parameters (attention, embedding, router) are replicated across data-parallel peers, while expert parameters are sharded with no in-memory replica.
We present \textbf{MoEGambit}, a recovery-side framework for Megatron-LM that introduces an explicit, auditable recovery contract comprising three software-engineering artifacts: a safe-point repair controller that prevents partial optimizer commits~(R1), an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ as a runtime-checkable quality specification with an empirically calibrated cliff threshold~(R2), and a guarded reintegration state machine with structured audit logs~(R3).
The contract cleanly separates \emph{when} hybrid repair is permitted from \emph{how} state is reconstituted; the mechanism pulls non-expert state (attention, embedding, router) from a healthy peer at the current step while loading only rank-local experts from a checkpoint shard.
On Qwen3-30B-A3B (128 experts, 64 GPUs) and DeepSeek-V2-Lite (64 experts), MoEGambit reduces recovery latency by $20.6\%$--$55.0\%$ and eliminates replay cost entirely ($36.9\times$ end-to-end speedup); post-recovery quality stays within the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band across single-, repeated-, and burst-failure scenarios under paired evaluation with identical seeds, data order, and failure steps.
The broader claim is that failure recovery in distributed MoE training should be engineered as a contract-governed runtime subsystem---specified against the post-recovery training trajectory, not merely against resumption.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Training a large language model is a weeks-long, multi-thousand-GPU software process in which hardware failures are routine rather than exceptional. Meta's Llama-3 training trace reports a failure roughly every 45\,minutes across a 16\,384-GPU cluster~\cite{dubey2024llama3}; MegaScale documents hundreds of distinct failure modes during a 10K-GPU production deployment~\cite{jiang2024megascale}; the OPT-175B logbook records weeks of human-in-the-loop intervention~\cite{zhang2022opt}. The dominant recovery mechanism, \emph{checkpoint restart}, reloads the most recent globally consistent snapshot and replays all iterations since that snapshot on every failure, regardless of which state was actually invalidated. On a large cluster where failures arrive every tens of minutes, the cumulative cost of repeated checkpoint I/O and replay amounts to days of GPU time per training run.

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures---adopted by Mixtral~\cite{jiang2024mixtral}, DeepSeek-V2/V3~\cite{deepseekv2,deepseekv3}, and Qwen3-MoE~\cite{qwen3report}---amplify this reliability challenge along a dimension that dense models do not exhibit. Each MoE model carries $5$--$10\times$ more checkpoint state than a compute-equivalent dense model, because the bulk of the state is per-expert optimizer moments sharded across expert-parallel (EP) ranks with no in-memory replica. A single rank failure therefore invalidates two structurally different state classes: \emph{replicated} non-expert parameters (attention, embedding, router---held by every data-parallel peer and recoverable in milliseconds) and \emph{sharded} expert state (unique to the failed rank and recoverable only from disk). Checkpoint restart ignores this heterogeneity entirely---it rolls back the whole job, reloads every byte, and replays every lost iteration, even though the vast majority of the model state is still live in memory on healthy peers.

A substantial body of fault-tolerant training systems has been developed to reduce recovery cost, spanning save-side checkpoint optimization~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq,nicolae2020deepfreeze,eisenman2022check,wang2023gemini,wang2024reft,wan2024bytecheckpoint}, topology adaptation~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo,jang2023oobleck,gandhi2024recycle}, and peer-based dense-model recovery~\cite{zhang2025flashrecovery}. However, none of these systems addresses the \emph{recovery side} of MoE training: save-side systems reduce checkpoint cost but still reload the full snapshot on failure; topology-adaptation systems assume ranks are interchangeable, which is true for dense data-parallel replicas but false for EP-sharded experts; and peer-based recovery assumes all state is replicated, which does not hold for MoE expert parameters. The only MoE-specific system we are aware of, MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc}, optimizes save-side cost via Partial Experts Checkpointing but at recovery time still falls back to full checkpoint restart. The gap is a \emph{recovery-side} framework that exploits MoE's heterogeneous state to repair a failed rank without rolling back the entire job.

We argue that this gap is fundamentally a \emph{software engineering} problem, not merely a systems-optimization opportunity. A fast recovery path that bypasses full checkpoint restart introduces a new risk: the recovered rank's expert state is stale by $\Delta = t - c$ iterations (where $t$ is the current step and $c$ is the checkpoint step), and repeated fast recoveries accumulate this staleness across an increasing fraction of the expert population. Without a runtime-checkable specification of \emph{when} the fast path is safe and \emph{how much} quality degradation it may cause, operators face an unauditable choice between speed and correctness---the ``restart and hope'' operational model. Drawing on the self-adaptive systems literature~\cite{kephart2003autonomic,garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}, we observe that the recovery path should be engineered as a contract-governed runtime subsystem: its correctness specified against the post-recovery training trajectory (not merely against resumption), its quality impact bounded by a runtime-checkable invariant, and its decisions auditable after the fact.

\textbf{Our insight} is that distributed MoE training already carries enough cross-rank redundancy to bypass disk-based recovery for most of its state. Non-expert parameters (attention, embedding, router) are replicated across the data-parallel dimension; only expert parameters and their optimizer state are EP-sharded. When a rank fails, the replacement can pull current-step non-expert state from a healthy peer and load only the rank-local expert shard from the latest checkpoint---eliminating replay entirely and reducing I/O to the rank-unique expert volume.

We present \textbf{MoEGambit}\footnote{Like a chess gambit, MoEGambit tolerates bounded expert staleness to skip global restart and resume at memory-to-memory speed---a small, quantified sacrifice for a decisive time advantage.}, to the best of our knowledge the first recovery-side framework for MoE training, integrated into Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron}. MoEGambit introduces an explicit recovery contract comprising three SE artifacts: a safe-point repair controller that prevents partial optimizer commits~(R1), an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ as a runtime-checkable quality specification with an empirically calibrated cliff threshold~(R2), and a guarded reintegration state machine with structured audit logs~(R3). The contract cleanly separates \emph{when} hybrid repair is permitted from \emph{how} state is reconstituted, so each can be audited and tested independently. A weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase protocol further overlaps optimizer-state restoration with resumed training, bounding time-to-resume by a single weight transfer.

We evaluate MoEGambit on Qwen3-30B-A3B~\cite{qwen3report} (128 experts, 64 H20 GPUs) and DeepSeek-V2-Lite~\cite{deepseekv2} (64 experts) under SE experimentation standards: every recovered run is paired with a NoFault baseline sharing identical seed, data order, checkpoint, and injected failure step~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}, so residual quality differences can be attributed specifically to recovery rather than to seed noise. \textbf{MoEGambit reduces raw recovery latency by $20.6\%$--$55.0\%$ and eliminates replay cost entirely ($36.9\times$ end-to-end speedup). Across single-, repeated-, and burst-failure scenarios, post-recovery validation loss, perplexity, gradient norms, token drop rate, and expert load balance all stay within the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band.}

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Runtime recovery contract.} Three SE artifacts---safe-point invariant~(R1), expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ with an empirically calibrated cliff threshold~(R2), and a guarded reintegration state machine with structured audit logs~(R3)---that separate \emph{when} hybrid repair is permitted from \emph{how} state is reconstituted (\S\ref{sec:moegambit}).
    \item \textbf{MoE-aware hybrid repair mechanism.} A recovery-side mechanism that pulls DP-replicated state from a healthy peer at step $t$ and loads only EP-sharded expert state from a per-rank checkpoint shard at step $c$, with a two-phase protocol that overlaps optimizer restoration with resumed training (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}--\S\ref{sec:twophase}).
    \item \textbf{Paired fault-injection evaluation.} A reproducible benchmark on Qwen3-30B-A3B and DeepSeek-V2-Lite with paired NoFault baselines following SE experimentation guidelines~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}, demonstrating $20.6\%$--$55.0\%$ recovery-latency reduction, $36.9\times$ end-to-end speedup, and no statistically detectable quality degradation across six RQs (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}

The broader claim is that failure recovery in distributed MoE training should be engineered as a contract-governed runtime subsystem---specified against the post-recovery training trajectory, not merely against resumption---making the decision auditable, the trajectory falsifiable, and the failure surface testable. A full comparison with prior fault-tolerant training systems appears in \S\ref{sec:related}.

This section establishes the minimum context required to read the rest of the paper. §\ref{sec:bg-concept} defines distributed MoE training as a stateful software system and identifies the heterogeneous state we will repair. §\ref{sec:bg-flow} describes the de facto recovery flow in production---checkpoint restart---and the structural reason it fails to exploit MoE-specific redundancy. \subsection{Concepts: MoE Training as a Heterogeneous-State Distributed Program}
\label{sec:bg-concept}

A distributed MoE training job~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard,fedus2022switch,rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe,hwang2023tutel,gale2023megablocks} is a long-running, stateful, multi-process program executing collective communication on tens to thousands of GPUs. Unlike dense Transformers, MoE training maintains a \emph{heterogeneous state} that we partition along its data-parallel (DP), tensor-parallel (TP), pipeline-parallel (PP), and expert-parallel (EP) axes (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Replicated state.} Non-expert layers (attention, embedding, layer normalization) and router weights are replicated across every DP rank that owns the same PP/TP coordinate. At any iteration $t$ at least one healthy peer holds the current step's value.
    \item \emph{Sharded expert state.} Expert weights and their AdamW first/second moments are partitioned across EP ranks. In a $128$-expert, EP$=8$ layout, each rank exclusively owns a fixed subset of $128/8=16$ experts; no peer holds a current in-memory replica.
    \item \emph{Runtime metadata.} The expert directory, the dispatch topology used by all-to-all routing~\cite{he2022fastermoe,zhai2023smartmoe}, and the process-group view are derived from the rank layout and become stale the moment any rank is replaced.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Expert Data Parallelism (EDP).}
The degree to which each expert is replicated across the cluster is captured by the \emph{Expert Data Parallelism} (EDP) degree, defined as $\text{EDP} = W / (\text{TP} \times \text{PP} \times \text{EP})$, where $W$ is the total number of ranks.
When $\text{EDP}=1$, every expert shard exists on exactly one rank; no other rank holds an in-memory copy, so a rank failure can only be repaired from disk (path~C in \S\ref{sec:mechanism}).
When $\text{EDP}>1$, each expert is replicated across multiple data-parallel groups, and in principle a failed expert could be recovered from a surviving peer---the same strategy that works for non-expert parameters (path~P).
In practice, however, the dominant production configuration is $\text{EDP}=1$: DeepSeek-V2 uses 160 routed experts with EP$=$160~\cite{deepseekv2}; DeepSeek-V3 uses 256 routed experts with EP$=$256~\cite{deepseekv3}; Mixtral-8$\times$7B uses 8 experts with EP$=$8~\cite{jiang2024mixtral}; and Qwen3-30B-A3B uses 128 experts with EP$=$8 on 64 GPUs~\cite{qwen3report}.
The reason is economic: expert counts in modern MoE architectures are large (64--256), and the EP dimension is set high to distribute expert memory across ranks; replicating experts ($\text{EDP}>1$) would multiply memory consumption with no compute benefit, since each token activates only $K\!\ll\!E$ experts.
This $\text{EDP}=1$ regime is precisely what makes MoE recovery harder than dense recovery: the sharded expert state has no live peer, forcing any hybrid recovery scheme to fall back to checkpoint I/O for the expert portion of the state.

\subsection{Existing Flow: Checkpoint Restart and the Hidden Risk of Partial Recovery}
\label{sec:bg-flow}

\textbf{Motivating example.} Consider a concrete failure on our 64-GPU Qwen3-30B-A3B job. At step $t\!=\!170$ (checkpoint at $c\!=\!100$), one GPU triggers an uncorrectable ECC error. Standard Megatron-LM recovery halts all 64 ranks, reloads the full 56\,GB distributed checkpoint ($T_{\text{load}}\!=\!36$\,s), and replays 70 lost iterations ($70\!\times\!10.3$\,s $=\!721$\,s)---a total outage of $\sim$757\,s. Yet the failed rank owns only 16 of 128 experts (12.5\% of expert state); the remaining 87.5\% of the model---non-expert layers, routers, and 112 other experts---is still live in memory on healthy peers. MoEGambit exploits this asymmetry: it pulls the 3.2\,GB non-expert state from a DP peer in $<$1\,s, loads only the 16 rank-local experts from the checkpoint shard in 28\,s, and resumes at step $t$ with zero replay---reducing the outage from 757\,s to 29\,s ($26\times$). The remainder of this section formalizes why this fast path is structurally available and what risks it introduces.

The de facto recovery mechanism in production LLM training is \emph{checkpoint restart}~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,dubey2024llama3,jiang2024megascale}: on a failure, the job reloads the most recent globally consistent snapshot at step $c$ and replays the $t-c$ lost iterations. Classical first-order analysis~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher} shows that the optimal checkpoint interval scales as $\tau^{*}\!\approx\!\sqrt{2CM}$, where $C$ is the per-checkpoint cost and $M$ is the mean time between failures; with $M$ collapsing to tens of minutes on 10K-GPU clusters~\cite{dubey2024llama3,jiang2024megascale}, every individual failure costs hundreds of seconds of checkpoint I/O plus hundreds of replayed iterations and, cumulatively, days of wall time per training run.

Restart treats the entire job as a single all-or-nothing state object. Looking at Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}, this is structurally wasteful: a single failed rank invalidates only its EP-sharded expert state, while the DP-replicated non-expert state of every other rank at the same PP/TP coordinate remains current at step $t$. A natural alternative is \emph{hybrid recovery}: synchronize non-expert state from a healthy DP peer at the current step, and load only the rank-local experts from the latest checkpoint shard at step $c$. Hybrid recovery, however, introduces a side effect that restart does not: the recovered rank's expert state is now stale by exactly $\Delta = t-c$ iterations relative to the rest of the model.

This staleness is not a binary defect---restored experts still produce valid forward/backward signals---but it perturbs the auxiliary-loss-driven balancing dynamics that MoE training depends on~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,zoph2022stmoe,komatsuzaki2023sparse}. Two regimes of risk arise:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Per-event staleness.} A single hybrid recovery exposes the experts owned by the failed rank to stale-by-$\Delta$ weights. As long as the affected fraction $|E_{\text{new}}|/N_{\text{expert}}$ stays small, the auxiliary load-balancing loss can reabsorb this staleness within the next few hundred iterations.
    \item \emph{Window-level staleness debt.} Because each rank owns a stable subset of experts, repeated hybrid recoveries---even distributed across different ranks---accumulate stale-by-$\Delta$ exposure across an increasingly large fraction of the expert population. No per-failure or per-rank metric (checkpoint gap, time since last failure, number of past failures) captures this accumulating risk.
\end{enumerate}
A recovery system that admits hybrid repair therefore needs more than a fast mechanism; it needs a \emph{runtime-checkable bound} on both regimes. The MoEGambit policy in §\ref{sec:policy} is exactly that bound.


\label{sec:problem}
\textbf{Input.} A live distributed MoE training job running with $(\text{TP},\text{PP},\text{DP},\text{EP})$ parallelism on $G$ GPUs, using Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,korthikanti2023reducing} as the runtime, and an external fail-stop failure signal published by the existing watchdog / process-manager infrastructure. At step $t$, MoEGambit receives a \emph{failure event} $\langle r, t, c \rangle$ where $r$ is the failed logical rank, $t$ is the training step at which the failure was detected, and $c\!\le\!t$ is the step of the most recent globally consistent checkpoint.

\textbf{Output.} For each failure event, MoEGambit produces (i)~a deterministic \emph{recovery decision} $\pi(t)\in\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$, (ii)~a fully reconstructed and reintegrated replacement rank whose post-recovery state satisfies one of two correctness levels depending on the chosen path:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Restart path:} all state classes are restored from the checkpoint at step $c$; the job replays $t-c$ iterations and resumes with full-restart semantics (identical to standard Megatron-LM recovery).
    \item \emph{Hybrid path:} non-expert state is current at step $t$ (from a healthy peer); expert state is stale by exactly $\Delta=t-c$ iterations (from the per-rank shard). No partial or corrupted optimizer commits are possible (R1), and the accumulated staleness is bounded by $\Phi'(t)\leq\Phi_{\max}$ (R2).
\end{itemize}
Under both paths, the first post-recovery \texttt{optimizer.step()} is a valid AdamW update given the rank's reconstructed state---no gradient is applied to uninitialized parameters. (iii)~A structured recovery log entry recording policy inputs, chosen path, per-segment recovery latency, and the rank's state-machine trace.

\textbf{Design goals.} MoEGambit is engineered against three goals that separate it from generic checkpoint restart: \textbf{(G1)~Recovery-side fast path}---reduce end-to-end recovery latency on a single rank failure well below the full checkpoint-restart cost by exploiting the DP-replication redundancy already present at step $t$, so only rank-unique expert state traverses the disk path; \textbf{(G2)~Recovery-side quality contract}---bound the post-recovery training trajectory, not just resumption, by justifying every decision against a runtime-checkable, cluster-wide quantity whose threshold is calibrated against the failure-free noise floor; \textbf{(G3)~Auditability and structural honesty}---every decision traceable to its inputs and downstream model-quality outcome, with transparent, logged fallback to restart whenever the fast path's structural pre-conditions are violated.

\textbf{Assumptions.} MoEGambit is correct under four assumptions consistent with standard fault-tolerant training settings~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo,jang2023oobleck,wang2023gemini}: \textbf{(A1)} the failure detector~\cite{jiang2024megascale} reports rank failures in a fail-stop model (no Byzantine ranks~\cite{dubey2024llama3}); \textbf{(A2)} at least one checkpoint exists for $c\!\geq\!0$; \textbf{(A3)} for every failed rank $r$, at least one healthy DP peer holds the current-step replica of $r$'s non-expert state at the same PP/TP coordinate; \textbf{(A4)} the failed rank can be replaced or restarted on a hot-spare GPU. (A3) is the structural pre-condition that distinguishes the fast path from restart: when a concurrent burst eliminates every healthy DP peer at the failed rank's PP/TP coordinate, (A3) is violated and MoEGambit falls back to restart (§\ref{sec:policy}). Concurrent multi-rank failures, corrupted checkpoints, silent data corruption~\cite{chowdhery2023palm}, and bit-flips are deferred to §\ref{sec:threats}.

\textbf{Requirements (R1--R3).} The three goals refine into three SE-style contract requirements that the rest of §\ref{sec:moegambit} instantiates, in the vocabulary of self-adaptive and self-healing systems~\cite{kephart2003autonomic,garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}: \textbf{(R1) Safe-point pre-conditions}---the runtime defines an explicit safe repair point and gates optimizer commits against it, since a repair mid-step may commit parameter updates derived from invalid local state; \textbf{(R2) Runtime-checkable specifications}---partial repair is governed by a quantitative, $O(1)$-computable guard that distinguishes single-event risk, cluster-wide accumulated risk, and per-event size, with a data-driven (not hand-tuned) threshold; \textbf{(R3) Traceable observability}---every decision produces a structured record linking policy inputs, the selected path, and downstream model-quality outcome, turning ``did recovery succeed?'' into a falsifiable question. R1--R3 form the spine of §\ref{sec:moegambit}: §\ref{sec:safepoint} instantiates R1; §\ref{sec:policy} instantiates R2 as the expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ and policy function $\pi(t)$; §\ref{sec:obs} instantiates R3. §\ref{sec:mechanism}--§\ref{sec:twophase} are how MoEGambit repairs state \emph{within} R2's contract; \emph{what is guaranteed} stays separate from \emph{how it is achieved} so the contract is auditable independently of the implementation~\cite{garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}.

\section{MoEGambit}
\label{sec:moegambit}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:arch}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{moegambit_runtime_architecture.pdf}
\caption{MoEGambit runtime architecture, numbered by control-flow order. \emph{(1)}~Sparse MoE distributed training job (3 DP $\times$ 4 pipeline stages); a failed rank $r$ is detected. \emph{(2)}~Failure detection and safe-point repair (R1): the controller marks $r$ as \texttt{RECOVERING}, blocks optimizer commits, and discards the current iteration. \emph{(3)}~Staleness-density guarded policy (R2) evaluates $\Phi'(t)$ and returns $\pi(t)\!\in\!\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$. \emph{(4a)}~Hybrid recovery (fast path): path~P pulls non-expert state from a healthy peer at step $t$; path~C loads rank-$r$'s experts from the checkpoint shard at step $c$; the two-phase protocol resumes training during optimizer restoration; the guarded state machine advances $r'$ through \texttt{RECOVERING}$\to$\texttt{REPAIRED}$\to$\texttt{BARRIER}$\to$\texttt{HEALTHY}. \emph{(4b)}~Checkpoint restart (fallback): reloads the full checkpoint and replays $t{-}c$ iterations. \emph{(5)}~Observability and structured logs (R3): every decision is auditable via policy inputs, per-segment latencies, state-machine trace, and quality metrics.}
\label{fig:moegambit-overview}

MoEGambit is a runtime recovery layer that intercepts rank-failure events and reconstitutes the replacement rank's state from the freshest available source for each state class, without rolling back the rest of the job. Its design separates \emph{what is guaranteed} (the recovery contract, R1--R3) from \emph{how state is repaired} (the hybrid restoration mechanism and two-phase protocol), so the contract remains auditable independently of the implementation.

The framework comprises five components executed in sequence on each failure event (Fig.~\ref{fig:moegambit-overview}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Failure detection.} An external watchdog emits a failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$ identifying the failed rank, the current training step, and the latest checkpoint step.
    \item \emph{Safe-point repair controller} (§\ref{sec:safepoint}, R1). Installs an optimizer commit guard that prevents any rank from applying gradients computed during the recovery window, ensuring no partial or corrupted updates enter the model state.
    \item \emph{Staleness-density guarded policy} (§\ref{sec:policy}, R2). Evaluates the $O(1)$ guard $\Phi'(t)$ and returns a deterministic decision $\pi(t)\!\in\!\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$. If the projected staleness density exceeds the empirical cliff threshold, MoEGambit falls back to checkpoint restart.
    \item \emph{MoE-aware hybrid restoration and two-phase protocol} (§\ref{sec:mechanism}--§\ref{sec:twophase}). On the hybrid path, non-expert state is pulled from a healthy DP peer at step $t$ (path~P) while rank-local experts load from the per-rank checkpoint shard at step $c$ (path~C). The two-phase protocol further overlaps optimizer-state restoration with resumed forward/backward computation, bounding time-to-resume by a single weight transfer.
    \item \emph{Reintegration state machine and structured logs} (§\ref{sec:obs}, R3). Advances the replacement rank through a guarded four-state machine to \texttt{HEALTHY} and emits a structured record linking policy inputs, per-segment latencies, and the state-machine trace for post-hoc auditability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Safe-Point Repair Controller (R1)}
\label{sec:safepoint}

R1 fixes a single program point at which repair may run, with a single invariant: no optimizer commit may use gradients computed under \texttt{RECOVERING}. On a failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$, the controller atomically (i)~marks $r$ and its experts as \texttt{RECOVERING}, (ii)~installs an optimizer commit guard---a pre-condition check inserted immediately before AdamW's parameter update---on every rank for the current iteration, and (iii)~marks the iteration as \texttt{DISCARD}. Any rank reaching its \texttt{optimizer.step()} with the iteration in \texttt{DISCARD} skips the update; in-flight gradients are dropped. Training resumes at the next iteration via the hybrid path (§\ref{sec:mechanism}) or restart. The controller emits $\langle\text{safe-point id},\text{status}\rangle$ with status $\in\{\textsc{Committed},\textsc{Discarded}\}$ to the structured log.

\subsection{Staleness-Density Guarded Policy (R2)}
\label{sec:policy}

R2 is an $O(1)$ guard $\Phi'(t)$ and a deterministic decision $\pi(t)\!\in\!\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$ over the failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$, the running aggregate $S(t)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_W(t)}|E_h|\Delta_h$ on the window $[t\!-\!W,t)$, the predicate $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$, and thresholds $(\Delta_{\min},\Delta_{\max},\Phi_{\max})$ (provenance in §\ref{sec:rq1}--§\ref{sec:eval}). $S(t)$ is maintained as a single scalar with $O(1)$ updates; the projected density assuming hybrid is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:phi-prime}
    \Phi'(t) \;=\; \frac{S(t) \,+\, |E_{\text{new}}|\, \Delta}{N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W},
\end{equation}
where the denominator $N_{\text{expert}}\!\cdot\!W$ normalizes the maximum debt to 1, so $\Phi'(t)\!\in\![0,1]$. The running aggregate $S(t)$ is maintained as a single scalar: on each hybrid event $h$ entering the window, add $|E_h|\Delta_h$; on each event leaving the window (older than $W$ iterations), subtract the same product---yielding $O(1)$ per-iteration bookkeeping. When multiple ranks fail simultaneously, each failed rank contributes its own $|E_h|\Delta_h$ term to $S(t)$ before the policy is evaluated for the next rank.

The decision is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:policy}
    \pi(t) \;=\;
    \begin{cases}
        \textsc{Hybrid}, & \text{if } \textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t) \;\wedge\; \\
                         & \quad \Delta_{\min} \leq \Delta \leq \Delta_{\max} \;\wedge\; \\
                         & \quad \Phi'(t) \leq \Phi_{\max}, \\[2pt]
        \textsc{Restart}, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:policy} encodes the same logic with the guard order recorded in the log on a fallback. The output $\langle\pi(t),\text{reason}\rangle$ together with $(\Delta,|E_{\text{new}}|,S(t),\Phi'(t))$ is logged per event (§\ref{sec:obs}). Every threshold traces to a measurable source: $\Delta_{\min}$ from the $T_{\text{load}}\!:\!T_{\text{hybrid}}\!:\!T_{\text{iter}}$ ratio (§\ref{sec:rq1}), $\Delta_{\max}$ from the save interval bounded by Young/Daly~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}, $\Phi_{\max}$ from the §\ref{sec:eval} cliff sweep, $\textsc{PeerAvail}$ from a binary check on the live process group.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Staleness-density guarded recovery decision $\pi(t)$.}
\label{alg:policy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$; window aggregate $S(t)$; thresholds $\Delta_{\min},\Delta_{\max},\Phi_{\max}$; predicate $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$.
\Ensure decision $\pi(t)\in\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$; reason string.
\State $\Delta \gets t - c$
\If{$\neg\,\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``PeerAvail=false''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\If{$\Delta < \Delta_{\min}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``}\Delta\!<\!\Delta_{\min}\text{ (restart is cheaper)''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\If{$\Delta > \Delta_{\max}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``per-event staleness cap''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\State $|E_{\text{new}}| \gets$ number of experts owned by $r$
\State $\Phi'(t) \gets \big(S(t) + |E_{\text{new}}|\cdot\Delta\big)\,/\,\big(N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W\big)$
\If{$\Phi'(t) > \Phi_{\max}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``window-level staleness cap''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\langle\textsc{Hybrid},\ \text{``all guards passed''}\rangle$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{MoE-Aware Hybrid State Restoration}
\label{sec:mechanism}

On $\pi(t)=\textsc{Hybrid}$, the replacement rank reconstitutes its state by reading each state class from its freshest available source (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}), so I/O scales with the rank-unique expert volume rather than the full distributed checkpoint. Two paths run concurrently. \emph{Path~P (peer pull, replicated state):} synchronize current-step non-expert parameters (and DP-replicated optimizer state) from a healthy peer at the same PP/TP coordinate via a single P2P broadcast over NCCL/Gloo, completing in tens of milliseconds. \emph{Path~C (shard read, sharded expert state):} load rank-$r$'s experts from the per-rank shard at step $c$ with no collectives. The result is a rank whose state is \emph{mixed by construction}: non-expert state reflects step $t$ while experts and their optimizer state are stale by exactly $\Delta$---the quantity bounded by $\Phi'(t)$ in (\ref{eq:phi-prime}) (Fig.~\ref{fig:moegambit-overview}, step 4a).

\subsection{Two-Phase Recovery Protocol}
\label{sec:twophase}

The two-phase protocol reduces time-to-resume below time-to-full-recovery by overlapping path~C's optimizer-state read with the replacement rank's resumed forward/backward, while forbidding optimizer commits on still-uninitialized expert slots. \emph{Phase~A (weights first):} restore non-expert state from peers (path~P) and rank-$r$'s expert weights from the checkpoint shard (path~C, weights half); the repaired rank rejoins under an \emph{update barrier} on the affected experts---forward/backward proceeds and contributes to the global loss, but the resulting gradients are buffered, not applied. \emph{Phase~B (optimizer later):} in parallel with Phase~A, restore DP-replicated non-expert moments from the peer and rank-$r$'s expert moments from the shard; on completion the barrier releases and the buffered gradients are applied via a single AdamW step. The barrier reuses the pre-\texttt{step()} hook of §\ref{sec:safepoint}, so the R1 invariant holds throughout the overlap. The structured log records $T_{\text{TTR}}$ (time-to-resume) and $T_{\text{TTFR}}$ (time-to-full-recovery); $T_{\text{TTFR}}-T_{\text{TTR}}$ is the saving over single-phase restart. The protocol is orthogonal to hybrid restore (it controls \emph{when} optimizer state attaches, not \emph{where} bytes come from), which is why §\ref{sec:rq1}'s $2{\times}2$ factorial finds the two main effects additive (Fig.~\ref{fig:moegambit-overview}, step 4a).

\subsection{Reintegration State Machine and Structured Logs (R3)}
\label{sec:obs}

R3 requires that every recovery decision and its downstream model-quality outcome be traceable. We realize R3 through two mechanisms: a guarded state machine that enforces the reintegration protocol, and a structured log that records the inputs and outputs of each recovery event.

\textbf{Guarded state machine.} Each replacement rank advances through a four-state linear protocol:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:state-machine}
\texttt{RECOVERING} \xrightarrow{g_1} \texttt{REPAIRED} \xrightarrow{g_2} \texttt{BARRIER} \xrightarrow{g_3} \texttt{HEALTHY},
\end{equation}
where each transition is gated by a guard predicate:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $g_1$: state restoration complete---all state classes (non-expert via path~P, experts via path~C) have been loaded and verified against their source checksums.
    \item $g_2$: dispatch topology re-derived---the expert directory and all-to-all routing tables have been recomputed to reflect the replacement rank's position in the EP layout.
    \item $g_3$: optimizer state attached and update barrier released---Phase~B of the two-phase protocol (\S\ref{sec:twophase}) has completed, and the buffered gradients have been applied.
\end{itemize}
Because every transition requires its guard to hold, premature reintegration---e.g., routing tokens to an expert whose optimizer state has not yet been restored---is ruled out by construction.

\textbf{Structured recovery log.} On each recovery event, the state machine emits a single structured record containing: (i)~policy inputs ($\Delta$, $|E_{\text{new}}|$, $S(t)$, $\Phi'(t)$), (ii)~the decision $\pi(t)$ and its reason string from Algorithm~\ref{alg:policy}, (iii)~per-segment latencies ($T_{\text{TTR}}$, $T_{\text{TTFR}}$, path~P and path~C durations), and (iv)~the state-machine trace (timestamps of each guard evaluation and transition). This record lets every recovery path trace back to the guards in (\ref{eq:policy}) and supports post-hoc correlation with training-quality metrics under the paired-run methodology of \S\ref{sec:eval}~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}. The runtime overhead of $\Phi'(t)$ accounting and log emission is $O(1)$ per iteration and empirically negligible (\S\ref{sec:rq4}).

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

We implement MoEGambit as a 2{,}847-line Python patch atop Megatron-LM (commit \texttt{core\_r0.9.0}), organized into four modules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Safe-point controller} (412 LoC): hooks into \texttt{MegatronOptimizer.step()} to install the commit guard and discard flag; reuses the existing gradient-accumulation barrier with no additional NCCL calls.
    \item \emph{Policy engine} (198 LoC): maintains the $O(1)$ sliding-window aggregate $S(t)$ and evaluates Algorithm~\ref{alg:policy}; emits the structured log record via Python \texttt{logging} with JSON formatting.
    \item \emph{Hybrid restore} (1{,}483 LoC): implements path~P via \texttt{torch.distributed.broadcast} on the existing DP process group, and path~C via rank-local \texttt{torch.load} on the per-rank shard; handles ZeRO-1 optimizer-state partitioning by pulling the DP-replicated master weights and moments from the peer.
    \item \emph{Reintegration state machine} (754 LoC): manages the four-state protocol of (\ref{eq:state-machine}), re-derives the expert directory and all-to-all dispatch topology after rank replacement, and coordinates the Phase~B barrier release.
\end{itemize}
The patch requires no changes to Megatron-LM's forward/backward kernels, parallelism configuration, or checkpoint-save path. Process-group reconstruction after rank replacement reuses Megatron-LM's existing \texttt{initialize\_model\_parallel} with the replacement rank's new address; the only additional collective is a single \texttt{barrier()} to synchronize the ``safe-point committed'' signal. All experiments use NCCL 2.20 and PyTorch 2.3.

\textbf{Low-invasiveness deployment.} MoEGambit is activated or deactivated by a single command-line flag (\texttt{-{}-enable-moegambit}) passed to the standard \texttt{torchrun} launcher; no source-code modification, custom launcher, or separate daemon process is required. When the flag is absent, the training script follows the unmodified Megatron-LM code path with zero overhead. This design allows operators to adopt MoEGambit incrementally---enabling it on a per-job basis without affecting other jobs sharing the same codebase or cluster scheduler.

\textbf{Artifact availability.} The implementation, fault-injection scripts, paired-run harness, and all configuration files used in \S\ref{sec:eval} will be released as an open-source artifact upon acceptance.



\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

This section evaluates whether MoEGambit's recovery contract and mechanism achieve their design goals. Our evaluation is organized around six research questions, each linked to a specific contract requirement or design goal:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (G1: Recovery-side fast path).} How much recovery cost does MoEGambit reduce compared with checkpoint restart, and how do its two mechanisms---hybrid restore and two-phase protocol---compose?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (G2: Quality contract).} Does hybrid recovery preserve training quality and stability within the failure-free noise floor?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (R2: Staleness specification).} How does MoEGambit behave under repeated and burst failures, and does the $\Phi'(t)\!\leq\!\Phi_{\max}$ guard correctly identify the quality cliff?
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (Deployability).} What is the runtime overhead of MoEGambit's contract-monitoring hooks during failure-free training?
    \item \textbf{RQ5 (Scalability).} Does the recovery-cost advantage hold as cluster size scales from 64 to 128 GPUs?
    \item \textbf{RQ6 (Generalization).} Does MoEGambit generalize across MoE architectures with different expert granularities and shared-expert structures?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RQ3: Multiple Failures and Expert-Weighted Staleness Density}
\label{sec:rq3}

RQ3 validates the R2 specification: does the $\Phi'(t)\!\leq\!\Phi_{\max}$ guard correctly identify the quality cliff under multi-rank burst failures?

To answer RQ3, we evaluate MoEGambit under multi-rank burst failures and validate the cliff-edge $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ from §\ref{sec:policy}. Using \texttt{find\_multi\_fault.sh} we sweep $|F|\!\in\!\{8,16,24\}$ simultaneous rank failures across $\Delta\!\in\!\{50,100,150,200\}$ on the same $3\!\times\!4$ grid as Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}, covering both \emph{distributed} (failures on distinct ranks) and \emph{concentrated} (repeated failures on one rank) patterns---a gap-only or always-hybrid policy would treat them alike, whereas $\Phi'(t)$ separates them through $|E_{\text{new}}|$.

Result: under the default policy MoEGambit applies hybrid recovery on the seven $\Phi'(t)\!\leq\!10^{-2}$ cells (deviations $\leq 0.71\sigma_{\text{base}}$, all inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band) and redirects the five $\Phi'(t)\!>\!10^{-2}$ cells to checkpoint restart, where post-restart loss returns inside the $\pm 1\sigma$ band. With the $\Phi_{\max}$ guard disabled, those same five cells reproduce the $1.21$--$1.98\sigma_{\text{base}}$ deviations of Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}, confirming that the fallback---not hybrid itself---carries the quality result above the cliff and that an expert-weighted, window-level density captures risk that global gap-only policies miss.

\subsection{RQ4: Failure-Free Overhead}
\label{sec:rq4}

We compare 1{,}000 failure-free iterations with and without MoEGambit's runtime hooks. The overhead is within measurement noise ($+0.1\%$ on mean step time, $p\!<\!0.05$ not reached on a paired $t$-test): the $\Phi'(t)$ scalar update and log-emission hook add $<6$\,\textmu s per iteration---four orders of magnitude below $T_{\text{iter}}$.

\subsection{RQ5: Scalability}\label{sec:rq6}

We rerun the single-failure injection of \S\ref{sec:rq1} at 64 and 128 GPUs (TP$=$1, PP$=$8, EP$\in\{$8,16$\}$; 10 seeds each). Checkpoint restart grows with global checkpoint size ($36.4$\,s $\to 47.2$\,s), while MoEGambit grows more slowly ($28.9$\,s $\to 33.7$\,s) because the hybrid path's I/O scales with the per-rank shard rather than the global checkpoint. The Restart/MoEGambit ratio therefore widens from $1.26\times$ at 64~GPUs to $\mathbf{1.40\times}$ at 128~GPUs (Fig.~\ref{fig:scalability}). Post-recovery throughput returns to within $\pm 1\%$ of the failure-free baseline within 50 steps at both scales.

\subsection{RQ6: Cross-Architecture Generalization}\label{sec:rq7}

To assess generalization beyond Qwen3-30B-A3B, we rerun the core single-failure and multi-rank burst experiments on \textbf{DeepSeek-V2-Lite}~\cite{deepseekv2} (15.7B total / 2.4B activated, 64 routed experts, 2 shared experts, top-6), which simultaneously varies expert granularity, shared-expert structure, and top-$k$. Shared experts are DP-replicated and recovered through path~P; only the 64 routed experts traverse path~C. Policy parameters are inherited from Table~\ref{tab:policy} with $N_{\text{expert}}\!=\!64$.

\textbf{Result.} On the single-failure benchmark, checkpoint restart (load-checkpoint) takes $26.09$\,s while MoEGambit recovers in $11.73$\,s---a $55.0\%$ reduction ($2.22\times$ speedup), substantially larger than the $20.6\%$ on Qwen3-30B-A3B because DeepSeek-V2-Lite's smaller per-expert shard (64 vs.\ 128 experts, each with a narrower FFN) makes path~C faster while path~P cost stays comparable. The multi-rank burst sweep with $|F|\!\in\!\{4,8,12\}$ and $\Delta\!\in\!\{50,100,150,200\}$ shows the same cliff behavior at $\Phi'(t)\!=\!10^{-2}$: all cells below the threshold stay within the $\pm 1\sigma$ band, while cells above exhibit $1.1$--$1.7\,\sigma_{\text{base}}$ deviations. Routing shared experts on path~C rather than path~P degrades the recovery advantage by $3.2$~pp, confirming that correct state-class assignment matters for the mechanism's effectiveness.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

We organize threats following the standard classification~\cite{wohlin2012ese}.

\textbf{Internal validity.} (i)~The MoC-System comparison relies on a byte-identical accuracy overlay rather than the original implementation (which is not publicly available). \emph{Mitigation:} we use the most favorable published numbers ($K_{\text{pec}}=16$, $N=128$, PLT~$\approx 3.75\%$) as a conservative upper bound on MoC-System's performance; the overlay reproduces the post-recovery model state exactly, but we do \emph{not} use it to measure MoC-System's save- or storage-side overheads. We will integrate the original implementation if it becomes available. (ii)~The $36.9\times$ end-to-end speedup includes replay cost ($\sim$100 iterations at $T_{\text{iter}}=10.31$\,s). \emph{Mitigation:} we report both the raw recovery-latency improvement ($1.26\times$) and the end-to-end figure separately throughout the paper so readers can distinguish mechanism from replay savings. (iii)~The $\pm 1\sigma$ quality band is derived from ten independent NoFault seeds. \emph{Mitigation:} ten seeds is standard practice in SE experimentation~\cite{arcuri2014hitchhiker}; a larger seed pool would tighten the estimate but would not change the pass/fail classification of any cell in Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}.

\textbf{External validity.} (i)~End-to-end results are reported on two Megatron-LM MoE configurations (Qwen3-30B-A3B and DeepSeek-V2-Lite) with H20-3e interconnect. \emph{Mitigation:} RQ6 varies expert granularity, shared-expert structure, and top-$k$ simultaneously; the recovery-latency advantage holds across both architectures ($20.6\%$ on Qwen3, $55.0\%$ on DeepSeek-V2-Lite). Other parallelism layouts and interconnects may shift the magnitudes. (ii)~We restrict to fail-stop GPU/rank failures detected by the existing watchdog; silent data corruption, slow-degrading hardware, and network partitions are out of scope. \emph{Mitigation:} the fail-stop model is standard in fault-tolerant training~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo,jang2023oobleck,wang2023gemini}; extending to Byzantine faults is noted as future work. (iii)~The $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ threshold is calibrated on Qwen3-30B-A3B. \emph{Mitigation:} the same cliff location holds on DeepSeek-V2-Lite (RQ6), suggesting the threshold generalizes across MoE architectures with similar auxiliary-loss coefficients; we recommend re-calibration for substantially different configurations.

\textbf{Construct validity.} (i)~Hybrid recovery is predicated on $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$ (A3 in \S\ref{sec:background}); when a concurrent burst eliminates every DP peer at the failed PP/TP coordinate, MoEGambit transparently degrades to restart and offers no speedup. \emph{Mitigation:} the fallback is automatic and logged; closing this regime requires an in-memory replica plane~\cite{wang2023gemini} or save-side PEC~\cite{cai2024moc}. (ii)~We measure recovery quality via validation loss, perplexity, and eight zero-shot downstream tasks; other quality proxies (e.g., generation quality, instruction-following accuracy) are not evaluated. \emph{Mitigation:} the eight tasks span reasoning, commonsense, and reading comprehension; we release all checkpoints so additional evaluations can be performed. (iii)~The paired-run methodology controls for seed noise but not for hardware-level non-determinism in NCCL collectives. \emph{Mitigation:} residual non-determinism is bounded by the $\pm 1\sigma$ band width ($0.024$ on eval loss), which is small relative to the cliff deviations ($1.21$--$1.98\sigma$).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Self-adaptive and self-healing systems.} The MAPE-K reference model~\cite{kephart2003autonomic} and its architectural instantiations~\cite{garlan2004rainbow} prescribe that runtime repair operate through an explicit specification, an invariant-protecting execute stage, and an auditable trace---principles codified in two SE research roadmaps~\cite{cheng2009software,weyns2019softwareengineering}. Salehie and Tahvildari's survey~\cite{salehie2009selfadaptive} further identifies \emph{what}, \emph{when}, \emph{where}, and \emph{how} as the four dimensions of self-adaptation. MoEGambit instantiates all four for the distributed MoE training data plane: \emph{what} to adapt is the recovery path (hybrid vs.\ restart); \emph{when} is governed by the $\Phi'(t)\!\leq\!\Phi_{\max}$ guard; \emph{where} is the failed rank's state-class boundary (DP-replicated vs.\ EP-sharded); and \emph{how} is the hybrid restoration mechanism. The key difference from classical self-adaptive systems is that MoEGambit's specification $\Phi'(t)$ bounds a \emph{cumulative quality risk over a sliding window} rather than a point-in-time SLA violation, and the ``execute'' stage produces a state correct-by-construction for the next optimizer step rather than merely available. MoEGambit also contributes a recovery-quality metric---expert-weighted staleness density---absent from the classical self-adaptive vocabulary.

\textbf{Save-side checkpointing.} One systems line attacks the per-checkpoint cost $C$ in Young/Daly's $\tau^{*}\!\approx\!\sqrt{2CM}$~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}: CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq} profiles I/O bandwidth to schedule checkpoints at minimal throughput loss; DeepFreeze~\cite{nicolae2020deepfreeze} pipelines incremental snapshots with training; Check-N-Run~\cite{eisenman2022check} exploits model sparsity to compress checkpoint writes; Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini} and REFT~\cite{wang2024reft} replace NVMe writes with in-memory replicas across nodes, achieving sub-second save latency; and ByteCheckpoint~\cite{wan2024bytecheckpoint} provides a unified API that automatically reshards checkpoints across different parallelism configurations. All of these reduce $C$ but leave the recovery path unchanged: on failure, the job still reloads the full global snapshot and replays lost iterations. MoEGambit is orthogonal---it changes neither $C$ nor the save protocol, but instead reduces the recovery-side cost by exploiting the heterogeneous state structure of MoE training.

\textbf{Topology adaptation.} A second line avoids reloading any snapshot by reconfiguring the job's parallelism layout around surviving ranks: Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo} maintains redundant pipeline stages; Oobleck~\cite{jang2023oobleck} pre-computes pipeline templates; ReCycle~\cite{gandhi2024recycle} adapts pipeline depth at runtime; Varuna~\cite{athlur2022varuna} and Parcae~\cite{duan2024parcae} re-partition data parallelism; Litz~\cite{qiao2019litz} and Or et al.~\cite{or2020resource} provide elastic scaling primitives. These systems assume \emph{ranks are interchangeable}---true for dense DP replicas but false for MoE, where an EP-sharded expert subset has no in-memory peer. MoEGambit fills this blind spot by repairing the failed rank in place.

\textbf{Peer-based recovery for dense models.} FlashRecovery~\cite{zhang2025flashrecovery} exploits the DP-replica redundancy inherent in dense training to achieve checkpoint-free single-step recovery: on a rank failure, the replacement pulls the full model and optimizer state from a healthy DP peer, losing at most one training step. This validates the core premise that peer-held replicas can replace disk-based checkpoint loads. MoEGambit generalizes this insight to MoE training, where only the DP-replicated non-expert state can be peer-recovered (path~P); the EP-sharded expert state has no in-memory peer and must still be loaded from a checkpoint shard (path~C), introducing bounded staleness that FlashRecovery's dense setting does not encounter. MoEGambit further contributes a runtime-checkable quality contract ($\Phi'(t)\!\leq\!\Phi_{\max}$) absent from FlashRecovery's design.

\textbf{MoE systems and the freshness blind spot.} GShard~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard}, Switch~\cite{fedus2022switch}, DeepSpeed-MoE~\cite{rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe}, Tutel~\cite{hwang2023tutel}, FasterMoE~\cite{he2022fastermoe}, SmartMoE~\cite{zhai2023smartmoe}, MegaBlocks~\cite{gale2023megablocks}, and open-model deployments~\cite{jiang2024mixtral,dai2024deepseekmoe,deepseekv3,qwen3report} optimize routing, dispatch, and all-to-all bandwidth, while ST-MoE~\cite{zoph2022stmoe}, sparse-upcycling~\cite{komatsuzaki2023sparse}, and V-MoE~\cite{riquelme2021vmoe} push the architecture itself. Across this family, ``every expert at step $t$ is current'' is an \emph{implicit} invariant that no system monitors or enforces at runtime; MoEGambit differs by lifting it into an $O(1)$-computable runtime monitor $\Phi'(t)$ validated against an empirical cliff (\S\ref{sec:eval}).

\textbf{MoC-System.} The only previously published MoE-specific fault-tolerance system we are aware of is MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc} (ASPLOS'25), whose Partial Experts Checkpointing (PEC) writes only $K_{\text{pec}}$ of $N$ experts per save round, rotating the subset so every expert is eventually checkpointed. PEC is a save-side optimization: it reduces $C$ but does not change the recovery path. MoEGambit targets the complementary recovery side: it does not reduce $C$ but eliminates replay and reduces load cost by $20.6\%$. On a (\emph{save-side vs.\ recovery-side}) $\times$ (\emph{contract or not}) grid, MoEGambit occupies the previously empty recovery-side / contract quadrant. The two are orthogonal and composable.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Lessons.} Three cross-cutting lessons emerge from \S\ref{sec:rq1}--\S\ref{sec:rq6}, each grounded in software engineering principles. (i)~\emph{Recovery in MoE training must be state-aware.} Triggering a collective $\textsc{LoadCkpt}$ over a uniform global state object leaks $20$--$55\%$ of recovery wall time relative to a baseline that distinguishes DP-replicated state (peer-recoverable in ms) from rank-unique expert state (disk-only); the same asymmetry explains why our advantage widens from $1.26\times$ to $1.40\times$ from 64 to 128 GPUs (RQ5) and reaches $2.22\times$ on DeepSeek-V2-Lite (RQ6). (ii)~\emph{A runtime-checkable quality contract is necessary, not optional}---without $\Phi'(t)$ an always-hybrid system silently drifts on bursty traces (RQ3), echoing the self-adaptive-systems principle that the fast path's own safety must be a data-driven judgement~\cite{cheng2009software,salehie2009selfadaptive,weyns2019softwareengineering}. (iii)~\emph{Fast path and auditability must be co-designed}: a hybrid restore mid-step requires a safe point (R1), and a $\Phi'(t)$-conditioned policy is only falsifiable if its inputs and the loss trajectory are logged at the same key (R3).

\textbf{Implications for practice.} First, eliminating replay cost on a cluster where failures arrive every 45\,minutes~\cite{dubey2024llama3} can save days of GPU time over a multi-week run. Second, the $\Phi'(t)$ guard and structured logs shift the operational model from ``restart and hope'' to ``recover and verify'': operators set a single threshold ($\Phi_{\max}$) and let the system decide per-event whether hybrid recovery is safe, with every decision auditable after the fact.

\textbf{Limitations.} We identify four limitations of the current design. (i)~MoEGambit requires at least one healthy DP peer at the failed rank's PP/TP coordinate (A3); when a correlated failure (e.g., a switch failure) eliminates all peers simultaneously, the system falls back to checkpoint restart with no speedup. (ii)~The $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ threshold is calibrated on Qwen3-30B-A3B; models with different expert counts, routing strategies, or auxiliary-loss coefficients may exhibit a different cliff location, requiring re-calibration. (iii)~The current implementation assumes a static EP layout; dynamic expert placement or expert migration across ranks~\cite{he2022fastermoe} would require extending the path-P/path-C boundary at runtime. (iv)~Our evaluation uses injected fail-stop failures; real production failures include slow-degrading hardware, silent data corruption~\cite{chowdhery2023palm}, and partial network partitions, which the current fault model does not cover.

\textbf{Calibration cost of $\Phi_{\max}$.} The $3\!\times\!4$ burst sweep (Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}) that calibrates $\Phi_{\max}$ requires 12 paired runs of $\sim$2{,}000 iterations each, totaling $\sim$68 GPU-hours on our 64-GPU cluster---less than 3\% of a single full training run. Because the cliff is determined by the auxiliary-loss coefficient and expert count rather than by model weights, calibration can be performed once during the early warmup phase and reused for the remainder of training. An online variant that tracks the running $\sigma_{\text{base}}$ from periodic eval and adjusts $\Phi_{\max}$ adaptively is a promising direction for eliminating even this one-time cost (see Future Work).

\textbf{Future work.} (i)~Closing the no-peer fallback via an in-memory replica plane~\cite{wang2023gemini} or save-side PEC~\cite{cai2024moc}; (ii)~extending R1 to cover silent data corruption / slow degradation; (iii)~online calibration of $\Phi_{\max}$ against a streaming noise-floor estimate; (iv)~porting the R1--R3 contract to DeepSpeed-MoE~\cite{rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe} and expert-replication variants~\cite{he2022fastermoe}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We argued that runtime failure recovery in distributed MoE training should be engineered as a software reliability artifact rather than as a checkpoint-optimization heuristic. MoEGambit realizes this view inside Megatron-LM as three explicit SE artifacts---safe-point repair (R1), expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ as a runtime-checkable specification (R2), and structured recovery logs (R3)---cleanly separating the recovery \emph{contract} from the recovery \emph{mechanism}. On Qwen3-30B-A3B (128 experts, 64 H20 GPUs), MoE-aware hybrid restore and the two-phase protocol compose additively for a $20.6\%$ raw recovery-latency reduction and a $36.9\times$ end-to-end speedup (including eliminated replay); on DeepSeek-V2-Lite (64 experts, 64 GPUs) the reduction reaches $55.0\%$ ($2.22\times$). Post-recovery quality stays within the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band on every cell with $\Phi'(t)\leq 10^{-2}$, and the system correctly falls back to restart beyond the empirical cliff. The broader methodological claim is that \emph{recovery must be specified against the post-recovery training trajectory, not merely against resumption}---making the decision auditable, the trajectory falsifiable, and the failure surface testable. We believe this contract-first perspective applies beyond MoE to any distributed training system whose state is heterogeneous enough to admit partial recovery.
