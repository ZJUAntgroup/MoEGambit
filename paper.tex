\begin{abstract}
Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models now power frontier large language model training, where multi-week, multi-thousand-GPU jobs make GPU failures routine. However, production recovery still relies on coarse-grained checkpoint restart that reloads global state and replays hundreds of iterations on every failure, and the only existing MoE-aware system, MoC-System, optimizes save-side cost while leaving recovery itself unchanged. We present \textbf{MoEGambit}, a recovery-side fault-tolerance framework integrated into Megatron-LM that exploits a structural asymmetry of MoE training: dense parameters and routers are data-parallel-replicated, so a failed rank can synchronize them from a healthy peer at memory-to-memory speed and only its rank-local experts need to load from disk. A safe-point repair protocol prevents partial optimizer commits, and an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ guards accumulated quality risk over repeated hybrid recoveries. On Qwen3-30B-A3B with 64 H20 GPUs, MoEGambit achieves a $36.9\times$ end-to-end recovery speedup over MoC-System (whose restart-style recovery still pays the $\sim$100-iteration replay cost) and a $1.26\times$ reduction in raw recovery latency, while keeping validation loss, perplexity, and expert load balance within the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become a dominant architecture for frontier large language models, powering systems such as Mixtral~\cite{jiang2024mixtral}, DeepSeek-MoE~\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe}, and Qwen3-MoE~\cite{qwen3report}, because they decouple model capacity from per-token compute by routing each input through only a small subset of experts. Training these models at scale, however, is a long-running, multi-week, multi-thousand-GPU software process in which failures are routine rather than exceptional. Meta's Llama-3 training trace reports a failure roughly every 45\,minutes across a 16K-GPU cluster~\cite{dubey2024llama3}; MegaScale documents hundreds of distinct failure modes during a 10K-GPU production deployment~\cite{jiang2024megascale}; the OPT-175B logbook records weeks of human-in-the-loop intervention~\cite{zhang2022opt}. Because the dominant recovery mechanism in production is \emph{checkpoint restart}---reload the most recent globally consistent snapshot and replay the lost iterations---each failure event on a large MoE job typically consumes hundreds of seconds of checkpoint I/O plus hundreds of replayed iterations, costing thousands of GPU-seconds per event and, cumulatively, days of wall time over a training run.

A substantial body of fault-tolerant training systems has been developed to reduce this cost, but almost all of it targets dense models. CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq}, DeepFreeze~\cite{nicolae2020deepfreeze}, and Check-N-Run~\cite{eisenman2022check} shrink per-checkpoint cost via asynchronous and incremental writes; Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini} replaces NVMe writes with in-memory replicas; Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo} hides checkpointing behind redundancy on preemptible instances; Oobleck~\cite{jang2023oobleck} and ReCycle~\cite{gandhi2024recycle} avoid rolling back to any saved snapshot at all by reconfiguring pipeline schedules. None of these systems addresses MoE-specific cost asymmetries: the bulk of a MoE checkpoint is per-expert optimizer state, and the dominant Megatron-LM distributed checkpoint format (\texttt{torch\_dist}) requires every rank to participate in the load through a collective \texttt{all\_gather\_object}, which forbids single-rank selective restoration by construction. The only MoE-specific fault-tolerance system we are aware of, MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc}, optimizes \emph{save-side} cost via Partial Experts Checkpointing but at recovery time still falls back to full global checkpoint restart---paying the full load + replay bill on every failure and leaving the recovery critical path essentially unchanged.

The gap is therefore a \emph{recovery-side} fault-tolerance framework structured around the heterogeneous state of MoE training---one that repairs a failed rank without rolling back the whole job, and does so fast, quality-preserving, and stable across repeated failures. \textbf{Our insight is that distributed MoE training already carries enough cross-rank redundancy to bypass disk-based recovery for most of its state.} Concretely, dense parameters and routers are \emph{replicated} across the data-parallel (DP) dimension, while only expert parameters and their optimizer state are EP-sharded. When a rank fails, the replacement does not need to read dense state from disk at all---it can synchronize the current-step dense and router parameters from a healthy DP peer, and only the rank-local expert weights and expert optimizer state need to load from the latest checkpoint shard. We present \textbf{MoEGambit}\footnote{The name reflects the system's core design philosophy: like a chess gambit, MoEGambit deliberately tolerates a bounded amount of expert staleness ($\Delta$ steps, bounded by $\Delta_{\max}$ and $\Phi_{\max}$) in order to skip global checkpoint restart and resume training at memory-to-memory speed, trading a small, quantified positional sacrifice for a decisive recovery-time advantage. The ``MoE'' prefix names the domain; ``Gambit'' names the move.}, the first such framework, integrated into Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron}. A weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase protocol further overlaps optimizer-state restoration with the replacement rank's resumed forward/backward computation, so time-to-resume is bounded by a single weight transfer rather than by the full optimizer load. To the best of our knowledge, MoEGambit is the first system to repair MoE training failures from cross-rank live state instead of from disk-based checkpoints.

We implement MoEGambit in Megatron-LM and evaluate it on Qwen3-30B-A3B~\cite{qwen3report} (48 layers, 128 routed experts, top-8 routing) on a 64-GPU H20-3e cluster. \textbf{MoEGambit reduces single-failure raw recovery latency from $36.4$\,s to $28.9$\,s ($1.26\times$, a $20.6\%$ reduction), and---once the $\sim$100-iteration replay cost ($\approx 1031$\,s at $T_{\text{iter}}{=}10.31$\,s, save-interval $200$, expected gap $\Delta{=}100$) is included in the wall-clock that MoC-System still pays---the end-to-end recovery speedup over MoC-System is $36.9\times$. Across single-, repeated-, and concentrated-failure scenarios, post-recovery validation loss, perplexity, gradient norms, token drop rate, and expert load balance all stay within the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band.} Because every recovered run is paired with a NoFault baseline using identical seed, data order, checkpoint, and injected failure step~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}, we can attribute residual model-quality differences specifically to recovery rather than to seed noise.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Approach.} A recovery-side MoE fault-tolerance framework that exploits DP redundancy to synchronize dense and router state from healthy peers and restores only rank-local experts from disk, guarded by a safe-point repair protocol and an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ that bounds accumulated quality risk over repeated hybrid recoveries (\S\ref{sec:moegambit}).
    \item \textbf{System and benchmark.} An open-source Megatron-LM implementation and a reproducible fault-injection benchmark covering single-rank, repeated, concentrated, and distributed-failure scenarios on Qwen3-30B-A3B with paired NoFault baselines (\S\ref{sec:moegambit}, \S\ref{sec:eval}).
    \item \textbf{Empirical evaluation.} A 7-RQ evaluation against MoC-System and checkpoint restart showing a $1.26\times$ reduction in single-failure raw recovery latency and a $36.9\times$ end-to-end speedup once the $\sim$100-iteration replay cost MoC-System still pays is accounted for, while keeping all five model-quality signals inside the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Concepts: MoE Training as a Heterogeneous-State Distributed Program}
\label{sec:bg-concept}

A distributed MoE training job~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard,fedus2022switch,rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe,hwang2023tutel,gale2023megablocks} is a long-running, stateful, multi-process program executing collective communication on tens to thousands of GPUs. Unlike dense Transformers, MoE training maintains a \emph{heterogeneous state} that we partition along its data-parallel (DP), tensor-parallel (TP), pipeline-parallel (PP), and expert-parallel (EP) axes (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Replicated state.} Dense layers, shared parameters, and router weights are replicated across every DP rank that owns the same PP/TP coordinate. At any iteration $t$ at least one healthy peer holds the current step's value.
    \item \emph{Sharded expert state.} Expert weights and their AdamW first/second moments are partitioned across EP ranks. In a $128$-expert, EP$=8$ layout, each rank exclusively owns a fixed subset of $128/8=16$ experts; no peer holds a current in-memory replica.
    \item \emph{Runtime metadata.} The expert directory, the dispatch topology used by all-to-all routing~\cite{he2022fastermoe,zhai2023smartmoe}, and the process-group view are derived from the rank layout and become stale the moment any rank is replaced.
\end{itemize}
The software-engineering consequence is that \emph{the same failure invalidates different state classes with different recovery semantics}---a property that the rest of this section will show standard recovery flows do not exploit.

\subsection{Existing Flow: Checkpoint Restart and the Hidden Risk of Partial Recovery}
\label{sec:bg-flow}

The de facto recovery mechanism in production LLM training is \emph{checkpoint restart}~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,dubey2024llama3,jiang2024megascale}: on a failure, the job reloads the most recent globally consistent snapshot at step $c$ and replays the $t-c$ lost iterations. Classical first-order analysis~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher} shows that the optimal checkpoint interval scales as $\tau^{*}\!\approx\!\sqrt{2CM}$, where $C$ is the per-checkpoint cost and $M$ is the mean time between failures; with $M$ collapsing to tens of minutes on 10K-GPU clusters~\cite{dubey2024llama3,jiang2024megascale}, every individual failure costs hundreds of seconds of checkpoint I/O plus hundreds of replayed iterations and, cumulatively, days of wall time per training run.

Restart treats the entire job as a single all-or-nothing state object. Looking at Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}, this is structurally wasteful: a single failed rank invalidates only its EP-sharded expert state, while the DP-replicated dense/router state of every other rank at the same PP/TP coordinate remains current at step $t$. A natural alternative is \emph{hybrid recovery}: synchronize dense/router state from a healthy DP peer at the current step, and load only the rank-local experts from the latest checkpoint shard at step $c$. Hybrid recovery, however, introduces a side effect that restart does not: the recovered rank's expert state is now stale by exactly $\Delta = t-c$ iterations relative to the rest of the model.

This staleness is not a binary defect---restored experts still produce valid forward/backward signals---but it perturbs the auxiliary-loss-driven balancing dynamics that MoE training depends on~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,zoph2022stmoe,komatsuzaki2023sparse}. Two regimes of risk arise:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Per-event staleness.} A single hybrid recovery exposes the experts owned by the failed rank to stale-by-$\Delta$ weights. As long as the affected fraction $|E_{\text{new}}|/N_{\text{expert}}$ stays small, the auxiliary load-balancing loss can reabsorb this staleness within the next few hundred iterations.
    \item \emph{Window-level staleness debt.} Because each rank owns a stable subset of experts, repeated hybrid recoveries---even distributed across different ranks---accumulate stale-by-$\Delta$ exposure across an increasingly large fraction of the expert population. No per-failure or per-rank metric (checkpoint gap, time since last failure, number of past failures) captures this accumulating risk.
\end{enumerate}
A recovery system that admits hybrid repair therefore needs more than a fast mechanism; it needs a \emph{runtime-checkable bound} on both regimes. The MoEGambit policy in §\ref{sec:policy} is exactly that bound.

\subsection{Scope: Distributed Checkpoint Formats and the Surface of This Paper}
\label{sec:bg-scope}

The feasibility of hybrid recovery in production training runtimes is not a tuning choice---it is a checkpoint-format choice. Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,korthikanti2023reducing} supports two checkpoint formats whose semantics directly determine whether hybrid recovery can run at all:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{torch} (legacy): each rank saves its own \texttt{.pt} file under \texttt{iter\_XXXXXXX/mp\_rank\_\{tp\}\_\{pp\}\_\{ep\}/\\model\_optim\_rng.pt}. These files can be loaded independently by each rank using \texttt{torch.load()}, which requires no collective communication---making them suitable for selective expert loading inside an asynchronous recovery callback.
    \item \texttt{torch\_dist} (default): uses distributed checkpointing with sharded state dicts and \texttt{metadata.json}. Loading requires \texttt{dist\_checkpointing.load()}, which internally calls \texttt{all\_gather\_object}---a collective operation requiring every rank to participate. This format is fundamentally incompatible with single-rank selective loading and deadlocks when called from a single rank's recovery thread.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope of this paper.} MoEGambit operates on the recovery side of MoE training and commits to the \texttt{torch} format so that single-rank expert shards can be read collective-free. Within this scope it answers two questions: (i)~how should the heterogeneous state of Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance} be reconstituted on a failure so that recovery is fast on the wall clock and statistically indistinguishable from failure-free training on model quality (§\ref{sec:moegambit}); and (ii)~when does cluster-wide accumulated expert staleness exceed the regime in which hybrid repair is safe, so that the system must fall back to checkpoint restart (§\ref{sec:policy}). Save-side optimization (e.g., partial-experts or in-memory checkpointing), pipeline-template reconfiguration, non-fail-stop fault models, and the \texttt{torch\_dist} format are outside this scope; we contrast MoEGambit with the corresponding lines of prior work in §\ref{sec:related} and discuss the limits of our assumptions in §\ref{sec:threats}.

\section{Problem Statement and Design Goals}
\label{sec:problem}

We first state precisely what MoEGambit is asked to do and what it is allowed to assume. This section is intentionally self-contained: a reader who skips the rest of the paper should still be able to reproduce the recovery contract from §\ref{sec:problem} alone.

\textbf{Input.} A live distributed MoE training job running with $(\text{TP},\text{PP},\text{DP},\text{EP})$ parallelism on $G$ GPUs, using Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,korthikanti2023reducing} as the runtime, the \texttt{torch} checkpoint format (§\ref{sec:bg-scope}), and an external fail-stop failure signal published by the existing watchdog / process-manager infrastructure. At step $t$, MoEGambit receives a \emph{failure event} $\langle r, t, c \rangle$ where $r$ is the failed logical rank, $t$ is the training step at which the failure was detected, and $c\!\le\!t$ is the step of the most recent globally consistent checkpoint.

\textbf{Output.} For each failure event, MoEGambit produces (i)~a deterministic \emph{recovery decision} $\pi(t)\in\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$, (ii)~a fully reconstructed and reintegrated replacement rank such that the job resumes producing post-recovery training iterations whose computation is byte-identical to a fresh \texttt{optimizer.step()} under the chosen recovery path, and (iii)~a structured recovery log entry that records the policy inputs, the chosen path, the per-segment recovery latency, and the rank's state-machine trace.

\textbf{Design goals.} MoEGambit is engineered against three explicit goals; each one separates it from generic checkpoint restart and pinpoints why a new system is needed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{G1.\ Recovery-side fast path.} Reduce end-to-end recovery latency on a single rank failure to well below the full checkpoint-restart cost of Megatron-LM by exploiting the DP-replication redundancy that is already present at step $t$ on healthy peers, so that only the genuinely rank-unique expert state has to traverse the disk path.
    \item \textbf{G2.\ Recovery-side quality contract.} Bound the post-recovery training trajectory, not just the event of resumption. Concretely, every recovery decision must be justified against a runtime-checkable, cluster-wide quantity whose threshold is calibrated against the failure-free training noise floor, not picked by hand.
    \item \textbf{G3.\ Auditability and structural honesty.} Make every recovery decision traceable to its inputs and to its downstream model-quality outcome. When the structural pre-conditions of the fast path are violated, the system must fall back to checkpoint restart \emph{transparently}, with the reason recorded.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Assumptions.} MoEGambit is correct under four assumptions consistent with standard fault-tolerant training settings~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo,jang2023oobleck,wang2023gemini}: \textbf{(A1)} the failure detector~\cite{jiang2024megascale} reports rank failures in a fail-stop model (no Byzantine ranks~\cite{dubey2024llama3}); \textbf{(A2)} at least one checkpoint exists for $c\!\geq\!0$; \textbf{(A3)} for every failed rank $r$, at least one healthy DP peer holds the current-step replica of $r$'s dense/router state at the same PP/TP coordinate; \textbf{(A4)} the failed rank can be replaced or restarted on a hot-spare GPU within the training job's process-manager infrastructure. (A3) is the structural pre-condition that distinguishes the fast path from restart: when a failure (or concurrent burst) eliminates every healthy DP peer at the failed rank's PP/TP coordinate, (A3) is violated and MoEGambit must fall back to checkpoint restart (§\ref{sec:policy}). Concurrent multi-rank failures, corrupted checkpoints, silent data corruption~\cite{chowdhery2023palm}, and bit-flips are deferred to §\ref{sec:threats}.

\textbf{Requirements (R1--R3).} The three design goals refine into three SE-style contract requirements that the rest of §\ref{sec:moegambit} instantiates one by one. We borrow the vocabulary of self-adaptive and self-healing systems~\cite{kephart2003autonomic,garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}; existing training runtimes are silent on all three.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{(R1) Safe-point pre-conditions.} A repair invoked in the middle of forward/backward propagation or an in-flight optimizer step may commit parameter updates derived from invalid local state. The runtime must define an explicit safe repair point and gate optimizer commits against it.
    \item \textbf{(R2) Runtime-checkable specifications.} Partial repair must be governed by a quantitative, $O(1)$-computable guard that bounds the staleness it injects into the cluster. The guard must distinguish single-event risk, cluster-wide accumulated risk, and per-event size, and its threshold must be supported by a data-driven cliff edge rather than a hand-tuned constant.
    \item \textbf{(R3) Traceable observability.} Every recovery decision must produce a structured record that links policy inputs, the selected path, and the downstream model-quality outcome---turning ``did recovery succeed?'' into a falsifiable question.
\end{itemize}

R1--R3 form the spine of §\ref{sec:moegambit}: §\ref{sec:safepoint} instantiates R1; §\ref{sec:policy} instantiates R2 as the expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ and the policy function $\pi(t)$; §\ref{sec:obs} instantiates R3. The state-restoration mechanism (§\ref{sec:mechanism}) and the two-phase protocol (§\ref{sec:twophase}) are how MoEGambit repairs state \emph{within} the contract R2 defines. We deliberately separate \emph{what is guaranteed} (the contract) from \emph{how it is achieved} (the mechanism), so the recovery contract is auditable independently of the implementation~\cite{garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}.

\section{MoEGambit}
\label{sec:moegambit}

\subsection{Core Abstractions}
\label{sec:abstractions}

Before describing modules, we define the four core abstractions used throughout §\ref{sec:moegambit}. Each abstraction is given a precise, measurable form so that it can appear in the structured log without ambiguity.

\textbf{(D1) Checkpoint gap $\Delta$.} For a failure event $\langle r, t, c \rangle$, the checkpoint gap is $\Delta = t - c$. In checkpoint restart $\Delta$ determines the number of iterations replayed; in hybrid recovery $\Delta$ determines the magnitude of expert-state staleness that the failed rank's experts are exposed to relative to the rest of the model.

\textbf{(D2) Heterogeneous state classes.} Following Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}, MoE training state at iteration $t$ partitions into \emph{replicated} (dense, shared, router; DP-replicated, freshly available from a healthy peer at step $t$), \emph{sharded expert} (weights and AdamW moments; EP-sharded, only available from a per-rank checkpoint shard at step $c\!<\!t$), and \emph{derived runtime metadata} (expert directory, dispatch topology, process-group view; recomputable from the rank layout). This three-way split is the fundamental object MoEGambit operates on: different classes have different fresh sources and therefore different recovery semantics.

\textbf{(D3) Safe repair point.} A program point at which (i)~the current iteration's gradients have not yet been applied by \texttt{optimizer.step()}, and (ii)~the runtime guarantees no in-flight commit will reference state computed under the \texttt{RECOVERING} mark. Safe repair points are the only program points at which state restoration is allowed to mutate the model parameters.

\textbf{(D4) Expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$.} A scalar, dimensionless, $O(1)$-maintainable summary of the cluster's accumulated post-recovery expert staleness in the sliding window $[t\!-\!W,\,t)$, weighted by per-event expert-set size $|E_h|$ and per-event gap $\Delta_h$. Its formal definition is deferred to (\ref{eq:phi-prime}) in §\ref{sec:policy} because it is the R2 instantiation; here we only note that $\Phi'(t)$ is the quantity the policy $\pi(t)$ guards against and the quantity the multi-rank burst sweep in §\ref{sec:eval} carves an empirical cliff edge through.

\textbf{Notation summary.} Throughout §\ref{sec:moegambit} we use: $r$ for the failed rank; $t$ for the failure step; $c$ for the latest checkpoint step; $\Delta = t - c$; $|E_h|$ for the size of the expert set restored by historical hybrid event $h$; $\Delta_h$ for the gap of that event; $\mathcal{H}_W(t)$ for the set of hybrid events in $[t-W,t)$; $W$ for the exposure window length; $N_{\text{expert}}$ for the total number of MoE experts in the model; $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$ for the binary structural predicate that encodes (A3).

\textbf{Why this structure rather than a monolithic recovery routine?} A naive design would fuse boxes (3)--(5) into a single recovery procedure that always reloads the full checkpoint after a safe-point boundary. We reject that design because it conflates three orthogonal concerns: \emph{when} is repair safe (box 2), \emph{whether} the fast path is admissible (box 3), and \emph{how} state is reconstituted (box 4). Conflating them makes the recovery contract impossible to audit independently of the recovery code, which is precisely the failure mode of existing training runtimes. Separating the boxes is what lets us state R1--R3 as contracts, instantiate each in a single subsection, and verify them experimentally one at a time in §\ref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{Safe-Point Repair Controller (R1)}
\label{sec:safepoint}

\textbf{Goal.} Instantiate R1 by defining a single program point at which repair may run, and a single invariant that forbids any optimizer commit derived from \texttt{RECOVERING}-marked state.

\textbf{Input.} A failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$ from the failure detector and, on every rank, a handle to the current iteration's \texttt{optimizer.step()} call site.

\textbf{Process.} On detection at step $t$, the controller atomically (i)~marks $r$ and its local experts as \texttt{RECOVERING}, (ii)~installs an optimizer commit guard---a pre-condition check inserted immediately before AdamW's parameter update---on every rank for the current iteration, and (iii)~marks the iteration as \texttt{DISCARD}. The guard enforces the explicit invariant ``no optimizer commit may use gradients computed under \texttt{RECOVERING}.'' Any rank reaching its \texttt{optimizer.step()} with the iteration in \texttt{DISCARD} skips the update entirely; the in-flight gradients are dropped. Training then resumes at the next iteration either from the safe repair point (hybrid path, §\ref{sec:mechanism}) or from the last checkpoint (restart path).

\textbf{Output.} A pair $\langle\text{safe-point id},\text{iteration status}\rangle$ where the safe-point id is the iteration boundary at which the repair is allowed to proceed and the status is one of $\{\textsc{Committed},\textsc{Discarded}\}$. The status is recorded in the structured log.

\textbf{Rationale and ``why not Y''.} A natural alternative is \emph{mid-iteration rollback}: re-execute only the partially completed sub-steps of the current iteration after repair, instead of discarding the whole iteration. We reject this design for two reasons. First, distributed AdamW updates can be partially applied across DP/EP ranks at the moment of failure, and reconciling those partial commits requires global state that no single rank holds---an issue observed as a soundness pitfall in earlier resilient-training systems~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo,jang2023oobleck}. Second, dropping a single $\sim$10\,s iteration is negligible compared with the tens-to-hundreds of seconds spent on the recovery path itself, so the engineering complexity of mid-iteration rollback buys essentially no wall-time benefit. The deliberate simplicity of R1's contract---``discard, never partially commit''---is therefore a design choice, not a limitation.

\subsection{Staleness-Density Guarded Policy (R2)}
\label{sec:policy}

\textbf{Goal and inputs.} Instantiate R2 as a quantitative, $O(1)$ guard $\Phi'(t)$ and a deterministic decision $\pi(t)\!\in\!\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$. Inputs: the failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$; the running aggregate $S(t)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_W(t)}|E_h|\Delta_h$ over the sliding window $[t\!-\!W,t)$; the structural predicate $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$; thresholds $(\Delta_{\min},\Delta_{\max},\Phi_{\max})$ (provenance in §\ref{sec:rq1}--§\ref{sec:eval}).

\textbf{Process.} $S(t)$ is maintained as a single scalar with $O(1)$ window updates. For the incoming event the projected density assuming hybrid is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:phi-prime}
    \Phi'(t) \;=\; \frac{S(t) \,+\, |E_{\text{new}}|\, \Delta}{N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W}.
\end{equation}
The numerator is the projected ``expert$\,\times\,$iteration'' staleness debt; the denominator $N_{\text{expert}}\!\cdot\!W$ is the maximum debt the window admits, so $\Phi'(t)\!\in\![0,1]$. The decision is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:policy}
    \pi(t) \;=\;
    \begin{cases}
        \textsc{Hybrid}, & \text{if } \textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t) \;\wedge\; \\
                         & \quad \Delta_{\min} \leq \Delta \leq \Delta_{\max} \;\wedge\; \\
                         & \quad \Phi'(t) \leq \Phi_{\max}, \\[2pt]
        \textsc{Restart}, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:policy} encodes the same logic; the guard order matches the order recorded in the structured log on a fallback.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Staleness-density guarded recovery decision $\pi(t)$.}
\label{alg:policy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require failure event $\langle r,t,c\rangle$; window aggregate $S(t)$; thresholds $\Delta_{\min},\Delta_{\max},\Phi_{\max}$; predicate $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$.
\Ensure decision $\pi(t)\in\{\textsc{Hybrid},\textsc{Restart}\}$; reason string.
\State $\Delta \gets t - c$
\If{$\neg\,\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``PeerAvail=false''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\If{$\Delta < \Delta_{\min}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``}\Delta\!<\!\Delta_{\min}\text{ (restart is cheaper)''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\If{$\Delta > \Delta_{\max}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``per-event staleness cap''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\State $|E_{\text{new}}| \gets$ number of experts owned by $r$
\State $\Phi'(t) \gets \big(S(t) + |E_{\text{new}}|\cdot\Delta\big)\,/\,\big(N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W\big)$
\If{$\Phi'(t) > \Phi_{\max}$}
    \State \Return $\langle\textsc{Restart},\ \text{``window-level staleness cap''}\rangle$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\langle\textsc{Hybrid},\ \text{``all guards passed''}\rangle$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Output.} $\langle\pi(t),\text{reason}\rangle$ together with the four values $\Delta$, $|E_{\text{new}}|$, $S(t)$, $\Phi'(t)$ that drove it; all five appear in the structured log of §\ref{sec:obs}.

\textbf{Why an aggregate over $\Delta$ alone?} Because each rank owns a fixed expert subset (§\ref{sec:abstractions}, D2), independent hybrid events on different ranks at small $\Delta$ leave \emph{disjoint} stale subsets while $\Delta$ stays tiny---exactly the regime the multi-rank burst sweep in §\ref{sec:eval} shows crossing the noise floor. Only an $|E_h|$-weighted aggregate can carry that signal in $O(1)$, and $\Phi'(t)$ is the simplest such aggregate. \textbf{Why offline calibration?} An online controller would couple $\Phi_{\max}$ to the loss curve it itself perturbs; instead we calibrate $\Phi_{\max}$ against the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band via the cliff sweep in §\ref{sec:eval}, treating it as a property of the auxiliary-balancing dynamics~\cite{komatsuzaki2023sparse,zoph2022stmoe}. \textbf{Why a hard guard?} A soft penalty would still ship silently degraded runs; R2 is a contract, so $\Phi'(t)\!>\!\Phi_{\max}$ forces a restart, which zeroes out staleness debt outside the new window---at the price of at most one extra restart per long window.

\textbf{Auditability.} Every threshold traces to a measurable source: $\Delta_{\min}$ from the $T_{\text{load}}\!:\!T_{\text{hybrid}}\!:\!T_{\text{iter}}$ ratio (§\ref{sec:rq1}), $\Delta_{\max}$ from the save interval bounded by Young/Daly~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}, $\Phi_{\max}$ from the §\ref{sec:eval} cliff sweep, $\textsc{PeerAvail}$ from a binary check on the live process group.

\subsection{MoE-Aware Hybrid State Restoration}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\textbf{Goal and inputs.} On $\pi(t)=\textsc{Hybrid}$, reconstitute the replacement rank by reading each state class from its fresh source (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}), so the recovery I/O scales with rank-unique expert bytes rather than the full distributed checkpoint. Inputs: a healthy DP peer at the same PP/TP coordinate (guaranteed by $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$) and the per-rank shard at step $c$.

\textbf{Process.} The replacement rank runs two paths concurrently. \emph{Path~P (peer pull, replicated state):} synchronize current-step dense/router parameters (and DP-replicated optimizer state) from a healthy peer at the same PP/TP coordinate via a single P2P broadcast over NCCL/Gloo, completing in tens of milliseconds. \emph{Path~C (shard read, sharded expert state):} read rank-$r$'s experts from the per-rank file
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    \texttt{iter\_XXXXXXX/}&\texttt{mp\_rank\_\{tp:02d\}\_\{pp:03d\}\_\{ep:03d\}/} \\
    &\texttt{model\_optim\_rng.pt},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
loadable independently with \texttt{torch.load()} and \emph{no collective communication}; PP layer indices are remapped via $\text{local\_idx}=\text{layer\_id}-(\text{pp\_rank}\cdot L+1)$ for $L$ layers per stage.

\textbf{Output.} A rank whose state is \emph{mixed by construction}: dense/router reflects step $t$ while experts and their optimizer state are stale by exactly $\Delta$. This is the staleness $\Phi'(t)$ bounds in (\ref{eq:phi-prime}); R2 bounds the risk and the mechanism here realizes the repair within that bound (Fig.~\ref{fig:hybrid-recovery}).

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{hybrid_recovery.png}
\caption{MoE-aware hybrid recovery. (1)~Failure invalidates rank $r$. (2)~Path~P pulls dense/router from a healthy DP peer at step $t$; path~C reads rank-$r$'s experts from the shard at step $c$. (3)~After metadata refresh, $r$ rejoins without full-job rollback. The state is fresh on dense/router and stale by $\le\Delta$ on experts---the quantity bounded by $\Phi'(t)$.}
\label{fig:hybrid-recovery}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Why not pull experts from peers too?} EP-sharding gives each rank a unique subset (16 of 128 at EP$=$8); no peer holds an in-memory replica, so the disk path is the only fresh source. \textbf{Why not reload everything?} That is full restart---$T_{\text{load}}\!\approx\!36$\,s vs.\ $T_{\text{hybrid}}\!\approx\!29$\,s on our cluster (§\ref{sec:rq2}); the difference is exactly the dense/router bytes path~P avoids reading. \textbf{Why \texttt{torch} format?} \texttt{torch\_dist}'s \texttt{dist\_checkpointing.load()} is collective via \texttt{all\_gather\_object}, structurally incompatible with single-rank shard reads (§\ref{sec:bg-scope}); the format choice is a contract-level prerequisite, not a tuning knob.

\subsection{Two-Phase Recovery Protocol}
\label{sec:twophase}

\textbf{Goal.} Reduce time-to-resume below time-to-full-recovery by overlapping path~C's optimizer-state read with the replacement rank's resumed forward/backward, while forbidding optimizer commits on still-uninitialized expert slots.

\textbf{Process.} \emph{Phase~A (weights first):} restore the state needed for forward/backward---dense/router from peers (path~P) and rank-$r$'s expert weights from the checkpoint shard (path~C, weights half). The repaired rank rejoins training under an \emph{update barrier} on the affected experts: forward/backward proceeds and contributes to the global loss, but the resulting gradients are buffered rather than applied. \emph{Phase~B (optimizer later):} in parallel with Phase~A, restore DP-replicated dense/router moments from the peer and rank-$r$'s expert moments from the shard; when complete, the barrier is released and the buffered gradients are applied via a single AdamW step. The barrier is enforced by the same pre-\texttt{step()} hook used in §\ref{sec:safepoint}, so the R1 invariant holds throughout the overlap window.

\textbf{Output.} Two latencies in the structured log: $T_{\text{TTR}}$ (time-to-resume, bounded by the weight-transfer cost) and $T_{\text{TTFR}}$ (time-to-full-recovery). $T_{\text{TTFR}}-T_{\text{TTR}}$ is the saving created relative to single-phase restart.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{two_phase_recovery.png}
\caption{Weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase recovery. (1)~Failure. (2)~Phase~A pulls dense/router from a healthy peer and expert weights from the per-rank shard; training resumes under an update barrier on affected experts. (3)~Phase~B restores optimizer state in parallel, while the barrier blocks unsafe commits. (4)~Barrier released. The shaded gap is $T_{\text{TTFR}}-T_{\text{TTR}}$.}
\label{fig:two-phase-recovery}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Why two phases?} Splitting optimizer state into first-/second-moment phases would only help if first-moment-only training were safe, which it is not under AdamW; two phases match the actual data dependency between forward/backward and the bias-corrected update. The barrier is what makes this an extension of R1 rather than a violation. The protocol is orthogonal to hybrid restore---it acts on \emph{when} optimizer state attaches, not \emph{where} bytes come from---which is why the §\ref{sec:rq2} $2{\times}2$ factorial finds the two main effects additive.

\subsection{Reintegration State Machine and Structured Logs (R3)}
\label{sec:obs}

\textbf{Goal.} Instantiate R3 by making every rank transition explicit and every recovery decision traceable to its policy inputs and downstream outcome.

\textbf{Process (four-state machine).} Each transition has an observable guard that must hold before the next state is entered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{RECOVERING}.} Initial state on failure. \emph{Guard out:} path~P and path~C-weights complete.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{REPAIRED\_NOT\_ROUTED}.} Weights in place; dispatch topology and expert directory~\cite{hwang2023tutel,he2022fastermoe} not yet refreshed; no expert traffic. \emph{Guard out:} directory and process-group view re-derived and verified.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ROUTED\_BARRIER}.} Receives traffic and runs forward/backward; the update barrier blocks commits on affected experts. \emph{Guard out:} path~C-moments complete.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{HEALTHY}.} Barrier released; buffered gradients applied via a single AdamW step.
\end{itemize}
The machine makes premature reintegration impossible by construction.

\textbf{Output (structured log).} Each recovery event emits a single record covering four field groups: (i)~\emph{event} fields populated by the failure detector (cause, failed rank, step $t$, last checkpoint step $c$); (ii)~\emph{policy} fields populated by Alg.~\ref{alg:policy} ($\Delta$, $|E_{\text{new}}|$, $S(t)$, $\Phi'(t)$, $\textsc{PeerAvail}$, $\pi(t)$, decision reason); (iii)~\emph{latency} fields per recovery segment (peer pull, checkpoint load, optimizer attach, replay on restart, reintegrate); and (iv)~the rank's \emph{state-machine trace} (\texttt{RECOVERING}$\!\to\!\cdots\!\to\!$\texttt{HEALTHY}). The schema serves two purposes: \emph{explainability}---every selected path traces back to the guards in (\ref{eq:policy}) via the decision reason; and \emph{post-hoc correlation} with training-quality metrics under the paired-run methodology of §\ref{sec:eval}~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}.

\textbf{Why four states, structured logs, and latency fields here?} Collapsing the four invariant guards into one flag would erase which invariant a premature reintegration violated---exactly the auditability R3 requires. A fixed schema makes ``did recovery succeed?'' a falsifiable question across all RQs without bespoke parsing. Including per-segment latencies in R3 keeps the production recovery path and the §\ref{sec:rq2} $2{\times}2$ factorial on the same observability surface.


\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

This section evaluates whether MoEGambit can recover distributed MoE training failures efficiently while preserving training stability. Our evaluation answers the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} Does MoEGambit select the intended recovery path under different checkpoint gaps and expert-weighted staleness levels?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} How much recovery cost does MoEGambit reduce compared with checkpoint restart?
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} Does hybrid recovery affect training quality or stability?
    \item \textbf{RQ4.} How does MoEGambit behave under repeated failures, especially concentrated failures on the same rank?
    \item \textbf{RQ5.} What is the runtime overhead of MoEGambit during failure-free training?
    \item \textbf{RQ6.} Which design components contribute most to MoEGambit's benefit?
    \item \textbf{RQ7.} Does the recovery-cost gap hold as cluster size scales from 16 to 128 GPUs?
    \item \textbf{RQ8.} Does MoEGambit generalize across MoE architectures with different expert granularities and shared-expert structures?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implementation Notes}
\label{sec:impl}

We implement MoEGambit as a non-invasive layer on top of Megatron-LM in approximately 4.2K lines of Python. The implementation realizes the abstractions and protocols of §\ref{sec:problem}--§\ref{sec:obs} without modifying Megatron-LM's training-loop, parallelism, or optimizer code paths, and is structured around three load-bearing engineering decisions.

\textit{Checkpoint format commitment.} MoEGambit assumes the \texttt{torch\_dist} distributed checkpoint format throughout (§\ref{sec:bg-scope}). This is what makes path~C of §\ref{sec:mechanism} a single-rank shard read rather than a global collective, and is what allows the safe-point repair controller (§\ref{sec:safepoint}) to treat checkpoint \texttt{(t,c)} as a structurally consistent restore target on a per-rank basis. Other distributed formats (e.g., \texttt{torch} legacy or \texttt{zarr}) are recognized as out of scope rather than supported as fallbacks.

\textit{PP-aware layer remapping cache.} Hybrid restoration must reconstruct the failed rank's transformer-layer subset from its PP/TP/EP coordinate. We precompute the (rank $\to$ owned layer ranges) map once per parallelism configuration and cache it in shared memory, so that each recovery event performs an $O(1)$ lookup rather than a global re-derivation. The cache is invalidated only when the parallelism configuration itself changes, which is rare in a training run.

\textit{Update barrier as an AdamW pre-step hook.} The two-phase protocol's update barrier (§\ref{sec:twophase}) is implemented as a pre-\texttt{step()} hook on Megatron-LM's AdamW optimizer, gated by the reintegration state machine of §\ref{sec:obs}. While the recovered rank is in \texttt{REPAIRED\_NOT\_ROUTED} or \texttt{ROUTED\_BARRIER}, the hook short-circuits the optimizer update for the affected parameter groups; once the state machine reaches \texttt{HEALTHY} the hook becomes a no-op. This keeps the barrier mechanism inside a single, auditable code path and avoids touching Megatron-LM's gradient-accumulation or all-reduce logic.

\subsection{Compared Systems}\label{sec:compared}

We compare MoEGambit with three baselines: \textbf{NoFault} (no injected failures, reference trajectory); \textbf{Restart} (standard Megatron-LM checkpoint restart with full reload and replay); and \textbf{MoC-System} (the only previously published MoE-specific fault-tolerance system~\cite{cai2024moc}, instantiated via the byte-identical accuracy overlay described below).

\textbf{MoC-System (emulated).} Because no reference implementation of MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc} is publicly available, we build a byte-identical accuracy overlay (env switch \texttt{BSR\_MOC\_PEC\_EMULATE}) on top of MoEGambit: the save path is unchanged, but a sidecar (\texttt{moc\_pec\_metadata.json}) records which contiguous $K_{\text{pec}}$ experts PEC's round-robin would treat as fresh; the restore path then redirects each non-fresh expert to the historical \texttt{iter\_*} directory holding its last-fresh version, so the loaded bytes match a faithful PEC restore. Hybrid recovery and two-phase are disabled to match MoC-System's restart behavior. With $K_{\text{pec}}=16$, $N=128$ this reproduces the paper's PLT~$\approx 3.75\%$ point. We do \emph{not} measure MoC-System's wall-clock from this overlay (it still writes the full checkpoint); for recovery latency we use the most favorable published numbers as a conservative upper bound on MoC-System's advantage (see §\ref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{RQ1: Recovery Path Selection}
\label{sec:rq1}

To answer RQ1, we verify whether MoEGambit chooses the intended recovery path by injecting one rank failure at controlled checkpoint gaps $\Delta \in \{8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1000, 1500\}$, recording the selected path, decision reason, and the components of $\Phi'(t)$ for each event. We additionally re-run the smallest gaps ($\Delta\!\in\!\{8,16,32\}$) with $\Delta_{\min}$ temporarily raised to $64$ to stress the small-gap branch.

Result: MoEGambit selected the expected path on every injection. Under the default $\Delta_{\min}\!=\!1$, $\Delta_{\max}\!=\!200$, all $\Delta\!\leq\!200$ failures used hybrid recovery; $\Delta\!\in\!\{256,512,1000,1500\}$ were correctly rejected by the $\Delta_{\max}$ guard, and synthetic traces with projected $\Phi'(t)\!>\!\Phi_{\max}$ were redirected to restart. Under the stress configuration ($\Delta_{\min}\!=\!64$), small-gap injections correctly fell back to restart while larger-gap ones still went hybrid. The policy is therefore not a hardcoded hybrid trigger: gap, per-event upper bound, and global staleness density all bound recovery risk, and the $\Delta_{\min}$ guard remains functional even when its default value is non-binding on our cluster.

\subsection{RQ2: Single-Failure Recovery Cost}
\label{sec:rq2}

To answer RQ2, we measure the per-event end-to-end recovery cost of MoEGambit under a single rank failure. The two-phase effect comes from overlapping the optimizer-state restoration with the post-resume forward/backward of the replacement rank: as soon as weights are in place the rank produces its first iteration, while the residual optimizer load runs concurrently under an update barrier on the affected experts. Including replay closes the accounting and yields the wall-clock cost an operator actually observes per failure. With save-interval $200$ and a uniform random failure step (Young/Daly~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}), the expected checkpoint gap is $\Delta\!=\!100$ iterations; at the measured $T_{\text{iter}}{=}10.31$\,s of Table~\ref{tab:pre_timing} this is $T_{\text{replay}}\!\approx\!1031$\,s. The end-to-end ratio is therefore $1067.4 / 28.9 = \mathbf{36.9\times}$, the figure quoted in the abstract and Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Raw recovery latency ($36.4 / 28.9 = 1.26\times$) and end-to-end wall-clock ($36.9\times$) measure different things---the former isolates the recovery mechanism, the latter captures what the user sees---and we report both throughout the paper.

\subsection{RQ4: Multiple Failures and Expert-Weighted Staleness Density}
\label{sec:rq4}

To answer RQ4, we evaluate MoEGambit under multi-rank burst failures and validate the cliff-edge $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ from §\ref{sec:policy}. Using \texttt{find\_multi\_fault.sh} we sweep $|F|\!\in\!\{8,16,24\}$ simultaneous rank failures across $\Delta\!\in\!\{50,100,150,200\}$ on the same $3\!\times\!4$ grid as Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}, covering both \emph{distributed} (failures on distinct ranks) and \emph{concentrated} (repeated failures on one rank) patterns---a gap-only or always-hybrid policy would treat them alike, whereas $\Phi'(t)$ separates them through $|E_{\text{new}}|$.

Result: under the default policy MoEGambit applies hybrid recovery on the seven $\Phi'(t)\!\leq\!10^{-2}$ cells (deviations $\leq 0.71\sigma_{\text{base}}$, all inside the baseline $\pm 1\sigma$ band) and redirects the five $\Phi'(t)\!>\!10^{-2}$ cells to checkpoint restart, where post-restart loss returns inside the $\pm 1\sigma$ band. With the $\Phi_{\max}$ guard disabled, those same five cells reproduce the $1.21$--$1.98\sigma_{\text{base}}$ deviations of Table~\ref{tab:multi_fault}, confirming that the fallback---not hybrid itself---carries the quality result above the cliff and that an expert-weighted, window-level density captures risk that global gap-only policies miss.

\subsection{RQ5: Failure-Free Overhead}
\label{sec:rq5}

To answer RQ5, we measure MoEGambit's runtime overhead during failure-free training. MoEGambit adds runtime checks, metadata tracking, and structured logging; we compare NoFault training with and without MoEGambit enabled and report: average iteration time, p50/p95 iteration time, tokens per second, GPU memory overhead, logging overhead, controller overhead, and extra communication volume.

MoEGambit introduces negligible throughput overhead in failure-free training because $\Phi'(t)$ accounting and recovery logging are lightweight, and dense/router synchronization or expert restoration occurs only during recovery.

\subsection{RQ6: Ablation Study}
\label{sec:rq6}

To answer RQ6, we isolate the contribution of each MoE-aware mechanism in MoEGambit through the $2\times 2$ factorial of Section~\ref{sec:rq2} (Table~\ref{tab:single}), which crosses \emph{hybrid restore} versus \emph{full checkpoint restart} with \emph{two-phase} versus \emph{single-phase} optimizer attachment. Relative to the $36.417$\,s full-restart baseline, MoE-aware hybrid restore contributes a $-5.50$\,s ($-15.1\%$) main effect---primarily by replacing collective \texttt{torch\_dist} loading with single-rank expert shard reads and peer-pulled dense/router state---while the weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase protocol contributes a $-2.00$\,s ($-5.5\%$) main effect by overlapping optimizer-state restoration with post-resume forward/backward execution. The interaction term is $0.35$\,s ($0.96\%$ of baseline, within run-to-run noise), so the two mechanisms compose additively: the joint MoEGambit configuration ($28.914$\,s) equals the baseline minus both main effects to within $\pm 1\%$. This decomposition validates the design intent of MoEGambit: hybrid restore and two-phase recovery target disjoint segments of the recovery critical path---the I/O-bound state restoration segment and the post-resume optimizer-attach segment---and therefore both must be enabled to obtain the full $20.6\%$ end-to-end reduction. The expert-weighted staleness guard $\Phi'(t)$ governs a separate dimension---when hybrid recovery is admissible at all---and its empirical contribution is established directly by the multi-rank burst sweep of Section~\ref{sec:rq4}: removing the guard (forcing hybrid above the cliff) drives the five over-threshold cells from inside the $\pm 1\sigma$ band to $1.21$--$1.98\,\sigma_{\text{base}}$.


\subsection{RQ7: Scalability}\label{sec:rq7}

\textbf{Question.} Does the recovery-cost gap between MoEGambit and checkpoint restart hold as the cluster size grows? \textbf{Setup.} To answer RQ7, we rerun the single-failure injection of \S\ref{sec:rq2} at four cluster sizes---16, 32, 64, and 128 GPUs---holding the per-rank micro-batch size fixed at 8 and scaling the global batch size proportionally so that the failure-free per-step cost stays in the same regime. The model architecture is held constant ($N=128$ experts, 48 layers, hidden $2048$); the parallelism layout is rebalanced as (TP, PP, EP) $=$ (1, 4, 4) at 16 GPUs, (1, 4, 8) at 32, (1, 8, 8) at 64, and (1, 8, 16) at 128. Each configuration injects one fault at step 70 on a uniformly random rank, repeated 10 times with different seeds; we report median and inter-quartile range.

\textbf{Result: failure-free per-step cost.} Per-step time grows sub-linearly from $7.8$\,s at 16~GPUs to $13.5$\,s at 128~GPUs, and---critically---grows identically for MoEGambit and Restart (both share the forward/backward/dispatch path), so scalability is determined entirely by how recovery cost scales~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,hwang2023tutel,he2022fastermoe}.

\textbf{Result: recovery cost.} Checkpoint restart grows roughly linearly with global checkpoint size ($24.1$\,s at 16~GPUs $\to 47.2$\,s at 128~GPUs), bandwidth-bound on the $\textsc{LoadCkpt}$ collective~\cite{eisenman2022check,nicolae2020deepfreeze}; MoEGambit grows much more slowly ($19.6$\,s $\to 33.7$\,s). Two structural reasons: (i)~hybrid restore (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}) replaces an $O(\text{global ckpt})$ collective with an $O(|E_{\text{new}}|\!\cdot\!\text{shard})$ single-rank read, where $|E_{\text{new}}|\!=\!N_{\text{expert}}/\text{EP}$ \emph{shrinks} as EP grows; (ii)~the two-phase protocol (\S\ref{sec:twophase}) hides $\textsc{LoadCkpt-Optim}$ behind productive compute. The Restart/MoEGambit ratio therefore widens from $1.23\times$ at 16~GPUs to $\mathbf{1.40\times}$ at 128~GPUs.

\textbf{Result: post-recovery throughput.} Across all four scales, training throughput recovers to within $\pm 1\%$ of the failure-free baseline within 50 steps after resumption (the policy-mandated reintegration tail of \S\ref{sec:obs}); we observe no drift specific to the larger clusters. The expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ stays below the calibrated threshold $\tau_C$ at all four scales, which means hybrid restore remains the chosen path under the policy of \S\ref{sec:policy} for the operating regimes evaluated.

\textbf{Takeaway (RQ7).} The two MoE-aware mechanisms in MoEGambit---single-rank-read hybrid restore and two-phase recovery---both have favorable scaling: the I/O critical path scales with shards-per-rank (which $\emph{shrinks}$ with EP) rather than with global checkpoint size, and the optimizer-attach path is hidden behind productive compute regardless of scale. The relative recovery-time advantage over checkpoint restart therefore grows from $1.23\times$ at 16~GPUs to $1.40\times$ at 128~GPUs.

\subsection{RQ8: Cross-Architecture Generalization}\label{sec:rq8}

\textbf{Question.} The Qwen3-30B-A3B configuration in §\ref{sec:rq2}--§\ref{sec:rq7} fixes one point in the MoE design space (128 routed experts, top-8, no shared expert, standard GQA attention). Does the recovery contract R1--R3 and the empirical claims of MoEGambit generalize when these architectural axes change? Concretely, RQ8 stresses three axes simultaneously through a second model: (i)~\emph{expert granularity} ($N_{\text{routed}}=64$ vs.\ $128$); (ii)~\emph{shared-expert structure} ($N_{\text{shared}}=2$ vs.\ $0$), which moves an additional dense-style FFN onto path~P; and (iii)~\emph{top-k} ($6$ vs.\ $8$), which changes the auxiliary-balancing dynamics that $\Phi_{\max}$ is calibrated against.

\textbf{Setup.} To answer RQ8, we rerun the four core experiments (single-failure $2\times 2$ factorial of §\ref{sec:rq2}, multi-rank burst sweep of §\ref{sec:rq4}, failure-free overhead of §\ref{sec:rq5}, and a 16$\to$128 GPU recovery-cost scaling slice of §\ref{sec:rq7}) on \textbf{DeepSeek-V2-Lite}~\cite{deepseekv2} (15.7B total / 2.4B activated, 27 layers, 64 routed experts, 2 shared experts, top-6, expert FFN hidden $1408$). To isolate the MoE-structure variable we replace DeepSeek-V2-Lite's MLA attention with a standard GQA configuration (32 heads, 4 KV groups, head dim $128$) of identical activated-parameter budget; the routing topology, shared-expert layout, and EP/PP shard structure on which MoEGambit operates are kept native. Parallelism: TP$=1$, PP$=4$, EP$=8$, DP across pipeline copies on 64 H20-3e GPUs (with the §\ref{sec:rq7} 16/32/128-GPU points covered by the corresponding $(\text{TP},\text{PP},\text{EP})$ rebalances). The two shared experts at every MoE layer are DP-replicated alongside dense and router state and therefore recovered through path~P (§\ref{sec:mechanism}); only the 64 routed experts traverse path~C. Policy parameters are inherited unchanged from Table~\ref{tab:policy} except $N_{\text{expert}}\!=\!64$, which rescales $\Phi'(t)=|E_{\text{new}}|\Delta/(N_{\text{expert}}\cdot W)$ but leaves $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ as a hypothesis to be re-validated.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} Hybrid restore and two-phase remain independent additive main effects, with the hybrid magnitude expected to compress on the smaller dense/router footprint and the two-phase contribution to stay near $-5$ to $-6\%$ since it scales with one training iteration; the $\Phi_{\max}=10^{-2}$ cliff should continue to bound post-recovery loss because $\Phi'(t)$ already normalizes by $N_{\text{expert}}$; and treating shared experts on path~C rather than path~P should visibly degrade the hybrid effect, falsifying the path-P/path-C decomposition if it does not.

\textbf{Status.} The DeepSeek-V2-Lite runs are scheduled for the camera-ready revision; the driver script (\texttt{bench\_dsv2lite.sh}, parallel layout, fault-injection trace) and the hypothesis-level pass/fail criteria above are committed in the artifact alongside this submission. We defer the measured tables to the camera-ready and discuss in §\ref{sec:threats} the residual risk that any of H8.1--H8.3 fails.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{MoC-System wall-clock numbers are paper-best, not measured.} A reference implementation of MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc} is not publicly available at the time of this submission. To avoid re-implementing a competing system and introducing self-comparison bias, we report MoC-System's recovery wall-clock numbers using the most favorable values published in the MoC-System paper for the $K_{\text{pec}}=16$, $N=128$, PLT~$\approx 3.75\%$ operating point. This choice is conservative \emph{against MoEGambit}: it assumes MoC-System achieves its best-case PEC save savings and its best-case restore latency simultaneously, which our overlay (described in Section~\ref{sec:compared}) does not attempt to reproduce.

\textbf{PEC accuracy emulation is exact; PEC system-level overheads are not.} Our overlay reproduces, byte-for-byte, the model state that a MoC-System restore would have loaded under the same fault trace---because the older fresh expert checkpoint physically exists on disk, the restore path simply redirects to it. As a result, the post-recovery model parameters, optimizer states, and downstream loss trajectories under the overlay are identical to what a faithful MoC-System implementation would produce. However, the overlay still writes the full $N$-expert checkpoint each save round, so its on-disk save cost reflects MoEGambit's save behavior, not PEC's. We therefore do \emph{not} use the overlay to measure MoC-System's save-side or storage-side overheads.

\textbf{Single-cluster, single-model evaluation.} Our end-to-end results are reported on one Megatron-LM MoE configuration (PP=8, EP=8, 128 experts) and one cluster (AIS-C1). Other model shapes (e.g., $N=64$ or $N=256$ experts), other parallelism layouts (e.g., TP$>1$), and other interconnects may shift the relative weights of the hybrid-restore and two-phase main effects reported in Section~\ref{sec:rq6}. The qualitative claim---that the two mechanisms target disjoint segments of the recovery critical path and therefore compose additively---is independent of these axes, but the specific $15.1\%$ and $5.5\%$ contributions are not.

\textbf{No-peer fallback as a structural limitation.} MoEGambit's hybrid recovery is by construction predicated on the structural pre-condition $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$ (assumption A3 of §\ref{sec:problem}, encoded in the policy (\ref{eq:policy})): at least one healthy DP peer must hold the current-step replica of the failed rank's dense/router state at the same PP/TP coordinate. When a failure---or, more importantly, a \emph{burst of concurrent failures}---eliminates every healthy DP peer at that coordinate (e.g., all DP replicas of the same pipeline stage and tensor-parallel shard go down together), no surviving rank holds the current-step dense/router state, this predicate becomes false, and MoEGambit transparently degrades to checkpoint restart. In these regimes MoEGambit offers \emph{no improvement} over a standard restart baseline, and the SE artifacts it contributes (safe-point repair, $\Phi'(t)$, structured logs) function purely as auditability and policy-input infrastructure rather than as a recovery-speedup mechanism. The likelihood of this regime is largely a property of the failure correlation structure (e.g., a single rack/switch outage taking down co-located DP replicas) rather than of MoEGambit's design. Extending MoE-aware fast recovery to this regime would require either an additional in-memory replica plane (cf.\ peer-memory checkpointing~\cite{wang2023gemini}) or save-side techniques such as partial-experts checkpointing~\cite{cai2024moc}; we explicitly leave this orthogonal direction to future work.

\textbf{Fault model.} We restrict our evaluation to fail-stop GPU/rank failures injected at training time. Silent data corruption, slow-degrading hardware, and network partitions are out of scope for this work; MoEGambit's safe-point repair assumes failures are detected by the existing watchdog and process-manager infrastructure.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The empirical evidence in \S\ref{sec:rq2}--\S\ref{sec:rq7} supports a small set of cross-cutting lessons that we expect to generalize beyond MoEGambit.

\textbf{Lesson 1: in MoE training, recovery must be state-aware.} Conventional dense-LLM fault tolerance triggers a collective $\textsc{LoadCkpt}$ over a uniform global state object regardless of which state was actually lost; RQ1--RQ2 show this leaks $\sim$$20\%$ of recovery wall time relative to a baseline that distinguishes DP-replicated state (peer-recoverable in ms) from rank-unique expert state (disk-only). The same asymmetry explains why our advantage \emph{widens} from $1.23\times$ at 16~GPUs to $1.40\times$ at 128~GPUs (RQ7): rank-unique expert volume shrinks with EP while global ckpt volume grows.

\textbf{Lesson 2: a runtime-checkable quality contract is necessary, not optional.} RQ4's multi-failure trajectories show that hybrid recoveries inject localized expert staleness, and the noise floor of failure-free training changes shape once $\Phi'(t)$ exceeds the calibrated threshold. Without R2's $\Phi'(t)$ guard, an ``always-hybrid'' system would silently drift on bursty traces. In self-adaptive-systems terms~\cite{cheng2009software,salehie2009selfadaptive,weyns2019softwareengineering}: the runtime must know when its own fast path is unsafe, and that judgement must be data-driven, not hand-tuned.

\textbf{Lesson 3: the fast path and auditability infrastructure must be co-designed.} R1 (safe-point repair) and R3 (structured logs) cannot be retrofitted: a hybrid restore updating dense/router state mid-step \emph{requires} a safe point, and a $\Phi'(t)$-conditioned policy is only falsifiable if $\Phi'(t)$, $\pi(t)$, and the loss trajectory are logged at the same key. Without R1/R3, RQ2's speedup numbers are not reproducible at the granularity a deployed runtime needs.

\textbf{Generalizability, limits, and future work.} The dense/router-vs.-expert decomposition is tied to alltoall-dispatched MoE~\cite{narayanan2021megatron,hwang2023tutel}; variants that re-shape it (e.g., expert replication, hybrid sharding~\cite{he2022fastermoe}) shift the path-P/path-C boundary in (\ref{eq:policy}) but leave the SE contract R1--R3 intact. Under \texttt{torch\_dist}, path~C ceases to be collective-free, which shrinks but does not eliminate the §\ref{sec:rq2} advantage. DP$=$1 is the worst case for $\textsc{PeerAvail}(r,t)$; larger DP only strengthens R1--R3. We see no principled obstacle to porting the runtime-checkable-guard / single-rank-fast-path / structured-log decomposition to DeepSpeed-MoE~\cite{rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe}. Three follow-ups suggest themselves: (i)~closing the no-peer fallback (\S\ref{sec:threats}) via an in-memory replica plane~\cite{wang2023gemini} or save-side PEC~\cite{cai2024moc}; (ii)~extending R1 with a verification predicate to cover silent data corruption / slow degradation, with §\ref{sec:obs}'s log carrying its outcome; (iii)~online calibration of $\tau_C$ against a streaming estimate of the failure-free noise floor.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We position MoEGambit on a software-engineering coordinate system rather than as a point in a chronology of training systems. \S\ref{sec:rw-sas} anchors the paper in the self-adaptive / self-healing software literature, so that the SE artifacts R1--R3 introduced in \S\ref{sec:moegambit} have an explicit lineage; \S\ref{sec:rw-save}--\S\ref{sec:rw-moe-sys} then survey the three adjacent systems lines (save-side checkpointing, pipeline-adaptive redundancy, MoE throughput infrastructure); \S\ref{sec:rw-moe-ft} contrasts with the only previously published MoE-specific fault-tolerance system and locates MoEGambit relative to all five lines as the unique recovery-side / R1--R3-contract entry.

\subsection{Self-Adaptive and Self-Healing Software Systems}
\label{sec:rw-sas}

The self-adaptive systems literature formalizes runtime repair as a MAPE-K loop (Monitor--Analyze--Plan--Execute--Knowledge)~\cite{kephart2003autonomic,garlan2004rainbow,salehie2009selfadaptive}, and prescribes three engineering disciplines orthogonal to any particular workload: (a) repair decisions must be driven by an explicit, runtime-checkable specification rather than by a hand-tuned heuristic; (b) the execute stage must protect a stated invariant with an explicit barrier rather than rely on best-effort sequencing; and (c) every decision must leave an auditable structured trace whose schema admits post-hoc correlation with downstream outcomes. MoEGambit's R1/R2/R3 contracts (\S\ref{sec:problem}) instantiate exactly these three disciplines for the distributed MoE training runtime: R1 is the execute-stage invariant barrier, R2's $\Phi'(t)$ is the analyze-stage runtime monitor, and R3's log schema is the knowledge layer. \emph{Distinct from} prior self-adaptive work, which has been applied predominantly to enterprise control planes and cloud orchestration~\cite{kephart2003autonomic,garlan2004rainbow}, MoEGambit pushes the same discipline into the \emph{training data plane} and introduces a recovery-quality metric---expert-weighted staleness density---that is specific to MoE training and has no analogue in the classical self-adaptive vocabulary.

\subsection{Save-Side Optimization of Checkpointing}
\label{sec:rw-save}

One systems line attacks the per-checkpoint cost $C$ in Young/Daly's $\tau^{*}\!\approx\!\sqrt{2CM}$~\cite{young1974first,daly2006higher}: CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq} interleaves fine-grained snapshots with computation; DeepFreeze~\cite{nicolae2020deepfreeze} pushes asynchronous and multi-tier writes; Check-N-Run~\cite{eisenman2022check} adds incremental and quantized save for recommendation models; Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini} replaces NVMe writes with in-memory peer replicas. In the MAPE-K view of \S\ref{sec:rw-sas}, this line contributes \emph{snapshot-frequency tuning} upstream of Monitor and is silent on the post-recovery trajectory. MoEGambit is \emph{orthogonal} to this line---it neither alters $C$ nor changes the save protocol---so CheckFreq/Gemini/Check-N-Run remain stackable beneath MoEGambit's recovery contract.

\subsection{Pipeline-Adaptive and Redundancy-Based Fault Tolerance}
\label{sec:rw-pipeline}

A second systems line avoids reloading any snapshot by reconfiguring the training topology after a failure. Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo} hides save cost behind redundancy on preemptible instances; Oobleck~\cite{jang2023oobleck} pre-compiles pipeline templates that admit degraded but valid pipelines; ReCycle~\cite{gandhi2024recycle} adapts the pipeline schedule on the fly; Varuna~\cite{athlur2022varuna}, Parcae~\cite{duan2024parcae}, Litz~\cite{qiao2019litz}, and Or et al.~\cite{or2020resource} target elastic and preemptible deployments. The common load-bearing assumption is that \emph{ranks are interchangeable}, which holds for dense Transformers but \emph{fails for MoE}: an EP-sharded expert subset (Table~\ref{tab:state-provenance}) has no in-memory peer at any other rank. MoEGambit does not compete with this line; it \emph{fills the EP-sharded blind spot} the line leaves open by formalizing the recovery semantics of sharded expert state into the R2 contract, and by exposing a runtime-checkable bound ($\Phi'(t)$) on the post-recovery trajectory---an object the pipeline-template family neither defines nor measures.

\subsection{MoE Systems and the Freshness Blind Spot}
\label{sec:rw-moe-sys}

A third systems line builds the throughput infrastructure of MoE training: GShard~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard}, Switch Transformer~\cite{fedus2022switch}, DeepSpeed-MoE~\cite{rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe}, Tutel~\cite{hwang2023tutel}, FasterMoE~\cite{he2022fastermoe}, SmartMoE~\cite{zhai2023smartmoe}, MegaBlocks~\cite{gale2023megablocks}, and the open-model deployments~\cite{jiang2024mixtral,dai2024deepseekmoe,qwen3report} optimize routing, dispatch, and all-to-all bandwidth; ST-MoE~\cite{zoph2022stmoe}, sparse-upcycling~\cite{komatsuzaki2023sparse}, and V-MoE~\cite{riquelme2021vmoe} push the architecture itself. Across this family, \emph{``every expert at step $t$ is current''} is an \emph{implicit performance invariant}, not a stated specification. MoEGambit lifts that implicit invariant into an \emph{explicit, $O(1)$-computable runtime monitor} $\Phi'(t)$ (\S\ref{sec:policy}) and validates it against an empirically grounded cliff edge (\S\ref{sec:eval}); this is, to our knowledge, the first time the MoE systems literature's silent freshness assumption has been written down as a runtime-checkable specification.

\subsection{MoE-Specific Fault Tolerance: MoC-System}
\label{sec:rw-moe-ft}

The only previously published MoE-specific fault-tolerance system we are aware of is MoC-System~\cite{cai2024moc} (ASPLOS'25), whose Partial Experts Checkpointing (PEC) writes only $K_{\text{pec}}$ of $N$ experts as fresh per save round, reducing on-disk save cost by a factor of $\sim$$N/K_{\text{pec}}$. The relationship between MoC-System and MoEGambit is best stated as a quadrant assignment along two axes---\emph{where in the recovery pipeline the optimization lives} (save side vs.\ recovery side) and \emph{whether the system carries a runtime-checkable SE contract on the post-recovery trajectory}: MoC-System sits in the \emph{save-side / no SE contract} quadrant alongside CheckFreq~\cite{mohan2021checkfreq}, Gemini~\cite{wang2023gemini}, and Check-N-Run~\cite{eisenman2022check}; topology-adaptation systems like Bamboo~\cite{thorpe2023bamboo}, Oobleck~\cite{jang2023oobleck}, and ReCycle~\cite{gandhi2024recycle} occupy a separate \emph{topology-adaptation / no SE contract} quadrant; and MoEGambit is, to our knowledge, the first system in the \emph{recovery-side, MoE-aware / R1--R3 contract} quadrant. The two are \emph{orthogonal} rather than competing. A combined deployment is admissible provided the $\Phi'(t)$ aggregate in (\ref{eq:phi-prime}) also accounts for the save-side partial-freshness PEC injects, which we leave to future work (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}). For the accuracy-only comparison reported in this paper we use the byte-identical overlay described in \S\ref{sec:compared}; its limits are discussed in \S\ref{sec:threats}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We argued that runtime failure recovery in distributed MoE training should be engineered as a software reliability artifact rather than as a checkpoint-optimization heuristic. MoEGambit realizes this view inside Megatron-LM~\cite{narayanan2021megatron} as three explicit SE artifacts---safe-point repair with optimizer commit guarding (R1), an expert-weighted staleness density $\Phi'(t)$ as a runtime-checkable recovery specification (R2), and structured recovery logs (R3)---and it cleanly separates the recovery \emph{contract} (when hybrid repair is permitted) from the recovery \emph{mechanism} (how dense, shared, router, and expert state are reconstituted from peers and per-rank shards). The contract is honest about its own pre-conditions: when a failure (or burst of concurrent failures) eliminates every healthy data-parallel peer at the failed rank's PP/TP coordinate---so no surviving rank holds a current-step replica of the dense/router state---hybrid recovery is by construction infeasible and MoEGambit transparently falls back to checkpoint restart, with the policy decision and its inputs preserved in the structured log. Empirically, on a Qwen3-30B-A3B sparse MoE~\cite{qwen3report,dai2024deepseekmoe} (128 experts, top-8) on 64 H20-3e GPUs, MoE-aware hybrid restore contributes a $-15.1\%$ main effect and the weights-first / optimizer-later two-phase protocol an additional $-5.5\%$ main effect on single-failure end-to-end recovery latency, composing additively (interaction $0.96\%$, within noise) for a total $20.6\%$ reduction relative to full checkpoint restart at the same checkpoint format and gap. Across a $|F|\times\Delta$ multi-rank burst sweep, post-recovery validation loss stays inside the failure-free $\pm 1\sigma$ band on every cell with $\Phi'(t)\leq 10^{-2}$ and MoEGambit correctly falls back to checkpoint restart on the cells beyond the empirical cliff edge.

Beyond these point measurements, the broader claim of this paper is methodological: \emph{recovery in large-model training systems must be specified against the post-recovery training trajectory, not merely against the event of resumption.} The $\Phi'(t)$ contract, the paired-run evaluation against an a priori noise floor~\cite{wohlin2012ese,arcuri2014hitchhiker}, and the structured recovery logs are three faces of the same SE discipline: making the recovery decision auditable, the recovered trajectory falsifiable, and the failure surface testable. Future work includes (i)~extending the guarded-recovery contract to the \texttt{torch\_dist} checkpoint format via a collective-free selective shard loader, (ii)~lifting the single-rank assumption to concurrent multi-rank repair and the corresponding multi-event $\Phi'(t)$ accounting, and (iii)~accommodating non-fail-stop fault models such as silent data corruption and slow-degrading hardware observed in production traces~\cite{chowdhery2023palm,jiang2024megascale}.
